# Lift Heavy, Jump Often, Skip Cycle Syncing

> **Read alongside the follow-up,** [training-research-women-and-failure.md](training-research-women-and-failure.md), which revisits two points made here: how young women's muscle growth differs from men's, and when to train to failure. Where the two differ, the follow-up is the more careful account.

A 22-year-old woman who wants lean muscle should train by the same evidence-based hypertrophy rules as a man: at least 10, and for most trained lifters 12–20, hard sets per muscle per week, every muscle trained on two or more days, sets of roughly 5–30 reps stopped 0–3 reps short of failure, at least 60–90 seconds of rest, reps-then-load progression and a lighter week every couple of months, all fed by 1.6–2.2 g/kg/day of protein at maintenance calories or a small surplus. Women gain the same *relative* muscle as men on identical programs, and the best-controlled recent studies, a 2025 muscle-protein-synthesis study and a 2025/26 randomized trial, find no advantage in training by menstrual-cycle phase, so her cycle belongs in the program as a health signal rather than a scheduling variable. Dr. Stacy Sims' central message holds up (lift heavy, add jumps and sprints, eat enough protein, never chronically under-fuel), but her most distinctive prescriptions, namely phase-based training and fuelling, "never train fasted", a 30-minute protein window and luteal hydration rules, rest on small studies, extrapolation or opinion, and her lift-jump-sprint package was designed for women around menopause. Age 22 adds an opportunity that will not last: hip bone density peaks around 19 and spine density around 20 or later, so 100–200 multidirectional jumps a week, a few heavy low-rep compound sets, one hard interval session and small doses of balance, carries and injury-prevention work buy large long-term returns for an estimated 15–30 extra minutes a week. "Natasha" is most likely Natacha Océane, the UK science communicator and hybrid athlete, and her varied, athletic style fits a muscle goal as long as about three-quarters of every session stays hard hypertrophy work, with the fun formats built around it rather than replacing it.

## Stacy Sims built her lift-jump-sprint model for menopause

Sims' own site names "three pillars of training that are essential for power", **heavy lifting, plyometrics and sprint intervals**, in an article titled "How to Power Your Way Through Menopause" ([Sims](https://www.drstacysims.com/newsletters/articles/posts/How_to_Power_Your_Way_Through_Menopause)), and on Huberman Lab she calls jump training, heavy resistance training and sprint interval training "the three key things" ([Huberman Lab](https://www.hubermanlab.com/episode/dr-stacy-sims-female-specific-exercise-nutrition-for-health-performance-longevity)). The numbers attached to these pillars come mostly from secondary summaries, and they disagree. One gives **three or more sessions a week of compound lifts heavy enough for only 6–8 reps, stopping 1–2 reps short of failure (1–2 RIR, reps in reserve)** ([Laura Fish summary](https://laurafishtherapy.com/7-health-tips-dr-stacy-sims/)); another, aimed at women over 40, describes heavy work at 2–3 RIR at the "strength-power end of the spectrum" plus **1–2 sprint or HIIT sessions a week of 10–30-second efforts with 2–4 minutes of recovery** ([ForWomensHealth](https://forwomenshealth.substack.com/p/7-training-truths-from-dr-stacy-sims)). Plyometrics are dosed at roughly **three 10-minute blocks a week** ([Unfiltered](https://unfilteredonline.com/dr-stacy-sims-why-all-women-should-lift-heavy-weights/)), and her recurring advice to midlife women is "less time in the gym, not more", with moderate "junk miles" played down ([AGEIST](https://www.ageist.com/profile/dr-stacy-sims-53-less-time-in-the-gym-not-more/)).

Her nutrition advice is more prescriptive. She tells women not to do "any kind of fasted training" ([Dottir](https://empowerbydottir.com/blogs/empower-edit/to-fast-or-not-to-fast-here-s-what-dr-stacy-sims-has-to-say)), and her newsletter claims fasted exercise "can damage hormone balance over time" with an unnamed female-only study as its only support ([Sims newsletter](https://www.drstacysims.com/newsletters/articles/posts/fasting-for-active-women-risks)). Summaries of her books give **10–15 g protein plus 30–40 g carbohydrate before training** ([Running Week](https://www.therunningweek.com/post/dr-stacy-sims-says-runners-are-fueling-wrong-5-rules-that-contradict-standard-marathon-advice)) and **20–25 g protein within 30 minutes after** in *ROAR* ([Goodreads](https://www.goodreads.com/work/quotes/46061205)), later loosened to about 35 g within 45 minutes ([Huberman AI summary](https://ai.hubermanlab.com/s/0WSWccjm)). Her daily target is about **0.9–1.0 g/lb (roughly 2.0–2.2 g/kg)**, with about 20 g extra in the luteal phase ([100 Year Athlete](https://www.100yearathlete.com/blog/yl8zqj5nsywlgpx40mbhlbxz77ds2q); [Recall](https://www.recall.it/summary/fitness/dr-stacy-sims-female-specific-exercise-and-nutrition-for-health-performance-and-longevity)), and she prescribes more carbohydrate per hour of exercise in the "high-hormone" luteal phase than in the follicular phase, 0.45 versus 0.35 g per lb per hour ([Debbie Potts](https://debbiepotts.net/roar-like-a-female-athlete/)). Her most rigorous output is the peer-reviewed 2023 ISSN position stand she co-authored, which recommends **1.4–2.2 g/kg/day of protein**, names caffeine, iron and creatine as the best-evidenced supplements for women, and calls creatine "highly efficacious" ([ISSN 2023](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/pdf/10.1080/15502783.2023.2204066); [ESNL summary](https://exerciseandsportnutritionlab.com/issn-position-stand-female-athletes/)).

Graded against the evidence, Sims is strongest where she agrees with everyone else and weakest where her advice is most distinctive.

The useful reading is that Sims' **critique of how women have been studied is correct, while most of her specific mechanisms are extrapolations**. Her anti-fasting stance points at a real hazard, chronic low energy availability, but the evidence of harm concerns total energy restriction, not meal timing ([Physiologically Speaking](https://www.physiologicallyspeaking.com/p/what-science-says-about-fasted-exercise)). Her rep ranges are strength-biased, which is one valid hypertrophy zone among several, and her case for plyometrics and sprints is a health and power argument rather than a muscle-building one. An independent UK GP-practice review of her perimenopause advice reached the same ranking: resistance training best supported, plyometrics next, then HIIT, with sprint intervals "promising but smaller-scale" ([Walton Surgery](https://waltonsurgery.co.uk/health/stacy-sims-women-exercise-research-uk-perimenopause/)). For a 22-year-old, what transfers is heavy compound lifting, high daily protein and refusing to under-eat; the jump and sprint pillars transfer as longevity add-ons, not as the engine of muscle growth.

## Cycle syncing has failed its best-controlled tests

The case for training by phase, usually more volume and frequency in the follicular phase and less in the luteal phase, rests mainly on small early trials. **Sung et al. 2014** found follicular-phase training superior for quadriceps strength and diameter, but its diameter changes (0.57 ± 0.54 cm vs 0.39 ± 0.38 cm) carry standard deviations nearly as large as the means ([SpringerPlus](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/2193-1801-3-668)). A 14-woman trial in 2016 found "no major differences" between phase-based protocols ([Sakamaki-Sunaga 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26554551/)), and a 2022 *Sports Medicine* analysis drew only a tentative pro-follicular signal from very few studies ([Kissow 2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-022-01679-y)). For single-session performance, the McNulty and Elliott-Sale network meta-analysis concluded that performance "might be trivially reduced during the early follicular phase" ([McNulty 2020](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/The-Effects-of-Menstrual-Cycle-Phase-on-Exercise-in-McNulty-Elliott-Sale/94feed7015969fe958c05860ab3c3727f472d00f)), and women on the pill perform consistently across the pill cycle ([Elliott-Sale 2020](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/342939191_The_Effects_of_Oral_Contraceptives_on_Exercise_Performance_in_Women_A_Systematic_Review_and_Meta-analysis)).

The newer, better-controlled work all points one way. A 2023 McMaster umbrella review found that "current evidence shows no influence of women's menstrual cycle phase on acute strength performance or adaptations to resistance exercise training," and flagged "poor and inconsistent methodological practices" in the studies behind phase-based programs ([Colenso-Semple 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fspor.2023.1054542/full)). In 2025 the same group measured **no effect of cycle phase on muscle protein synthesis or breakdown** after resistance exercise ([J Physiol](https://physoc.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1113/JP287342)). The most direct test, a randomized trial in **24 regularly cycling women over three consecutive cycles**, with each woman's two legs on different schedules, found that emphasizing either phase gave no advantage over consistent, volume-matched training, and that **training volume, not timing, predicted growth and strength** ([MSSE RCT](https://www.ovid.com/jnls/acsm-msse/fulltext/10.1249/mss.0000000000004031~menstrual-cycle-phase-does-not-influence-training-induced)). A December 2025 NSCA review concluded that evidence for phase-based periodization "is lacking" ([SCJ](https://journals.lww.com/nsca-scj/fulltext/2025/12000/evidence_for_periodizing_strength_and_or_endurance.4.aspx)), and ACSM's commentary attributes adaptation to consistency, effort and progressive overload ([ACSM](https://acsm.org/?p=27047)). The dissent is thin: a 2026 *Retos* review says phase-based periodization "was shown to optimize outcomes" while conceding that strength findings need more research ([Retos 2026](https://www.revistaretos.org/index.php/retos/article/download/119754/85480/527454)). Part of the earlier signal is plausibly measurement error, because many studies estimated phase by counting days rather than confirming it with hormone tests, a practice a 2025 *Sports Medicine* paper says researchers "must stop" ([Sports Med 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-025-02189-3)).

What survives is personal, not calendar-based. Female athletes do report phase-related differences in *perceived* readiness ([JCSS](https://journal.foundae.com/index.php/jcss/article/view/882)), and cramps, poor sleep or heavy bleeding are real reasons to ease off on a given day. The defensible design is **symptom-based adjustment**: keep the plan, let the RIR targets set the load, and on a bad day trim a set per exercise, swap heavy spine-loading lifts for supported machine versions, or move the session by a day or two. Hormonal contraception needs no adjustment. The cycle's most valuable programming role is as a **health marker**, because a missed or irregular period is an early warning of low energy availability.

### Physiologists and practitioners agree on lifting and protein, split on the calendar

The academic physiologists oppose phase-based prescriptions while endorsing Sims' broader goals. **Kirsty Elliott-Sale** calls the cycle "a vital health marker, not a tool to measure sport performance" ([MMU](https://www.mmu.ac.uk/news-and-events/news/story/menstrual-cycle-vital-health-marker-not-tool-measure-sport-performance)), called the cycle-syncing narrative "quite superficial" in an August 2026 interview, and advises tracking and treating symptoms instead ([Tyla](https://www.tyla.com/womens-health/menstrual-cycle-syncing-workouts-exercise-diet-171668-20260805)); the 2025 UEFA consensus she co-authored says any link between tracking and performance or injury "remains unclear" ([BMJ Open SEM](https://doaj.org/article/c864dfcf85c440d7879ccff06db042c9)). **Lauren Colenso-Semple and Stuart Phillips** produced the umbrella review and the protein-synthesis study above, and a *J Physiol* commentary on their work is titled "Science, not socials, is doing the heavy lifting" ([PMC11870042](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11870042/)). **Greg Nuckols** co-authored the sex-difference meta-analyses discussed in the next section and calls the phase-training science "quite weak" ([Stronger By Science](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/training-menstrual-cycle-phase/)). **Asker Jeukendrup** finds "no evidence to adapt training to the phase of the menstrual cycle" ([mysportscience](https://www.mysportscience.com/post/no-evidence-to-adapt-training-to-the-phase-of-the-menstrual-cycle)) and questions whether energy availability can be measured outside a lab at all ([mysportscience](https://www.mysportscience.com/post/can-we-measure-low-energy-availability)). **Brad Schoenfeld** led the best-known fasted-versus-fed trial run entirely in young women, which found no difference ([PMC4242477](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4242477/)).

The practitioners side with Sims on protein and heavy lifting but differ in emphasis. **Gabrielle Lyon** ("muscle-centric medicine") stresses per-meal dose over workout timing, about **30–50 g of high-quality protein per meal**, 40–50 g at the first and last meals and roughly 2.5 g of leucine per meal ([Lyon Protocol](https://drgabriellelyon.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/The-Lyon-Protocol-Dr.-Gabrielle-Lyon.pdf); [Huberman Lab](https://www.hubermanlab.com/episode/dr-gabrielle-lyon-how-to-exercise-eat-for-optimal-health-longevity)), with resistance training three to four times a week ([Doctor's Kitchen](https://www.thedoctorskitchen.com/podcasts/317-how-to-support-muscle-health-for-longevity-and-improving-metabolism-dr-gabrielle-lyon)). **Bret Contreras** believes women "recover better and can handle somewhat more volume" while acknowledging the research is mixed ([Huberman Lab](https://www.hubermanlab.com/episode/build-ideal-physique-bret-contreras)); he suggests glutes need only about two sessions a week ([Contreras](https://bretcontreras.com/your-optimal-training-frequency-for-the-glutes-part-i-exercise-type/)) and that women's best volume sits slightly above men's "but not double" ([Perform podcast](https://performpodcast.com/bret-contreras-optimize-training-glute-development/)). Where Sims emphasizes heavy, low-rep, "less is more" work, Contreras favors higher volume and a wider rep menu; both positions are expert opinion. The published positions of two names often grouped with Sims, Emma Ross of The Well HQ and orthopaedic surgeon Vonda Wright, could not be verified for this report, and Wright's work targets midlife women in any case.

The core every one of these experts accepts is the part worth encoding: **progressive, reasonably heavy resistance training, adequate total protein, adequate energy and consistency**. The only live disagreement is whether the cycle should change the plan, and as of late 2026 the evidence says it should not, apart from day-to-day symptom management.

## Women build muscle on the same rules as men

### Relative gains match men's, so the rulebook does too

The foundational finding is that sex barely changes the response to training. A 2020 meta-analysis by Roberts, Nuckols and Krieger found **no meaningful sex difference in muscle growth (ES 0.07, P = 0.31)** and a **larger relative upper-body strength gain in women (ES −0.60, P = 0.002)**, mostly among untrained participants ([Roberts 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32218059/)). A 2025 Bayesian meta-analysis of 29 studies of adults aged 18–45 on identical programs found men gained slightly more *absolute* muscle (SMD 0.19), **only in the upper body**, while *relative* gains were similar; its authors conclude that "females appear to have a similar capacity for muscle hypertrophy to males" ([Refalo 2025](https://doaj.org/article/dbf856c9d64840b59318c009c4abd626); [Napier](https://www.napier.ac.uk/research-and-innovation/research-search/outputs/sex-differences-in-absolute-and-relative-changes-in-muscle-size-following-resistance)). The relationship between load and reps to failure is also sex-neutral: pooling 952 tests from 7,289 people, Nuzzo et al. found sex, age and training status "did not clearly moderate" how many reps people manage at a given percentage of one-rep max, although the exercise did, with the leg press allowing more reps than the bench press ([Nuzzo 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10933212)).

Women do differ in fatigability. They resist fatigue better in isometric and slow contractions ([Hunter 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24433272/)), an advantage that narrows in fast dynamic work ([Hunter 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5777316)), and one 2024 study of trained lifters found women recovered better between squat sets ([PubMed 38347629](https://www.pubmed.gov/38347629/)), yet another found that short rests cut women's total volume just as they cut men's ([Triathlete, on Millender](https://www.triathlete.com/training/should-women-use-different-weight-training-rest-intervals-than-men/)). No trial has assigned women and men to different volumes, rest periods or RIR targets, so the honest conclusion is to **use the same framework**, leaning toward the shorter end of rest on isolation work and tolerating slightly more sets if her logs show good recovery. Two practical consequences follow. "Light weights, high reps to tone" has no physiological basis, and because the only sex gap in absolute growth is in the upper body, back and shoulder volume deserves protection rather than being crowded out by glute work.

### Volume drives growth, with diminishing returns but no hard ceiling

Weekly hard sets per muscle are the strongest lever a program controls. The **2026 ACSM position stand**, built on 137 systematic reviews and more than 30,000 participants, found hypertrophy "enhanced by higher volumes (≥10 sets/wk)," while training to failure, equipment type, set structure, time under tension and periodization "did not consistently impact training outcomes" ([ACSM 2026](https://www.newswise.com/articles/acsm-unveils-landmark-2026-resistance-training-guidelines-first-update-in-17-years)). A review comparing moderate and high volumes concluded that **12–20 weekly sets per muscle** "may be an optimum standard recommendation," though only for young trained men ([Baz-Valle 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8884877/)). The largest dose-response analysis to date, Pelland et al.'s 2025 meta-regressions of 67 studies, found **no plateau but diminishing returns**, roughly **+0.24% muscle growth per extra weekly set** at average volumes, and showed that counting indirect sets, such as the triceps' work in a bench press, as half a set ("fractional" counting) predicted outcomes best ([Pelland 2025](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/398333408_The_Resistance_Training_Dose_Response_Meta-Regressions_Exploring_the_Effects_of_Weekly_Volume_and_Frequency_on_Muscle_Hypertrophy_and_Strength_Gains_The_Resistance_Training_Dose_ResponseJ_C_Pelland_et_al)). A companion preprint finds the benefit of extra sets within one session becoming undetectable beyond about **11 fractional sets per muscle** ([Remmert preprint](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/537)).

Frequency matters mainly as a container for volume. With volume equated, a 25-study meta-analysis found no growth difference between training a muscle once a week and three or more times ([Schoenfeld 2019](https://www.pubmed.gov/30558493/)), and Pelland found the frequency effect on growth weak and compatible with zero, although strength rises with frequency. Because sets beyond about 11 per session add little, weekly targets above roughly 16–20 sets need three exposures, and ACSM's practical floor is **every major muscle on at least two days a week** ([ACSM 2026](https://www.newswise.com/articles/acsm-unveils-landmark-2026-resistance-training-guidelines-first-update-in-17-years)). For a trainee whose sessions arrive unpredictably, which describes a generator that builds each workout at booking time, the sturdiest structure is not a fixed split but a **rolling seven-day ledger of fractional sets per muscle**, with each new session filling the largest deficits. No study tests this directly; it is an engineering inference from the volume and frequency evidence.

### Effort matters more than load

Hypertrophy is similar across roughly **30–85% of one-rep max (about 6–30 reps) when sets end near failure**, while heavier loads build more maximal strength ([Schoenfeld 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28834797/)). Near enough turns out to be fairly generous. A 2023 meta-analysis found only a trivial advantage for training to failure (ES 0.19), and it vanished when failure was strictly defined (ES 0.12, interval crossing zero) ([Refalo 2023](https://openrepository.aut.ac.nz/bitstreams/9e3df52b-a8b3-4139-b172-feefd51e5334/download)); a within-subject trial that included six trained women found failure and 1–2 RIR produced **identical quadriceps growth (+0.181 vs +0.182 cm)**, with failure causing more fatigue ([Refalo 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38393985/)); and exploratory meta-regressions show growth improving as sets end closer to failure while strength is largely unaffected ([Robinson 2024](https://rke.abertay.ac.uk/en/publications/exploring-the-dose-response-relationship-between-estimated-resist/)). One calibration problem matters for any app that relies on self-reported effort: lifters **underpredict their remaining reps by about one**, with wide individual variation ([Halperin 2022](https://pesquisa.bvsalud.org/ses/resource/pt/mdl-34542869)), so a logged "2 RIR" is often closer to 3. The practical targets are 1–3 RIR on free-weight compounds (never to failure), 0–2 on machines, and 0–1 on isolation and high-rep sets, which lose more stimulus when stopped short.

### Rest at least a minute; supersets save about a third of the time

A 2024 Bayesian meta-analysis found a small growth benefit for rests **longer than 60 seconds** and no clear further gain beyond about 90 seconds, with wide uncertainty ([Singer 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2024.1429789/text)); longer rest on heavy compounds mainly protects the total load lifted. Time-saving structures are well supported when they preserve effort and volume. A 2025 meta-analysis of 19 studies found **supersets matched traditional sets for hypertrophy (SMD −0.05)** with much higher time efficiency (SMD 1.74), though pairing two exercises for the same muscle cut volume load (SMD −1.08) ([Zhang 2025](https://eprints.leedsbeckett.ac.uk/id/eprint/11908)), and one secondary source puts superset sessions about 37% shorter ([Lyfta](https://lyfta.app/articles/supersets-hypertrophy)). Drop sets match straight sets when volume is equated ([Sødal 2023](https://doaj.org/article/21e0b210caf1433594359186eee617b9)), cluster sets match traditional sets ([PMC12174233](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12174233/)), and rest-pause and myo-reps have almost no controlled data.

### Pick exercises that load muscles at long lengths

Exercise choice adds a second layer. Seated leg curls, which stretch the hamstrings at the hip, grew them more than lying curls (**+14% vs +9%**) ([Maeo 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33009197/)), and overhead triceps extensions grew the long head more than the same movement with the arms at the sides (**+28.5% vs +19.6%**) despite lighter loads ([Maeo 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35819335/)). In 45 untrained women, partial knee extensions in the stretched range produced more lower-quadriceps growth ([Pedrosa 2022](https://researchgate.net/profile/Gustavo-Pedrosa/publication/351538891_Partial_range_of_motion_training_elicits_favorable_improvements_in_muscular_adaptations_when_carried_out_at_long_muscle_lengths/links/61f1e6bbc5e3103375c2e9f9/Partial-range-of-motion-training-elicits-favorable-improvements-in-muscular-adaptations-when-carried-out-at-long-muscle-lengths.pdf)), though full range of motion still edges out partials overall and remains the default ([Wolf 2023](https://journal.iusca.org/index.php/Journal/article/view/182)). Free weights and machines build equal muscle ([Haugen 2023](https://doaj.org/article/a7282dba5e674176bde2a8239832d2f7)). For glutes, a priority for many women, the hip thrust and back squat grew them similarly in untrained adults, with the squat also growing quads and adductors ([Plotkin 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2023.1279170/full)). (An earlier version of this report also cited Barbalho 2020, in which the squat beat the hip thrust for glute thickness in trained women; that paper has since been retracted and is no longer relied on. The conclusion below rests on Plotkin 2023.) The sensible weekly glute mix is therefore a deep knee-dominant lift, a hip-thrust or bridge pattern and a hinge, so the hip thrust is never the only glute exercise. Planned variation of accessories helps regional growth while random "muscle confusion" does not ([Kassiano 2022](https://journals.lww.com/nsca-jscr/fulltext/2022/06000/does_varying_resistance_exercises_promote_superior.40.aspx)), which argues for fixed anchor lifts and scheduled accessory rotation.

### Progress reps first, and deload every five to eight weeks

In trained lifters, adding reps at a fixed load and adding load at fixed reps produced similar results ([Plotkin 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36199287/)). That supports **double progression**, adding reps to the top of a range before adding load, which suits women especially well because the smallest available jump is often a large share of the load: moving from a 10 kg to a 12 kg dumbbell is a 20% increase, and wide rep ranges (8–15 rather than 8–10) absorb such jumps. Deloads, lighter weeks to shed fatigue, are a practitioner tradition rather than a tested intervention. A survey of 246 strength and physique athletes, 65 of them women, found deloads lasting **6.4 ± 1.7 days every 5.6 ± 2.3 weeks**, cutting sets and reps and raising RIR while keeping exercises and frequency ([Rogerson 2024](https://www.pubmed.gov/38499934/)), and coaches report similar 4–6-week cycles with "highly variable" timing ([Bell, SportRxiv](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/208)).

### Protein, creatine and a small surplus finish the job

Protein supplementation increases fat-free mass, with gains plateauing around **1.62 g/kg/day (95% CI up to 2.20)** ([Morton 2018](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/52/6/376)), and the authors' data hint that any true breakpoint sits higher ([Stronger By Science](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/?p=61316)). For a 60 kg woman, 1.6–2.2 g/kg means about **96–132 g a day**, spread over three to five meals. Creatine "appears to be effective for improving strength and exercise performance in pre-menopausal women," with no adverse kidney, liver, digestive or cardiovascular effects found in women ([Smith-Ryan 2021](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7998865/)); the usual 3–5 g/day monohydrate dose is convention, not confirmed in a female-specific primary source. Energy intake sets the ceiling: in trained lifters, a +15% calorie surplus built no more muscle than maintenance or +5%, but added more fat by skinfold ([Helms 2023](https://doaj.org/article/ab6ce09af2cc4daa9a1c2e9b88e1b1e9)). Maintenance suits beginners, who can gain muscle and lose fat at once; a small surplus suits intermediates; a deficit has no place in a building phase.

## At 22, bone and aerobic reserve are cheapest to build

### Hip bone density has just peaked; jumps and heavy lifts consolidate it

US NHANES data place women's peak bone density at **18.7 years at the femoral neck, 19.0 at the total hip and 20.1 at the lumbar spine** ([PubMed 31760214](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31760214/)), and an older Mayo cohort found the spine peaking around 28 and the forearm around 31 ([PubMed 10871911](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10871911)). At 22 her hip is at or just past its peak and her spine may still be gaining. Lifestyle factors account for an estimated **20–40% of adult peak bone mass** ([NOF position statement](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5101070)), which makes the next few years a window to maximize and lock in the reserve she will draw down after menopause.

Bone responds to the size, speed and novelty of strain rather than repetition count, which is why a hypertrophy program alone gives it little, and jumping fills the gap. In college-aged women averaging 20.7 years, just **10 maximal vertical jumps a day, three days a week**, raised lumbar spine density ([Kato 2006](https://search.pedro.org.au/search-results/record-detail/14578)). A 1998 trial of 50 jumps six days a week raised femoral density **2.8% in five months** in premenopausal but not postmenopausal women, and a meta-analysis confirms that jumping improves femoral neck and trochanter density before menopause ([2014 meta-analysis](https://www.springermedicine.com/efficiency-of-jumping-exercise-in-improving-bone-mineral-density/22254016)). A 16-week trial found 10–20 jumps twice daily raised hip density in women aged 25–50 ([BYU](https://scholarsarchive.byu.edu/etd/667)), and an Australian exercise-science position statement, as summarized by practitioners, suggests about 50 impacts per session four to seven days a week ([ESSA summary](https://wildphysiofitness.au/blogs/physiotherapy/exercise-for-bone-health)). Rest between jumps matters, at least in rats: about 14 seconds between loading cycles raised bone formation 66–190%, and 8 hours between bouts fully restored bone cells' sensitivity ([Robling 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11606612/)). Heavy loading adds to the effect. In the LIFTMOR trial, women with low bone mass doing twice-weekly 30-minute sessions of **5 × 5 above 85% of one-rep max** plus impact work gained **2.9% lumbar spine density while controls lost 1.2%**, with one minor adverse event ([LIFTMOR](https://academic.oup.com/jbmr/article/33/2/211/7605709)). LIFTMOR studied postmenopausal women, and no trial compares hypertrophy-style training, heavy low-rep work and added jumps head-to-head in women aged 18–30, so the inference that a few heavy sets twice a week add bone stimulus to a 6–12-rep program is plausible rather than proven.

### One hard interval session a week buys most of the aerobic dividend

Cardiorespiratory fitness is among the strongest observational predictors of survival. In 122,007 treadmill-tested adults, the fittest 2.5% had about **80% lower mortality** than the least fit, with no upper limit to the benefit, though among younger adults "elite" and "high" fitness did not differ ([Mandsager 2018 via TCTMD](https://www.tctmd.com/news/big-fitness-equals-big-survival-benefit-new-study-shows)); among 750,302 veterans, **each 1-MET gain carried a 14% lower risk of death (HR 0.86)** and the least fit had four times the mortality of the fittest ([Kokkinos 2022 via Healio](https://cm.healio.com/news/cardiology/20220801/high-cardiorespiratory-fitness-reduces-veterans-death-risk-regardless-of-age-sex-race)). The target for a young woman is therefore "high," not "elite." WHO recommends **150–300 minutes of moderate or 75–150 minutes of vigorous activity a week plus muscle strengthening on two or more days** ([WHO 2020](https://bjsm.bmj.com/node/121498.full)). Short, hard work is efficient: the Norwegian 4×4 protocol raised VO2max 7.2% in eight weeks ([Helgerud 2007](https://coachsci.sdsu.edu/csa/vol131/helgerud.htm)), and three 20-second all-out sprints in a 10-minute session improved VO2peak as much as 45 minutes of steady cycling ([Gillen 2016](https://doaj.org/article/0d8f38860885479c87ee1ba61460002b)), though both studied men. A 2025 review found **Zone 2 is not the optimal intensity** for mitochondrial or fat-oxidation adaptation and that higher intensities matter most when total volume is low ([Storoschuk 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40560504/)), which tempers the Zone-2-heavy prescriptions popularized by Peter Attia's *Outlive*; his backward-planning "centenarian decathlon" is a useful heuristic, but its hour targets are not evidence-based minimums ([UESCA](https://uesca.com/is-zone-2-really-the-endurance-panacea/)).

The fear that cardio "kills gains" is mostly unfounded. Across 43 studies, adding aerobic training had **no effect on whole-muscle hypertrophy (SMD −0.01)** or maximal strength, but blunted explosive strength (SMD −0.28), especially in the same session ([Schumann 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9474354/)). Fiber-level data show a small penalty that appears with running but not cycling ([Lundberg 2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-022-01688-x)), consistent with an older meta-analysis in which running, not cycling, reduced gains ([Wilson 2012](https://pesquisa.bvsalud.org/sms/resource/pt/mdl-22002517)). The longevity case for combining them is strong: muscle-strengthening alone is associated with about 15% lower mortality and **strength plus aerobic activity with 40% lower (RR 0.60)**, with the strength benefit peaking around 30–60 minutes a week ([Momma 2022](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/56/13/755.abstract)). The J-shaped upturn at higher lifting volumes rests on sparse, self-reported data and does not show harm from structured training, but it does show that her fifth hour of lifting adds little longevity value compared with her first interval session.

### Strength, power and balance come almost free

Grip strength predicts mortality more strongly than blood pressure: in 139,691 adults, each 5 kg lower grip carried a **16% higher risk of death** ([Leong 2015 via Healio](https://www.healio.com/news/cardiology/20150821/grip-strength-may-predict-risk-for-mortality-cvd)), though whether improving grip lowers risk is unknown. Relative sit-to-stand power drops sharply after 50 ([Alcazar 2021](https://doaj.org/article/f0054693f5bf4e24888c7e8abad09ced)) and falls faster in women, 1.5% a year versus 1.0% in men in one Spanish cohort ([Toledo 2025](https://doaj.org/article/e41fe3b487f3490789d34f95f0e2587c)). In adults aged 51–75, failing a 10-second one-leg stand nearly doubled mortality risk (HR 1.84) ([Araujo 2022](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/56/17/975)), and each point on the sit-to-floor-and-rise test was associated with 21% lower mortality ([Brito 2012 via ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2012/12/121213085202.htm)). All of this evidence is observational and from older adults, and a healthy 22-year-old will pass these tests easily. Their value is as lifelong tracking metrics, and the program maintains these qualities nearly for free: the bone jumps double as power training, heavy rows and carries train grip, and single-leg work trains balance.

### Injury prevention targets knees, hamstrings and the pelvic floor

Women tear the ACL about **1.7 times as often as men** per season, rising to 5.5 times in high-impact landing sports such as gymnastics; the often-quoted "up to 8 times" is not supported ([Montalvo 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6561829)). Multicomponent neuromuscular training **roughly halves ACL injury risk (OR 0.51)** ([NMT meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30001501/)), works best when it includes strength and hip and trunk control ([PEDro](https://search.pedro.org.au/search-results/record-detail/57948)) plus verbal feedback ([Sugimoto 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27251898/)), and depends heavily on compliance ([PMC3499896](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3499896)); the evidence comes from sport-playing adolescents, so its value to a gym-only trainee is extrapolated. Programs including Nordic hamstring curls halved hamstring injuries (RR 0.49) ([van Dyk 2019](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/53/21/1362)), though a reappraisal restricted to randomized trials found the result inconclusive (RR 0.59, CI 0.27–1.29) ([UTS](https://opus.lib.uts.edu.au/handle/10453/151354)), and a 2025 meta-analysis against the Copenhagen adductor exercise was retracted, leaving that exercise unresolved ([sponet](https://sponet.de/sponet/Record/4094912)). Jumping carries one female-specific cost: urinary incontinence affects about **26% of female athletes** and up to 76% of volleyball players ([Pires 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7386138)), but pelvic floor muscle training improves strength and reduces leakage ([Sports Health 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11346234)). Leakage during jumps is common, not normal, and trainable.

### Under-eating is the biggest threat to both muscle and bone

The 2023 IOC consensus on Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport (REDs) treats low energy availability as a continuum, with health problems likely at or below **30 kcal per kg of fat-free mass per day**, although that threshold "is not applied universally" ([IOC REDs 2023](https://stillmed.olympics.com/media/Documents/Athletes/Medical-Scientific/Consensus-Statements/REDs/BJSM-IOC-consensus-statement-on-Relative-Energy-Deficiency-in-Sport-REDs.pdf)). Prolonged low energy availability can cause **permanent bone loss**, directly undermining the peak-bone-mass goal ([Human Performance Alliance](https://humanperformancealliance.org/news/2023-international-olympic-committee-consensus-statement-on-reds/)), and because it cannot be measured accurately outside a lab, diagnosis relies on symptoms and the IOC's REDs CAT2 tool ([IOC CAT2](https://stillmed.olympics.com/media/Documents/Athletes/Medical-Scientific/Consensus-Statements/REDs/IOC-REDs-CAT-V2.pdf)). Iron deficiency travels with it. Sports medicine treats **ferritin below about 35 µg/L as stage 1 iron depletion**, below 20 as stage 2 and below 12 as anaemia ([Dr Antti summary](https://drantti.com/iron-deficiency/)); at the 35 cutoff, one study found suboptimal iron in **52% of female athletes versus 15% of male athletes** ([Stanford FASTR](https://fastr.stanford.edu/news/biomarkers/)), population data suggest women's physiological threshold sits above the WHO's 15 µg/L ([NHANES analysis](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10472073/)), and screening is typically annual for low-risk athletes and every 3–6 months for high-risk ones ([Dr Antti](https://drantti.com/iron-deficiency/)). The highest-risk scenario for a woman chasing lean muscle is a fat-loss phase with extra cardio stacked on high training volume, which is exactly where Sims' instinct that women are chronically under-fuelled is most right. One caution for app design follows from how the pill works: withdrawal bleeds on hormonal contraception are drug-induced, so a regular "period" does not confirm adequate energy availability, and other symptoms must carry that signal.

## Natacha Océane's athletic style fits if hard sets stay protected

### "Natasha" is most likely Natacha Océane, at roughly three-in-four odds

The most likely match for a recommended "Natasha" with "really good fun workout programs" is **Natacha Océane**, a London-based creator with about 1.6 million YouTube and 1 million Instagram followers ([Influencer Matchmaker](https://influencermatchmaker.co.uk/influencer/natacha-oceane)). "Natacha" is pronounced like "Natasha," she is the largest creator by that name who sells structured programs, and her content is built around varied, experiment- and challenge-style workouts; the research puts confidence at roughly three in four. The main alternative, Natasha Oakley, is a swimwear and lifestyle figure whose described routine of full-body circuits plus Pilates has no program attached ([Sportskeeda](https://Sportskeeda.com/health-and-fitness/natasha-oakley-s-diet-exercise-routine)), and several smaller "Natasha" coaches sell apps but fit less well. Asking the recommender whether she meant the creator of the BUILD or HYBRID programs would settle it.

Océane trained in biophysics at University College London ([Bustle](https://www.bustle.com/wellness/natacha-ocean-workouts)) and reportedly left a PhD in mass spectrometry to run her channel, after an endurance background that included Ironman ([Generation Iron](https://generationiron.com/natacha-oceane-profile/)); sources disagree on the details. She explains the science at the start of her workout videos and tested cycle-syncing claims on camera in "The Truth About Menstrual 'Cycle Syncing'" ([YouTube](https://m.youtube.com/watch?pp=ygUMI3BlcmlvZHNjeWxl&v=PSYxUn4NsJA)). Her flagship **HYBRID** is a 12-week running-plus-strength program in which users choose a running-focused, strength-focused or 50/50 emphasis, with mostly slow runs, some deliberate speed work and optional 15–20-minute running-strength add-ons ([natachaoceane.com](https://natachaoceane.com/)), sold as a one-off purchase ([Vitality Vixens](https://vitality-vixens.com/index.php/2025/04/23/natacha-oceane-hybrid-program-review/)). **BUILD**, her strength guide, is described by one reviewer as performance-focused rather than appearance-focused and effective for muscle and strength, but heavy on "a lot of plyometric jumping" that may not suit bad knees ([Imogen Molly](https://www.imogenmolly.co.uk/2020/03/natacha-oc-ane-build-review-pros-cons.html)), and a low-quality mirror of her store also lists READY and a HIIT guide ([store mirror](https://instarr.in/responsivl/Training-Guides-Natacha-Oceane-7481411.html)). Her philosophy, inferred from these descriptions rather than stated in an interview, is to train like an athlete, building strength, power and aerobic capacity together, explain the reasons, let people pick an emphasis and progress gradually. For a lean-muscle goal the strength-focused HYBRID option and BUILD fit best. Her Instagram line "You don't have to go heavy, but just really emphasise the core training cues" ([Celebwell](https://celebwell.com/natacha-oceane-fitness-routine/)) is fine for hypertrophy, which happens across a wide range of loads, but some genuinely heavy sets still matter for bone and strength.

### Most fun formats work if they keep effort, rest and volume

The test for any format is whether it preserves the three hypertrophy drivers: enough hard sets, sets ending near failure, and at least 60–90 seconds before the same muscle works again. Several pass with direct evidence: opposing-muscle supersets ([Zhang 2025](https://eprints.leedsbeckett.ac.uk/id/eprint/11908)), cluster sets ([PMC12174233](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12174233/)), rep tempos of 0.5–8 seconds ([Schoenfeld 2015](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/271533635_Effect_of_Repetition_Duration_During_Resistance_Training_on_Muscle_Hypertrophy_A_Systematic_Review_and_Meta-Analysis)) and push-ups matched to the bench press for load and effort ([Kikuchi 2017](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Low-load-bench-press-and-push-up-induce-similar-and-Kikuchi-Nakazato/0942e6f23e9a50972335e6b6d950b3de10e06ebf)). Isometric holds at long muscle lengths ([Stronger By Science](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/isometric-training/)) and plyometrics ([PMC9271893](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9271893/)) build some muscle, but less. Most research on post-activation performance enhancement uses jumps and sprints as the *outcome*, with one cited source finding enhanced jumping from 30 seconds to 12 minutes after a heavy effort ([Blazevich & Babault 2019](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2019.01359/pdf)), so placing jumps first in a session is mainly about doing them fresh, safely and with good landing mechanics. Circuits, EMOMs, AMRAPs, complexes, sleds, carries, kettlebell flows and Animal Flow have no direct hypertrophy trials; they become a problem when they shorten same-muscle rest, drop loads to cardio levels or eat into the time for hard sets.

| Format | Verdict for lean muscle | Where it goes |
|---|---|---|
| Opposing-muscle supersets; tri-sets of non-competing muscles | Supported; counts as hypertrophy volume | Accessory block, ≥90 s before the same muscle repeats |
| Cluster sets, tempo (≤8 s per rep), density blocks at fixed load | Equivalent when effort is matched | Anchor lift or accessories |
| Drop sets, myo-reps | Time-efficient; myo-reps untested | Final set of isolation or machine work only |
| Jumps, medicine-ball throws, short sprints | Power, bone and ACL landing practice; little hypertrophy | Start of session, full recovery, ≤8 min |
| Calisthenics ladders, push-up and inverted-row progressions | Hypertrophic if final sets end at 0–2 RIR | Accessories; skill holds early, while fresh |
| Long-length isometric holds (20–45 s) | Some growth; count as half a set | Accessory or "hold" finisher |
| EMOM, AMRAP, circuit, complex | Conditioning; counts only if sets stay near failure with adequate rest | Finisher, ≤10 min |
| Sled push/pull, loaded carries | Conditioning, grip and trunk; little soreness | Finisher; sleds instead of runs near leg days |
| Animal Flow, ground flows | Mobility and enjoyment | Warm-up or cool-down, 5–8 min |
| Running, cycling, HIIT | No whole-muscle interference; running costs a little at fiber level | Separate day or after lifting; bike near leg days |

### Planned variety helps adherence; random variety doesn't help growth

Changing exercises every session neither adds nor clearly costs overall growth ([Baz-Valle 2019](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/338197575_The_effects_of_exercise_variation_in_muscle_thickness_maximal_strength_and_motivation_in_resistance_trained_men)), while some planned variation improves regional development ([Kassiano 2022](https://journals.lww.com/nsca-jscr/fulltext/2022/06000/does_varying_resistance_exercises_promote_superior.40.aspx)). Enjoyment, meanwhile, falls as intensity climbs past the ventilatory threshold, where breathing becomes laboured, and people feel better at intensities they choose themselves ([Frontiers in Psychology 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2025.1533785/full)). The design that satisfies both findings is a stable core inside a rotating shell: **anchor lifts stay fixed for 4–8 weeks** so progress is measurable, accessories rotate on a schedule within the same movement family, and primers, finishers and conditioning formats rotate freely because progress is not tracked there. The fun should come from athletic, playful or game-like blocks such as throws, sleds, ladders, carries and partner relays rather than gruelling to-the-floor HIIT, with people who genuinely enjoy maximal intensity as the exception. No study has tested whether gamified formats improve long-term adherence and hypertrophy in women, so this is reasoned design, not tested fact.

## The generator should encode three tiers of rules

### Every session runs jumps first, hard sets in the middle, conditioning last

The recommendations converge on a simple architecture: a hypertrophy core driven by a per-muscle set ledger, wrapped in a small, sequenced dose of jumps, heavy sets, intervals, balance and injury prevention, with her cycle and recovery logged as health signals. Every lifting session runs in the same order. It opens with a combined warm-up and neuromuscular block of about 8–10 minutes that doubles as landing practice, moves to jumps or throws while she is fresh, then to the heavy anchor compound, then to supersetted hypertrophy accessories, then to carries or core, and finishes with any conditioning. Hard sets near failure should fill about 70–75% of lifting time, and when a session runs short the finisher is cut first. The 4×4 interval session belongs on an off day or after an upper-body session, never in the 24 hours before a heavy leg or jump day.

|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | Full-body A / B | 50–60 per session, plus optional 10–20-jump home "snacks" | Sprint finisher (3 × 20 s all-out) after both, plus daily walking |
| 3 | Full-body A / B / C | 40–50 per session | One 4×4 interval session plus one sprint finisher |
| 4 | Upper / Lower × 2, or 4 × full-body | 25–40 per session, or 40–50 on lower days | One 4×4 plus one sprint finisher or 20–40 min easy |
| 5 | Lower (glute) / Upper / Lower (quad-ham) / Upper / Full | 25–40 per session | One 4×4 plus one sprint or easy session |

| Creatine | 3–5 g/day monohydrate, daily, no loading (efficacy in women moderate; dose by convention) | [Smith-Ryan 2021](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7998865/) |
| Jumps | 100–200 multidirectional impacts a week over ≥3 days, sets of 5–10, 10–20 s between jumps; progress height, single-leg and direction every 2–4 weeks | [Kato 2006](https://search.pedro.org.au/search-results/record-detail/14578); [2014 meta-analysis](https://www.springermedicine.com/efficiency-of-jumping-exercise-in-improving-bone-mineral-density/22254016); [Robling 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11606612/) |

| Hamstrings | Nordic curls 2–3 × 3–6, 1–2 times a week, lowering-only to start | [van Dyk 2019](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/53/21/1362) |
| Pelvic floor | Ask about leakage each block; if yes, regress to pogo hops and step-downs and suggest pelvic floor training or a physio | [Sports Health 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11346234) |

What the generator should leave out matters as much as what it includes. It should not encode phase-based volume or macronutrient shifts, a hard 30-minute protein window, luteal sodium or heat protocols, "never train fasted" as a rule, or weekly Zone 2 hour quotas, because none of these survives the evidence above. It should encode **red-flag prompts** that send her to a clinician when she reports missed or irregular periods not explained by hormonal contraception, recurrent bone pain or stress fractures, persistent unexplained fatigue, frequent illness or dizziness, or stalled progress despite good adherence, especially during a calorie deficit. During any fat-loss phase it should keep impact work and heavy loading in place, hold protein high and refuse to stack extra cardio on top.

## Conclusion

What is specifically female about training a young woman for muscle sits almost entirely outside the hypertrophy prescription. Sets, reps, effort, rest and progression follow the same rules as for men; the differences live in the guardrails, namely energy availability, iron, a bone-building window that closes during her twenties, pelvic-floor tolerance of impact and a higher ACL risk in landing sports. That inverts the popular framing. The cycle-syncing movement Sims helped popularize puts the female difference in the training plan, where the evidence finds none, while the real differences belong in monitoring, where her concern about chronic under-fuelling is well founded. For the generator, her cycle is a health sensor, not a scheduler.

The second implication concerns marginal returns. A 22-year-old doing three or four hypertrophy sessions a week is already well past the 30–60 minutes where most of lifting's observed longevity benefit sits, so her next 20 minutes buy more as jumps and one hard interval session than as extra sets, and sequenced sensibly they cost her almost no muscle. Natacha Océane's hybrid model, minus its running emphasis, already looks roughly like this. The open question is evidential rather than practical: almost none of the volume, effort, rest or deload numbers have been tested in women specifically, and most session-level rules are coaching convention. The generator should therefore treat its weaker-evidence and expert-opinion values as starting points, log her reps, RIR, symptoms and bodyweight, and let her own response data overrule the defaults over time.
