# Heavier, fewer reps vs moderate weights, more reps: strength, muscle, health and weekly set-up for a 22-year-old woman

Researched 2 November 2026. The offer under review: one heavy main lift (3–6 reps), then two supersets of 6–15 reps, about 15–20 hard sets in a 60-minute session, every set stopped 1–3 reps short of failure (reps in reserve, RIR 1–3). She asked whether she should go heavier with fewer reps, why she should stop short of failure, and whether to add a full-body session every other week.

**Grades:** **[Strong]** = several meta-analyses or a position stand agree. **[Moderate]** = one meta-analysis, or several small RCTs that agree. **[Weak]** = a single small study, a secondary summary, or indirect evidence. **[Opinion]** = expert or coach view, or my own inference. **M-only** = the participants were men only.

Earlier sessions in this project also found pubmed, PMC, Springer, SportRxiv and Stronger By Science blocked. ## 1. Heavy (≥80–85% 1RM, 1–6 reps) vs moderate (8–12) vs light (15+): which builds more strength, which builds more muscle?

### Takeaway
For **maximal strength** (the most she can lift once), heavier is better. The load–strength relationship is a dose-response. Heavy loads beat light loads clearly. Heavy beats moderate (8–15 reps) by less, and not always significantly. For **muscle size**, load barely matters across roughly 30–85% 1RM, provided sets are hard. So heavy work buys extra strength at no cost to muscle per hard set. The main costs are time (longer rests) and fatigue. Part of the strength edge is **skill on the specific lift** she trains heavy. **[Strong]**

### Cited Findings
**Meta-analyses**
- **Lopez et al. 2021, Med Sci Sports Exerc 53(6):1206–1216** (network meta-analysis). 28 studies, 747 healthy adults, all sets to volitional failure. Load groups: low (>15RM), moderate (9–15RM), high (≤8RM). **Hypertrophy:** no difference between loads (overall P = 0.113–0.469; subgroups P = 0.871–0.995), so growth "seems to be load independent." **Strength:** high- and moderate-load both beat low-load. High beat moderate only nominally (P = 0.068, not significant). Untrained people gained more muscle. In trained people, more sessions gave better gains. **[Strong]** for equal hypertrophy; **[Moderate]** for heavy > moderate on strength. — [PubMed 33433148](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33433148/); [JYX repository record](https://jyx.jyu.fi/handle/123456789/75929)
- **Schoenfeld, Grgic, Ogborn & Krieger 2017, JSCR 31:3508–3523.** 21 studies, ≤60% vs >60% 1RM, all sets to failure, ≥6 weeks. **1RM gains were significantly greater with high loads.** Isometric strength did not differ. Hypertrophy was similar ("muscle hypertrophy can be equally achieved across a spectrum of loading ranges"). A secondary source puts the hypertrophy effect-size difference at 0.03 (95% CI −0.16 to 0.22). I could not verify that figure, or retrieve the 1RM effect size. **[Strong]** — [PubMed 28834797](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28834797/); [GetFitCraft summary (secondary)](https://getfitcraft.com/science/light-weights-build-muscle)
- An earlier Schoenfeld 2016 meta-analysis (Eur J Sport Sci) reported only a *trend* for greater strength with high vs low loads (difference = 1.07 ± 0.60; p = 0.09). It is a different paper and should not be mixed with the 2017 one. **[Moderate]** — [BISp record](https://www.bisp-surf.de/Record/PU201603001398)
- **Carvalho et al. 2022, Appl Physiol Nutr Metab** (meta-analysis, volume load matched; "high load" = ≥80% 1RM or ≤7RM). For 1RM, high beat both low and moderate, and moderate beat low. **Hypertrophy did not differ between loads.** **[Moderate–Strong]** — [PubMed 35015560](https://www.pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35015560/); [AMiner record](https://aminer.org/pub/61e002845244ab9dcb2718b7/muscle-hypertrophy-and-strength-gains-after-resistance-training-with-different-volume-matched)
- **Schoenfeld, Grgic, Van Every & Plotkin 2021, *Sports* 9(2):32** ("Loading Recommendations… A Re-Examination of the Repetition Continuum"). The research "tends to support a dose–response relationship between load and strength gains." The heavy-load advantage "dissipates when testing is carried out on a modality different than that used in the study training program." In other words, heavy training's extra strength is partly specific to the trained lift and testing method. Muscular adaptations "can be obtained, and in some cases optimized, across a wide spectrum of loading zones." A Spanish summary adds: similar growth with loads ≥~30% 1RM, no single "ideal hypertrophy zone," and moderate loads as the most time-efficient option in practice. I could not confirm the exact phrase "strength is load-specific" in the paper. **[Moderate–Strong]** — [DOAJ record](https://doaj.org/article/6d51b905504d46f0873bb43d5d84bf21); [MDPI article](https://www.mdpi.com/2075-4663/9/2/32); [Fisiología del Ejercicio summary](https://www.fisiologiadelejercicio.com/recomendaciones-de-carga-en-el-entrenamiento-de-fuerza-para-mejora-de-la-hipertrofia/)

**Key RCTs (mostly men)**
- **Kubo, Ikebukuro & Yata 2021, JSCR 35(4):879–885** (42 men; 4RM × 7 sets vs 8RM × 4 vs 12RM × 3 vs control; volume-equated; 2×/week; 10 weeks; bench press). Pectoralis muscle volume gains (MRI) did not differ between the three protocols. The relative 1RM gain was **significantly lower with 12RM than with 4RM (p = 0.029) or 8RM (p = 0.021)**. No 4RM vs 8RM difference was reported. Note that matching volume needed **7 sets of 4RM** against 3 sets of 12RM. **[Moderate]**, M-only. — [PubMed 32304514](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32304514/); [Fisiología del Ejercicio summary](https://www.fisiologiadelejercicio.com/efectos-de-protocolos-de-entrenamiento-de-fuerza-con-4-8-y-12-repeticiones-maximas-sobre-la-hipertrofia-y-la-fuerza/)
- **Lasevicius et al. 2018, Eur J Sport Sci 18(6):772–780** (30 men; within-subject, one limb at 20% 1RM and the other at 40%, 60% or 80%; volume load equated; 2×/week; 12 weeks). 20% 1RM was suboptimal. **Only 80% 1RM beat the low intensities for strength and muscle CSA.** The authors call 40–80% 1RM "viable options to increase muscle mass." **[Moderate]**, M-only. — [BVS/MEDLINE 29564973](https://search.bvsalud.org/portal/resource/pt/mdl-29564973); [USP repository](https://repositorio.usp.br/item/002898683)
- **Lasevicius et al. 2022 (JSCR 36(2):346–351)** (25 untrained men, knee extension, 8 weeks). Strength rose **~33–34% with 80% 1RM vs ~16–18% with 30% 1RM**, so heavy roughly doubled the strength gain over light. Growth was similar (80% to failure +8.1%, 80% non-failure +7.7%, 30% to failure +7.8%) except light sets stopped short of failure (+2.8%, not significant). **[Moderate]**, M-only. — [PubMed 31895290](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31895290/)
- **Mattocks et al. 2017, Med Sci Sports Exerc ("Practicing the Test Produces Strength Equivalent to Higher Volume Training")** (38 untrained people, 8 weeks, knee extension and chest press). One group did only up to five single-rep max attempts per visit. The other did 4 sets of ~8–12RM to failure. **1RM gains did not differ**, but the high-volume group grew more muscle at most sites. So 1RM strength is largely a specific skill of lifting heavy, while muscle growth needs volume. **[Moderate]** (sex mix not retrieved). — [PubMed 28463902](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28463902/); [Rowan University record](https://www.researchwithrowan.com/en/publications/practicing-the-test-produces-strength-equivalent-to-higher-volume/)
- **Schoenfeld et al. 2014, JSCR 28:2909–2918** (well-trained men, volume-equated 7 × 3 "powerlifting" vs 3 × 10 "bodybuilding," 8 weeks). Secondary sources say biceps growth was similar and strength favoured 7 × 3. I could not verify the numbers, and the secondary sources conflict. **[Weak]**, M-only. — [PDF host (blocked here)](https://bretcontreras.com/wp-content/uploads/Effects-of-different-volume-equated-resistance-training-loading-strategies-on-muscular-adaptations-in-well-trained-men.pdf); [SuppVersity summary](https://suppversity.blogspot.com/2014/07/lower-rep-numbers-power-up-strength.html); [Barbell Medicine forum (secondary)](https://forum.barbellmedicine.com/t/hypertrophy-and-metabolic-stress-from-high-reps/754)

**Women-only trials (small, short, mostly untrained)**
- **Stefanaki et al. 2019** (13 young women; one limb at 80% and the other at 30% 1RM, both to failure; 6 weeks). Gains in muscle thickness and strength did not differ between loads. **[Weak]** — [Glasgow ePrints](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/171849)
- **Cholewa et al. 2018, JSCR** (20 untrained young/collegiate women; 4 × 5–7 heavy vs 2 × 10–14 moderate, to failure; 8 weeks). Both were effective for muscle growth, body composition, strength and power. Per the search summary, no clear winner. **[Weak]** — [PubMed 28699923](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28699923/) (record-to-study mapping from search summary)
- **Alegre et al. 2015, Muscle & Nerve** (15 women; one leg with load rising from 50% to 80% 1RM, the other at 50% with rising volume; 10 weeks). Gains in size and strength were significant and comparable. **[Weak]** — [Zendy record](https://zendy.io/title/10.1002/mus.24271)
- Two registered women-only heavy-vs-light trials have no retrievable results yet: UBC FHALL (NCT04547972; 16 young women, ~80% vs ~30% 1RM) and HER-TRAIN (ISRCTN95727228). — [ClinicalTrials.gov](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT04547972); [ISRCTN](https://www.isrctn.com/ISRCTN95727228)

**Rep-to-load conversion (for programming)**
- Nuzzo et al. 2024 (Sports Med; 269 studies, 7,289 people). The classic table holds as a rough guide: about 4 reps at 90%, 6 at 85% and 8 at 80% 1RM. Sex and training status did not clearly moderate it, but exercise did (more reps on leg press than bench at the same %). **[Strong]** — [PMC10933212](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10933212)

### Inferences
- **Heavy vs light:** heavy gives clearly more 1RM strength, roughly double the gain in one direct comparison (Lasevicius 2022: ~33% vs ~17%). **Heavy vs moderate (8–12):** heavy is usually somewhat better for 1RM, but the gap is small and not always significant (Lopez P = 0.068; Kubo found 4RM ≈ 8RM > 12RM). **[Moderate]**
- **Does heavy cost muscle?** No, per hard set. It only costs muscle if switching to heavy means doing *fewer hard sets in total*, or so few reps that weekly volume falls (Kubo needed 7 × 4RM to match 3 × 12RM on volume load). **[Moderate]**
- The 3–6-rep main lift in the offered plan is the "heavy" part. The 6–15-rep supersets are where most of the muscle growth comes from, and they still build strength (moderate loads beat light loads for strength). The evidence supports a **mix**, not "all heavy." **[Moderate–Strong]**
- Because strength is partly a lift-specific skill (Schoenfeld 2021; Mattocks 2017), heavy sets matter most on the lifts she wants to be strong at (squat, hinge, press, pull). Accessories can stay in moderate rep ranges. **[Moderate]**
- The women-only data agree with the mixed-sex data: muscle grows similarly across loads. They show smaller heavy-vs-moderate strength gaps than the male data, probably because beginners gain from learning the lift whatever the load. **[Weak]**

### Gaps
- Exact pooled 1RM effect sizes (heavy vs moderate, heavy vs light) from Lopez 2021, Schoenfeld 2017 and Carvalho 2022 were not retrievable (full texts blocked).
- I could not verify Schoenfeld 2021's specific practical prescription (e.g., what share of training should be ≥80% 1RM) or the "strength is load-specific" wording.
- From background knowledge only, **unverified here**: Schoenfeld 2014 may have reported more joint complaints and fatigue in the 7 × 3 group. Do not use it as a finding.
- I found no women-only trial in *trained* women comparing 3–6 vs 8–12 reps for 1RM strength.

## 2. How many sets and reps does strength actually need? Could she do fewer, heavier sets and still grow muscle?

### Takeaway
Strength needs a **small dose** of heavy, focused practice: about **2–3 hard sets per lift per session**, with **frequency** (practising the lift ~2×/week) mattering more than piling on sets. Muscle growth needs **more weekly sets** (≥10 per muscle per week). So she can make the main lift heavier and use fewer reps without losing muscle, provided the accessory supersets keep weekly sets per muscle at ~10–20. **[Moderate–Strong]**

### Cited Findings
- **Androulakis-Korakakis et al. 2020, Sports Medicine** (minimum effective dose; 6 studies; resistance-trained men; PROSPERO CRD42018108911). In every included study, a **single set done 1–3×/week** produced significant 1RM gains. The authors' minimal prescription: **one set of 6–12 reps at ~70–85% 1RM, near failure, 2–3×/week for 8–12 weeks**, which gave "suboptimal, yet significant" squat and bench gains. They note there were **no data on women or the deadlift**. **[Moderate]**, M-only. — [PubMed 31797219](https://www.pubmed.gov/31797219/); [Bond University record](https://research.bond.edu.au/en/publications/the-minimum-effective-training-dose-required-for-1rm-strength-in-/)
- The same group's 2021 powerlifter project (Frontiers in Sports and Active Living) is summarised by a third party as finding **~3–6 sets per lift per week at high loads, near failure** to be the most effective minimal structure. **[Weak]** (third-party summary). — [Frontiers PDF](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2021.713655/pdf); [MTN Tactical review](https://mtntactical.com/knowledge/research-review-minimum-training-dose-for-1rm-strength-improvement-in-trained-lifters/)
- **Ralston et al. 2017, Sports Medicine 47:2585–2601** (9 studies, 61 treatment groups). There is a graded dose-response between weekly sets and strength. Medium vs low weekly sets: ES 0.15 (95% CI 0.01–0.30; p = 0.04), a small difference. In novice and intermediate men, low weekly sets gave smaller gains than medium or high. For advanced trainees, medium or high weekly sets "may be an appropriate dose." Stronger By Science describes the categories as ≤5 vs 6–10+ sets per exercise per week, with a small overall effect (ES 0.18). The paper's own cut-offs could not be confirmed. **[Moderate]**, mostly men. — [PubMed 28755103](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28755103/); [PMC5684266](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5684266/); [Stronger By Science](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/?p=52397)
- **Pelland et al. 2025/2026, Sports Medicine** (67 studies, ~2,058 participants, Bayesian dose-response). More weekly sets increase both strength and hypertrophy, but **strength shows more pronounced diminishing returns**. **Frequency had a 100% posterior probability of a positive effect on strength** (diminishing returns). For hypertrophy the frequency effect was compatible with negligible. **[Moderate–Strong]** — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/398333408_The_Resistance_Training_Dose_Response_Meta-Regressions_Exploring_the_Effects_of_Weekly_Volume_and_Frequency_on_Muscle_Hypertrophy_and_Strength_Gains_The_Resistance_Training_Dose_ResponseJ_C_Pelland_et_al); [train.fitness summary](https://train.fitness/personal-trainer-blogs/not-all-sets-are-created-equal)
- **Remmert, Pelland, Robinson, Hinson & Zourdos 2025 (SportRxiv preprint, not peer-reviewed).** Per session, added benefit becomes undetectable beyond **~2 direct sets for strength** and ~11 fractional sets per muscle for hypertrophy. The authors suggest more frequent, shorter exposures for strength. **[Weak]** — [SportRxiv 537](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/537)
- **ACSM 2026 Position Stand** (overview of 137 systematic reviews, >30,000 participants), per secondary reporting. For strength: **≥80% 1RM, 2–3 sets per exercise, ≥2×/week**. For hypertrophy: **30–100% 1RM** works when effort is high, with **≥10 sets per muscle per week**. Stopping at ~1–2 RIR works about as well as failure when volume is matched. **[Strong, guideline level]** — [Rep Fitness summary](https://repfitness.com/blogs/training/acsm-updated-resistance-training-guidelines); [Newswise/ACSM release](https://www.newswise.com/articles/acsm-unveils-landmark-2026-resistance-training-guidelines-first-update-in-17-years); [ACSM infographic](https://acsm.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Resistance-Training-Position-Stand-infographic.pdf)
- **Grgic et al. 2018, Sports Medicine** (frequency and strength). Higher frequency gave a small overall strength benefit (d = 0.265), but none when volume was equated (p = 0.421). A subgroup showed a significant frequency effect in **women** (p = 0.030), not volume-equated. **[Weak–Moderate]** — [PubMed 29470825](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29470825)
- Mattocks 2017 (see Q1): heavy single-rep practice alone matched 4 × 8–12 for 1RM gains in untrained people, but grew less muscle. **[Moderate]** — [PubMed 28463902](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28463902/)

### Inferences
- **Yes, she can do fewer, heavier sets for strength.** About 3–5 heavy sets of the main lift per session, 2 sessions a week per lift (one heavy, one lighter), covers what strength needs. Going beyond ~5 heavy sets mostly adds fatigue. **[Moderate]**
- **Keep the supersets for muscle.** Most weekly hypertrophy volume should come from 6–15-rep work, so that each muscle gets ≥10 (ideally 12–20) fractional sets a week. **[Strong]** for the ≥10 floor; **[Opinion]** for the exact range in her case.
- **15–20 hard sets per 60 minutes is not "too many reps"** for a two-muscle-group session (~7–10 sets per muscle, under the ~11-per-session ceiling). It is more than strength alone needs, but strength isn't her only goal. Heavy sets need 2.5–4 min of rest, so a session with a bigger heavy block will hold **fewer total hard sets (~14–18)** in 60 minutes. That trade is fine. **[Opinion]**, grounded in Remmert/Pelland and ACSM.
- **A strength-focused but muscle-friendly week:** main lift **4–5 × 3–5 at ~80–87% 1RM (RIR 1–3)**, then a secondary compound **2–3 × 5–8 (~75–82%, RIR 1–2)**, then accessories **2–3 × 8–15 (RIR 0–2)**. By Nuzzo's table, 3–5 reps at 80–87% leaves roughly 1–4 RIR, so the percentages and the RIR targets agree. **[Opinion]**, consistent with ACSM, Androulakis-Korakakis and Kubo.

### Gaps
- Pelland 2025's exact strength-volume curve values (predicted % 1RM gain at 4, 8, 12 or 16 sets/week) were not retrievable.
- The minimum-dose literature has **no women** and **no deadlift** data (stated by the authors).
- I found no study testing a "heavy main lift + moderate supersets" hybrid against all-heavy or all-moderate in women.

## 3. Is heavy lifting healthy and safe for a young woman? Injury rates, joints and tendons, bone, technique, failure

### Takeaway
Heavy lifting is one of the **lower-injury-risk** ways to train. Injury rates in weight-training sports are about **1–4 per 1,000 hours**, and lower (~0.24–1) for bodybuilding-style training. That is well below team sports such as soccer and basketball. Heavy loading also gives **bone and tendon** a stimulus that light loads may not. The safety levers are technique, a gradual warm-up ramp, small load jumps, stopping 1–3 reps short of failure on heavy compounds, and not lifting heavy legs when already fatigued. **[Moderate]**

### Cited Findings
**Injury rates**
- **Keogh & Winwood 2017, Sports Medicine 47(3):479–501** (systematic review, weight-training sports). Bodybuilding: **~0.24–1 injury per 1,000 h**. Most weight-training sports: **~2–4 per 1,000 h** and ~1–2 injuries per athlete per year. Strongman (4.5–6.1/1,000 h) and Highland Games (7.5/1,000 h) are the highest. The authors conclude these rates are "relatively low" compared with common team sports. Most studies were retrospective, and the samples were competitive lifters training near-maximal loads (mostly men). **[Moderate]** — [Bond University record](https://research.bond.edu.au/en/publications/the-epidemiology-of-injuries-across-the-weight-training-sports/); [USC record](https://research.usc.edu.au/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/The-Epidemiology-of-Injuries-Across-the/99451096702621)
- The NSCA estimates resistance-training injuries at **0.24–1 per 1,000 training hours** and says running can be 2–10 times higher. **[Weak–Moderate]** (professional-body article, secondary) — [NSCA, "The Safest and Riskiest Forms of Resistance Training"](https://www.nsca.com/education/articles/ptq/the-safest-and-riskiest-forms-of-resistance-training/)
- One comparison table puts **soccer at 7.21, basketball at 4.31 and weight training at 1.12 injuries per 1,000 h**. I did not open the table; the number-to-source mapping comes from the search tool. **[Weak]** — [IJERPH 2021 table via PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8125505/table/ijerph-18-04857-t002)
- A 1993 study of adult fitness participants: running had a higher injury risk than most other individual activities, and weight work fell in the low-to-medium range. **[Weak]** — [PubMed 8346764](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8346764/)

**Bone (young women)**
- **Mosti et al. 2014, JSCR 28(10)** (30 healthy women, 22 ± 2 y, randomised). 12 weeks of **squat maximal-strength training at 85–90% 1RM** with fast concentric intent. **Lumbar-spine BMD +2.2%, total-hip BMD +1.0%**, bone-formation marker P1NP +26.2%, and **squat 1RM +97.7%**. The authors call it "a simple, time-efficient strategy to optimize peak bone mass in early adulthood." **[Weak–Moderate]** (small, short). — [PubMed 24736773](https://www.pubmed.gov/24736773/); [NTNU Open](https://ntnuopen.ntnu.no/ntnu-xmlui/handle/11250/263187)
- **LIFTMOR (Watson et al. 2018, JBMR 33(2):211)** (101 postmenopausal women with low bone mass). **5 × 5 at >85% 1RM**, twice weekly, supervised, was safe and improved spine and hip BMD. This shows heavy lifting is tolerated even by a fragile population. Different age group. **[Moderate]** — [JBMR](https://academic.oup.com/jbmr/article/33/2/211/7605709)

**Tendons and joints**
- **Bohm, Mersmann & Arampatzis 2015, Sports Medicine – Open 1:7** (27 studies, 37 interventions, 264 healthy adults). Training increased tendon stiffness (SMD 0.70) and material quality (Young's modulus SMD 0.69). **"Stiffness adaptation significantly depends on loading intensity," not contraction type.** Interventions of ≥12 weeks had larger effects. **[Moderate]** — [Sports Med Open PDF](https://sportsmedicine-open.springeropen.com/track/pdf/10.1186/s40798-015-0009-9)
- Kubo et al. 2006 (9 participants): tendon stiffness rose only with **80% 1RM** training, not with 20% 1RM plus blood-flow restriction. **[Weak]** — [PubMed 16871002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16871002/). Seynnes et al. 2022 (MSSE) found low-load blood-flow restriction and high-load training gave comparable patellar-tendon changes, so the evidence conflicts. **[Weak]** — [NIH Brage PDF](https://nih.brage.unit.no/nih-xmlui/bitstream/handle/11250/3067391/Seynnes%2BMSSE%2B2022%2BLow-load.pdf?sequence=1)
- In older adults over 12 months, moderate and heavy loads grew patellar-tendon CSA equally, but only heavy loads appeared to preserve tendon mechanical properties. Different population. **[Weak]** — [PMC6357404](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6357404/)

**Fatigue, recovery and stopping short (safety angle)**
- **Morán-Navarro et al. 2017, Eur J Appl Physiol 117:2387–2399** (10 trained men, squat and bench). 3 × 10 to failure caused larger acute drops in jump height and bar speed and **slower recovery at 24–48 h** than 3 × 5 or 6 × 5 with the same load. Muscle-damage markers (CK) were higher at 24 and 48 h. Conclusion: avoiding failure leaves athletes "in a better neuromuscular condition" sooner. **[Moderate]**, M-only. — [SpringerMedizin](https://www.springermedizin.de/time-course-of-recovery-following-resistance-training-leading-or/15095268); [Universidad de Murcia record](https://portalinvestigacion.um.es/documentos/5d92fd9b299952412b477a2c)
- **Pareja-Blanco et al. 2017, Scand J Med Sci Sports** (22 young men, squat, 8 weeks). Stopping sets at **20% velocity loss** (well short of failure) gave **similar squat strength with ~40% fewer reps**, and a **larger jump gain (+9.5% vs +3.5%)**, than 40% velocity loss (closer to failure). VL40 gave more quadriceps hypertrophy and lost fast type IIX fibres; VL20 kept them. **[Moderate]**, M-only. — [Zendy record](https://zendy.io/title/10.1111/sms.12678); [Universidad Loyola record](https://www.uloyola.es/en/research-transfer/publications/effects-of-velocity-loss-during-resistance-training-on-athletic-performance-strength-gains-and-muscle-adaptations-article)
- **Pareja-Blanco et al. 2020** (64 trained men, bench, velocity loss 0/15/25/50%). Strength gains did not differ "despite the wide differences in the total volume." VL50 grew the pecs more. **[Moderate]**, M-only. — [idUS repository](https://idus.us.es/handle/11441/139806)
- **Carroll et al. 2019, IJSPP** (15 well-trained men). Sets to failure created more "training strain" and **smaller gains in jump, rate of force development and maximal strength** than sets stopped short. **[Weak–Moderate]**, M-only. — [Sacred Heart repository](https://digitalcommons.sacredheart.edu/pthms_exscifac/14)
- Sibling-note finding: no study has measured **injury rates** for compounds taken to failure vs stopped short. The safety argument rests on fatigue data and expert opinion. **[Opinion]** — see *training_to_failure.md*, Q3.

**Women-specific context**
- Judge & Burke 2010 (small sample): after a heavy low-rep bench session, women showed no strength loss at 4 h or 24 h, while men did. **[Weak]** — [PubMed 20625191](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20625191/)
- Ratamess et al. 2008 (46 trained women): when training alone, women self-selected only **~42% 1RM**, vs ~51% with a trainer. The usual risk for women is lifting *too light*, not too heavy. **[Moderate]** — [PubMed 18296962](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18296962/)

### Inferences
- **Healthier?** Heavy lifting is not less healthy than moderate-rep lifting. For a 22-year-old woman it may be *better* for bone (Mosti) and tendon stiffness (Bohm), and its injury rate is low compared with the sports and HIIT-style training she already does. Most injury risk comes from poor technique, grinding all-out reps, big jumps in load and lifting heavy while fatigued, not from heaviness itself. **[Moderate]** for the rates; **[Opinion]** for the attribution.
- **Practical safety rules [Opinion]:** (1) 2–4 ramp-up warm-up sets before the first heavy set (e.g., bar × 8, ~50% × 5, ~70% × 3, ~80–85% × 1–2). (2) Stop each heavy set when bar speed slows sharply or form changes, aiming for RIR 1–3 (2–3 on squat and deadlift). (3) Increase load in small steps of 2.5–5% once she hits the top of the rep range at the target RIR. (4) No true 1RM testing is needed; estimate from rep-max sets with Nuzzo's table. (5) Use safety pins or spotters for squat and bench.
- Stopping short also makes her **more athletic**: in Pareja-Blanco 2017 it kept fast fibres and jump power better than grinding sets. **[Moderate]**, M-only.

### Gaps
- No prospective injury data specific to recreational young women lifting heavy (≥85% 1RM) were found. Keogh & Winwood is mostly retrospective and mostly men.
- I found no trial comparing joint pain or tendon complaints between heavy (3–6) and moderate (8–12) programmes in women.
- The Mosti 2014 abstract did not report adverse events.

## 4. Why stop 1–3 reps short of failure? (plain language, evidence, exceptions)

### Takeaway
**In plain words:** almost all the strength benefit, and nearly all the muscle benefit, come *before* the last one or two reps. Those reps cost the most fatigue, recovery time and form breakdown. Stopping 1–3 reps short gives about the same results, lets her train each lift again sooner and better, keeps heavy lifts safer, and keeps her fast and springy for her classes. **For strength, closeness to failure barely matters. For muscle, close (0–3 RIR) is good and true failure adds at most a small bonus.** **[Strong]** for strength; **[Moderate]** for hypertrophy.

### Cited Findings
- **Robinson et al. 2024, Sports Medicine 54(9):2209–2231** (meta-regressions). "Strength gains were similar across a wide range of RIR, while muscle hypertrophy improves as sets are terminated closer to failure." **[Strong]** for strength; **[Moderate]** for hypertrophy. — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-024-02069-2)
- **Refalo et al. 2023, Sports Medicine 53(3):649–665** (15 studies). Failure gave only a "trivial" hypertrophy advantage (ES = 0.19, 95% CI 0.00–0.37), with no clear advantage when strict momentary failure was used. **[Moderate]** — [PubMed 36334240](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36334240/)
- **Grgic et al. 2022, J Sport Health Sci** (15 studies, young adults). Failure vs non-failure: strength ES −0.09 (95% CI −0.22 to 0.05) and hypertrophy ES 0.22 (−0.11 to 0.55), both not significant. **[Moderate]** — [DOAJ record](https://doaj.org/article/dc36ba7ccd994798964f66b93bd8f662)
- **Refalo et al. 2024, J Sports Sci 42(1):85–101** (trained, 12 men + **6 women**, one leg per condition). Quadriceps growth was **identical** with failure (0.181 cm) and with 1–2 RIR (0.182 cm). Failure "consistently induces higher levels of acute neuromuscular fatigue." **[Moderate]** — [Taylor & Francis](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/02640414.2024.2321021); [PubMed 38393985](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38393985/)
- **Hermann et al. 2025, MSSE** (42 trained men **and women**). One set to failure vs 2 RIR: strength gains similar; hypertrophy "tended to favor" failure, but differences were "generally modest." **[Moderate]** — [PubMed 40249908](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40249908/)
- **Martorelli et al. 2017** (89 active young **women**, biceps, 70% 1RM, 10 weeks; the only women-only failure trial found). Strength rose similarly with or without failure. Fast-speed peak torque improved only in the non-failure groups. **[Moderate]** — [PubMed 28713535](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28713535/)
- **ACSM 2026:** training to momentary failure "did not consistently impact training outcomes." **[Strong, guideline level]** — [Newswise/ACSM](https://www.newswise.com/articles/acsm-unveils-landmark-2026-resistance-training-guidelines-first-update-in-17-years)
- **Recovery cost:** failure slows recovery by 24–48 h (Morán-Navarro 2017) and causes more acute fatigue (Refalo 2024). Stopping well short kept strength gains and improved jumping (Pareja-Blanco 2017). Sources are in Q3. **[Moderate]**
- **Exception, light loads:** with light loads, stopping short costs muscle. Lasevicius 2022: 30% 1RM stopped short of failure gave +2.8% growth (not significant), vs +7.8% when taken to failure. **[Moderate]**, M-only. — [PubMed 31895290](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31895290/)
- **Judging RIR:** people under-predict how many reps they have left by ~0.95 reps on average. Accuracy is better with heavier loads and with ≤12-rep sets (Halperin 2022). In a bench-press study with 12 trained women and 12 trained men, the mean error was 0.65 ± 0.78 reps, with no sex effect (Refalo 2024). **[Moderate]** — [PubMed 34542869](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34542869/); [Refalo 2024 RIR accuracy PDF (ResearchGate)](https://www.researchgate.net/profile/Martin-Refalo-2/publication/375687905_Accuracy_of_Intraset_Repetitions-in-Reserve_Predictions_During_the_Bench_Press_Exercise_in_Resistance-Trained_Male_and_Female_Subjects/links/65d9591fadc608480ae7db13/Accuracy-of-Intraset-Repetitions-in-Reserve-Predictions-During-the-Bench-Press-Exercise-in-Resistance-Trained-Male-and-Female-Subjects.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Plain-language script for her [Opinion]:** "Lift until you could do only 1–3 more good reps, then rack it. Science shows that gets you just as strong and almost exactly as muscular as going all-out. You recover faster, your form stays clean on the heavy stuff, and you show up fresher for HIIT and your next lift."
- **Exceptions [Opinion, backed by the Moderate evidence above]:**
  - **Heavy barbell squat or deadlift, 3–6 reps:** RIR 2–3. These carry the most fatigue and the hardest bail-outs.
  - **Bench, overhead press, rows, pull-ups, 3–8 reps:** RIR 1–2.
  - **Moderate accessories, 8–12:** RIR 1–2.
  - **Light or high-rep sets (15–20+), band and bodyweight work:** RIR 0–1, since true failure is fine and often needed (Lasevicius).
  - **Last set of isolation or machine moves** (curls, lateral raises, leg extension, leg curl, calf raise, cable work): taking it to failure is fine.
- Beginners usually *underestimate* how many reps they have left, so her "2 left" may really be 3–4. An occasional all-out set on a safe machine exercise helps her calibrate. **[Moderate]**

### Gaps
- I found no injury data for failure vs non-failure on compound lifts.
- I found no trial testing different RIR targets for women vs men.

## 5. Should she add a full-body session every other week inside a 4-day split?

### Takeaway
**Optional. It's fine but not the key lever.** The evidence says (a) split and full-body routines give the same strength and muscle when weekly volume matches, and (b) **strength improves when each lift is practised ~2×/week**. A fortnightly full-body day does not deliver (b) on its own. The better fix is to give each main lift a second, lighter exposure *every* week inside the 4-day split. If she likes the variety, the full-body day should **replace** one split day every other week (not be added on top) and work as a "big-lift practice" day. **[Moderate]** for the evidence; **[Opinion]** for the format.

### Cited Findings
- **Ramos-Campo et al. 2024, JSCR 38(7):1330–1340** (meta-analysis, 14 studies, 4–12 weeks, 12 of 14 volume-matched). No significant difference between split and full-body routines for strength or hypertrophy when volume is equated. Choose by "personal preference." **[Moderate]** — [PubMed 38595233](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38595233/)
- **Pelland 2025:** frequency raises strength (posterior probability 100%, diminishing returns); its effect on hypertrophy is compatible with negligible. **[Moderate–Strong]** — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/398333408_The_Resistance_Training_Dose_Response_Meta-Regressions_Exploring_the_Effects_of_Weekly_Volume_and_Frequency_on_Muscle_Hypertrophy_and_Strength_Gains_The_Resistance_Training_Dose_ResponseJ_C_Pelland_et_al)
- **Remmert 2025 preprint:** ~2 direct sets per session is enough for strength, which supports short, frequent exposures. **[Weak]** — [SportRxiv 537](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/537)
- **ACSM 2026:** train all major muscle groups ≥2 days/week; strength work ≥2×/week. **[Strong, guideline level]** — [ACSM infographic](https://acsm.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Resistance-Training-Position-Stand-infographic.pdf); [Rep Fitness summary](https://repfitness.com/blogs/training/acsm-updated-resistance-training-guidelines)
- **Grgic 2018:** a small frequency benefit for strength overall, significant in the women subgroup (not volume-equated). **[Weak–Moderate]** — [PubMed 29470825](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29470825)

### Inferences
- **Recommendation [Opinion]:** Yes to the fortnightly full-body day if she enjoys it. It adds variety and an extra practice of every big lift. But first make sure each main lift appears twice in a normal week: a heavy day (4–5 × 3–5) plus a lighter day (2–3 × 6–8 at RIR 2–3, or a close variation such as paused squat, RDL, incline press or chin-up). That is where the strength-frequency benefit comes from.
- **How to run the full-body day [Opinion]:** it replaces one of the 4 split days in alternating weeks. Train 3–4 main lifts (squat, hinge, a press, a pull) × **2–3 sets of 3–5 at RIR 2–3**, i.e., heavy but crisp practice. Add 1–2 short supersets (2 × 8–12). Total ~12–15 hard sets. Keep weekly sets per muscle roughly unchanged, so muscle growth is unaffected (Ramos-Campo).
- **Good weeks for it:** weeks with **3 classes**, when a lower-volume full-body practice day is easier to recover from than a high-volume leg day. **[Opinion]**
- **Alternating weeks** (split one week, split + full-body the next) has no specific evidence for or against. Expect no penalty as long as weekly sets per muscle stay steady. **[Opinion]**

### Gaps
- I found no study of alternating-week structures, or of a full-body day every other week.
- Ramos-Campo's participant sex mix and training status were not retrievable.

## 6. Placing heavy lifting days around HIIT, tabata and a leg-heavy strength class

### Takeaway
Overall, cardio/HIIT does not reduce long-term strength or muscle gains. But **hard leg conditioning shortly before heavy squats or deadlifts lowers the quality of that session**, and same-session conditioning before lifting hurts lower-body strength gains. Rules: **heavy lower-body days should not fall within ~24 h after HIIT or tabata, nor within ~48 h after the leg-heavy strength class**. If a class and a lift share a day, **lift first** and separate them by ≥3 h if possible. Upper-body heavy days are the ones to put the day after a class. **[Moderate]** for direction; **[Opinion]** for exact hours.

### Cited Findings
- **Schumann et al. 2022, Sports Medicine 52:601–612** (43 studies). Concurrent aerobic + strength training **did not compromise hypertrophy (SMD −0.01) or maximal strength**. **Explosive strength was attenuated (SMD −0.28)**, more so when both were in the **same session**. A preprint subgroup found the attenuation was significant for same-session training (p = 0.043) but not with **≥3 h** separation (not confirmed in the published version). **[Moderate]** — [PubMed 34757594](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34757594/); [PMC8891239](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8891239/); [SportRxiv preprint 37](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/37)
- **Eddens, van Someren & Howatson 2018, Sports Medicine 48(1):177–188** (10 studies, ≥5-week programmes). **Resistance before endurance** in the same session improved **lower-body dynamic strength by ~6.91%** (95% CI 1.96–11.87; p = 0.006) vs the reverse order. No order effect on hypertrophy, static strength, VO₂max or body fat. **[Moderate]** — [Northumbria research portal](https://researchportal.northumbria.ac.uk/en/publications/the-role-of-intra-session-exercise-sequence-on-the-interference-e/); [NRL e-print](https://nrl.northumbria.ac.uk/id/eprint/31702)
- **Murlasits et al. 2018, J Sports Sci 36(11):1212–1219.** Lower-body 1RM was higher when strength preceded endurance (pooled +3.96 kg; 95% CI 0.81–7.10). Aerobic gains were unaffected by order. **[Moderate]** — [PubMed 28783467](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28783467/)
- **Sporer & Wenger 2003, JSCR 17(4)** (16 men). Leg strength performance was impaired **4 h and 8 h after interval cycling**, and **only in the muscles used**. HIIT vs steady cycling made no difference. The authors advised **~24 h** between aerobic and strength sessions when strength quality matters. **[Weak]**, M-only. — [Kravitz/UNM concurrent training review](https://www.unm.edu/~lkravitz/Article%20folder/concurrent.html)
- Failure-type or very fatiguing lifting needs 24–48 h to recover neuromuscular performance (Morán-Navarro 2017; see Q3). **[Moderate]**, M-only. — [SpringerMedizin](https://www.springermedizin.de/time-course-of-recovery-following-resistance-training-leading-or/15095268)

### Inferences (placement rules) [Opinion, grounded in the findings above]
1. **Heavy squat or deadlift day:** no HIIT or tabata in the previous ~24 h, and no leg-heavy strength class in the previous ~48 h. Best placed after a rest day (Saturday) or after an upper-body lifting day.
2. **The day after HIIT, tabata or the strength class:** do an **upper-body** heavy day (bench, press, pull), or the lighter "second exposure" lower day.
3. **Same day:** lift first and do the class ≥3 h later if possible (Eddens, Schumann preprint). Never do HIIT or tabata *before* heavy legs on the same day.
4. **Count the leg-heavy strength class as a lower-body session.** In 2-class weeks, keep leg volume as written. In 3-class weeks, cut lower-body accessory sets by about a third, or use the full-body practice day (Q5) instead of a high-volume leg day.
5. **Day before a class:** a heavy lower day the day before HIIT is acceptable (lifting first protects the lift). She may feel heavier legs in class, which matters less for her goals.
6. **Saturday stays a full rest day.** With classes on 2–3 of Sun/Tue/Thu/Fri, Monday and Wednesday are her most reliable class-free days. Put **heavy lower on Monday** when Sunday has no class. If Sunday *is* a class day, use the first lifting day that isn't preceded by a class, or move heavy lower to **Sunday morning** (lift first, class ≥3 h later).

### Gaps
- I found no study specific to tabata, group HIIT classes or women.
- The exact recovery windows (24 h after HIIT, 48 h after a leg strength class) are extrapolated from small male studies and practitioner convention.
- Her strength class content is assumed to be leg-heavy; its real volume and intensity are unknown.

## 7. Bottom line: concrete recommended numbers

### Takeaway
**Answer to "should I go heavier and do fewer reps?":** go heavier **on the first lift of each session**, not across the whole workout. The offered plan already has the right shape: a heavy main lift, then 6–15-rep supersets. To shift it toward strength: make the heavy block a bit bigger (4–5 sets of 3–5), practise each main lift twice a week, and keep moderate-rep accessories for muscle, joints and athletic balance. Heavy lifting at 1–3 reps short of failure is safe and good for bone and tendons, and it gets her stronger than moderate reps alone. Swapping *everything* to heavy would add little strength, cost time and recovery, and not add muscle. **[Moderate–Strong]**

### Cited Findings
- Load: heavy (≥80% 1RM) maximises 1RM; hypertrophy is load-independent when effort is high — [Lopez 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33433148/); [Schoenfeld 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28834797/); [Carvalho 2022](https://www.pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35015560/); [Kubo 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32304514/)
- Dose: strength needs few sets, ≥80% 1RM, 2–3 sets per exercise, ≥2×/week; muscle needs ≥10 sets/muscle/week — [ACSM 2026 summary](https://repfitness.com/blogs/training/acsm-updated-resistance-training-guidelines); [Androulakis-Korakakis 2020](https://www.pubmed.gov/31797219/); [Remmert preprint](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/537); [Pelland 2025](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/398333408_The_Resistance_Training_Dose_Response_Meta-Regressions_Exploring_the_Effects_of_Weekly_Volume_and_Frequency_on_Muscle_Hypertrophy_and_Strength_Gains_The_Resistance_Training_Dose_ResponseJ_C_Pelland_et_al)
- Proximity to failure: strength unaffected by RIR; failure adds trivial hypertrophy and more fatigue — [Robinson 2024](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-024-02069-2); [Refalo 2024](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/02640414.2024.2321021); [Morán-Navarro 2017](https://www.springermedizin.de/time-course-of-recovery-following-resistance-training-leading-or/15095268)
- Safety and bone: [Keogh & Winwood 2017](https://research.bond.edu.au/en/publications/the-epidemiology-of-injuries-across-the-weight-training-sports/); [Mosti 2014](https://www.pubmed.gov/24736773/); [Bohm 2015](https://sportsmedicine-open.springeropen.com/track/pdf/10.1186/s40798-015-0009-9)
- Split vs full-body and class order: [Ramos-Campo 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38595233/); [Eddens 2018](https://researchportal.northumbria.ac.uk/en/publications/the-role-of-intra-session-exercise-sequence-on-the-interference-e/); [Schumann 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34757594/)

### Inferences (recommended prescription) [Opinion, built on the graded evidence above]

| Element | Recommendation |
|---|---|
| **Main lift (first exercise, heavy day)** | **4–5 working sets × 3–5 reps at ~80–87% 1RM**; **RIR 2–3 on squat and deadlift, RIR 1–2 on bench, overhead press, row and weighted pull-up**; rest 2.5–4 min. 2–4 ramp warm-up sets first (not counted). |
| **Main lift, second weekly exposure** | Same lift or a close variation, **2–3 sets × 6–8 at ~70–78% (RIR 2–3)**, early in another session. |
| **Secondary compound** (e.g., RDL, split squat, incline DB press, row) | **2–3 × 6–10 at RIR 1–2** |
| **Accessories (supersets)** | **2–3 × 8–15 at RIR 0–2**; isolation last set may go to failure; any 15–20+ rep or bodyweight set at **RIR 0–1** |
| **Hard sets per 60-min session** | **~14–18** (4–5 heavy + 10–13 accessory). Hard cap ~20. Per muscle per session ≤~10–11 fractional sets |
| **Hard sets per muscle per week** | **10–20 fractional sets** (direct = 1, synergist = 0.5); ≥10 floor |
| **Frequency per main lift** | **2×/week**: 1 heavy + 1 lighter or variation. ≥2 heavy-ish sets per exposure |
| **Progression** | When all sets hit the top of the rep range at the target RIR, add load: upper body +1–2.5 kg, lower body +2.5–5 kg (~2.5–5%). Otherwise add a rep. Easier week (≈ half the sets, RIR 3–4) every ~5–8 weeks or when sleep or soreness slip |
| **1RM testing** | Not needed. Estimate from rep-max sets (≈ 8 reps @ 80%, 6 @ 85%, 4 @ 90%; Nuzzo 2024) |
| **Fortnightly full-body day** | **Optional, yes if she likes it**: replaces one split day every other week (ideally in 3-class weeks). 3–4 main lifts × 2–3 sets × 3–5 at RIR 2–3, plus 1–2 short supersets (2 × 8–12). ~12–15 hard sets |
| **Class placement** | Heavy lower ≥24 h after HIIT or tabata and ≥48 h after the leg-heavy strength class. Day after a class = upper or light-lower day. Same day = lift first, class ≥3 h later. Leg strength class counts as lower-body volume (cut leg accessories by ~⅓ in 3-class weeks). Saturday full rest |

### Gaps
- None of these exact numbers has been tested as a whole programme in young women. They combine meta-analytic findings (mostly from men or mixed samples) with guideline and practitioner conventions.
- Pelland's exact strength-volume curve values, Lopez's and Schoenfeld 2017's exact strength effect sizes, and the full ACSM 2026 strength wording were not retrievable. Full texts at mdpi.com, jyx.jyu.fi and bretcontreras.com (and PubMed, PMC and Springer in earlier sessions) were blocked.
