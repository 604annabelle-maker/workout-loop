# Stacy Sims' Approach to Training Women, Graded Against the Evidence (and Other Women's-Physiology Experts)

Scope note for the report writer: The end user is a 22-year-old woman whose goal is lean muscle gain. She may cycle naturally or use hormonal contraception. Evidence labels used below: **[STRONG]** = multiple RCTs or meta-analyses agree; **[MODERATE]** = some controlled data, consistent direction; **[WEAK/MIXED]** = few or small studies, conflicting results or heavy methodological problems; **[EXPERT OPINION]** = a practitioner's claim without direct trial support.

Method caveat: In this session WebFetch could not reach frontiersin.org, pubmed/pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov or strongerbyscience.com (DNS blocked by the session's network settings). Every finding therefore comes from search-engine summaries of the listed pages, not from full text read directly. Numbers marked "(verify)" were not visible in a retrieved snippet. They are the figures as I recall them from the original paper, and they should be checked before they are encoded.

## 1. What exactly does Sims prescribe for training and nutrition? (concrete numbers)

### Takeaway
Sims' training model rests on "three pillars": heavy lifting, plyometrics/jump training and sprint interval training (SIT). Her nutrition model is "never train fasted," protein shortly after training (20–35 g), a high daily protein intake (~0.9–1 g/lb) and phase-specific fuelling: more carbohydrate, protein and sodium in the "high-hormone"/luteal phase. Much of her signature training content (lift heavy, jump, sprint; "less is more"; low-intensity cardio de-emphasized) is explicitly framed for peri- and post-menopausal women. The cycle-phase fuelling content in *ROAR* (2016) is aimed at younger, naturally cycling athletes, mostly endurance athletes.

### Cited Findings
**Training prescriptions**
- Sims' own site names "three pillars of training that are essential for power": heavy lifting, plyometrics and sprint intervals. She calls sprints (efforts of about ≤30 s) the best HIIT format for midlife women. The article is titled "How to Power Your Way Through Menopause." — [Sims, drstacysims.com](https://www.drstacysims.com/newsletters/articles/posts/How_to_Power_Your_Way_Through_Menopause)
- In podcast interviews she says "jump training, heavy resistance training, and sprint interval training, those are the three key things." — [Huberman Lab episode](https://www.hubermanlab.com/episode/dr-stacy-sims-female-specific-exercise-nutrition-for-health-performance-longevity); [ZOE podcast summary (Mar 2024), "Fitness routines for menopause"](https://www.shortform.com/podcast/episode/zoe-science-nutrition-2024-03-07-episode-summary-fitness-routines-for-menopause-with-dr-stacy-sims-exercise-for-every-stage)
- Resistance training numbers attributed to her differ between secondary sources. One version is ≥3 sessions/week of compound lifts heavy enough for only 6–8 reps before failure (1–2 RIR) — [Laura Fish summary](https://laurafishtherapy.com/7-health-tips-dr-stacy-sims/). Another, for women over 40, is lifting heavy with 2–3 RIR at the "strength-power end of the spectrum" — [ForWomensHealth Substack, "after 40"](https://forwomenshealth.substack.com/p/7-training-truths-from-dr-stacy-sims). A third is low reps of lifts such as deadlifts to fatigue with good form — [ZOE podcast summary](https://www.shortform.com/podcast/episode/zoe-science-nutrition-2024-03-07-episode-summary-fitness-routines-for-menopause-with-dr-stacy-sims-exercise-for-every-stage) **[EXPERT OPINION; secondary sources conflict]**
- Plyometrics dosing attributed to her is roughly 3 × 10 min/week of jump training, or "2–3 short doses." — [Unfiltered interview](https://unfilteredonline.com/dr-stacy-sims-why-all-women-should-lift-heavy-weights/); [Substack summary](https://forwomenshealth.substack.com/p/7-training-truths-from-dr-stacy-sims)
- SIT/HIIT dosing attributed to her is 1–2 sessions/week of 10–30 s efforts with 2–4 min recovery. Another version is one SIT *or* one HIIT session, "not both, not every day — twice a week maximum." — [Substack summary](https://forwomenshealth.substack.com/p/7-training-truths-from-dr-stacy-sims); [Laura Fish summary](https://laurafishtherapy.com/7-health-tips-dr-stacy-sims/)
- "Less time in the gym, not more" is a recurring Sims message for midlife women (she says moderate-intensity "junk miles" add stress without adaptation). — [AGEIST profile](https://www.ageist.com/profile/dr-stacy-sims-53-less-time-in-the-gym-not-more/); [Sims, "Mainstream Menopause Advice is Misleading Active Women"](https://www.drstacysims.com/newsletters/articles/posts/Mainstream_Menopause_Advice_Misleading_Active_Women)
- An independent UK GP-practice review of Sims' perimenopause advice ranks the evidence as follows: resistance training strongest, then plyometrics (postmenopausal bone data), then HIIT, with SIT "promising but smaller-scale evidence." — [Walton Surgery review](https://waltonsurgery.co.uk/health/stacy-sims-women-exercise-research-uk-perimenopause/)

**Nutrition prescriptions**
- **Fasted training:** Sims is quoted as saying women should not do "any kind of fasted training." Her stated rationale is that the fat-oxidation benefit of fasted exercise comes from male-subject studies. — [Dottir summary of Sims](https://empowerbydottir.com/blogs/empower-edit/to-fast-or-not-to-fast-here-s-what-dr-stacy-sims-has-to-say). Her newsletter claims that fasted exercise in active women increases hunger, decreases energy output and "can damage hormone balance over time." It cites an unnamed female-only study. — [Sims, "Why Fasting Doesn't Work for Active Women"](https://www.drstacysims.com/newsletters/articles/posts/fasting-for-active-women-risks)
- **Pre-workout fuel:** Secondary summaries give 10–15 g protein plus 30–40 g carbohydrate before training (one variant says 30 g carbohydrate, another 40 g for sessions >90 min). — [Running Week summary](https://www.therunningweek.com/post/dr-stacy-sims-says-runners-are-fueling-wrong-5-rules-that-contradict-standard-marathon-advice); [Hello Strength ROAR review](https://hellostrength.com/2020/02/26/what-i-learned-reading-dr-stacy-sims-book-roar/)
- **Post-workout protein window:** *ROAR* advises 20–25 g protein within 30 min of finishing. Her later guidance is reported as ~35 g within 45 min of a hard session for women of reproductive age. — [Goodreads ROAR quotes](https://www.goodreads.com/work/quotes/46061205); [Huberman AI summary flagging the "30 min" attribution](https://ai.hubermanlab.com/s/0WSWccjm)
- **Daily protein:** Secondary summaries put her target at about 0.9–1.0 g/lb/day (≈2.0–2.2 g/kg; e.g., 122–135 g for a 135-lb woman). One summary says 1–1.1 g/lb. The luteal phase adds roughly 20 g/day (one extra protein serving). — [100 Year Athlete ROAR notes](https://www.100yearathlete.com/blog/yl8zqj5nsywlgpx40mbhlbxz77ds2q); [Recall summary of Huberman episode](https://www.recall.it/summary/fitness/dr-stacy-sims-female-specific-exercise-and-nutrition-for-health-performance-and-longevity); [WHOOP podcast ep. 212](https://www.whoop.com/us/en/thelocker/episode-212-how-to-train-based-on-your-menstrual-cycle/) (secondary; verify against books)
- **Carbs during exercise by phase:** In the high-hormone (luteal) phase, ~0.45 g carbohydrate per lb body weight per hour (≈61 g/h for a 135-lb woman). In the low-hormone (follicular) phase, ~0.35 g/lb/h. Her justification is that progesterone is catabolic and that luteal hormones reduce carbohydrate availability. — [Goodreads ROAR quotes](https://www.goodreads.com/author/quotes/15287203.Stacy_T_Sims); [Debbie Potts ROAR summary](https://debbiepotts.net/roar-like-a-female-athlete/); [Everfit ROAR review](https://everfit.trainingtiltapp.com/Articles/everfit-book-review-roar-by-stacy-sims) (secondary)
- **Sodium/hydration:** She says progesterone raises sodium loss and advises salting food and paying attention to electrolytes in the luteal phase. Search found no luteal-specific gram target. The only gram figures are her endurance pre-load protocol (≈7.7 g sodium citrate + 4.5 g NaCl per litre), which derives from her own heat-exercise research. — [Hello Strength ROAR review](https://hellostrength.com/2020/02/26/what-i-learned-reading-dr-stacy-sims-book-roar/); [Running Week summary](https://www.therunningweek.com/post/dr-stacy-sims-says-runners-are-fueling-wrong-5-rules-that-contradict-standard-marathon-advice) (secondary)
- **Her peer-reviewed position stand:** Sims was an author of the 2023 ISSN position stand "Nutritional concerns of the female athlete" (Sims, Kerksick, Smith-Ryan et al., *J Int Soc Sports Nutr* 2023;20:2204066, published 24 May 2023). It recommends protein in the mid-to-upper range of sports guidelines (1.4–2.2 g/kg/day), with the upper end for the luteal phase and peri/post-menopause. It names caffeine, iron and creatine as the supplements with the most evidence in females and calls creatine "highly efficacious." — [ISSN PDF (T&F)](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/pdf/10.1080/15502783.2023.2204066); [Exercise & Sport Nutrition Lab summary](https://exerciseandsportnutritionlab.com/issn-position-stand-female-athletes/); [Nutrify Performance review](https://nutrifyperformance.com/2023/05/research-review-nutritional-concerns-of-the-female-athlete/)

### Inferences
- The "lift heavy / jump / sprint / skip moderate cardio" package is built for counteracting menopausal loss of muscle, bone and insulin sensitivity. Its core message (heavy compound lifting) transfers to a 22-year-old. The "minimize endurance work" and "sprint instead of HIIT" framing is less relevant to her hypertrophy goal and is not a hypertrophy prescription. Plyos and SIT are useful for power, bone and cardiometabolic health, but they are optional add-ons, not muscle builders.
- The rep ranges attributed to her (6–8 reps at 1–2 RIR, or low reps at 2–3 RIR) are strength/power-biased. For a hypertrophy-focused 22-year-old, that is one valid zone among several (see the sex-difference findings in section 4: no sex-specific rep range is required).
- Her daily protein target (~2.0–2.2 g/kg) sits at the top of the ISSN range she co-authored (1.4–2.2 g/kg). It is defensible as a ceiling for a muscle-gain program, and there is no evidence that a woman needs more than a man per kg.
- Program encoding suggestions: creatine monohydrate 3–5 g/day is consistent with the ISSN stand Sims co-authored. Total daily protein of ~1.6–2.2 g/kg is the robust target. A pre-workout snack is a low-cost default (see section 3 for why "never fasted" is overstated).

### Gaps
- The exact rep/set prescriptions in *Next Level* (2022) and *ROAR* (2016, rev. 2023) could not be read from the books. The secondary summaries conflict (6–8 reps at 1–2 RIR vs. 2–3 RIR), so verify against the books.
- No gram-specific luteal sodium target was found. The 30 min vs. 45 min post-exercise window and 20–25 g vs. 35 g dose differ between sources and editions.
- The ISSN position stand's carbohydrate recommendations were not visible in the retrieved summaries.

## 2. Menstrual-cycle-phase training: what do the systematic reviews say, and what is the honest bottom line?

### Takeaway
The best current evidence finds no meaningful effect of cycle phase on acute strength or on long-term hypertrophy and strength adaptations. A 2023 umbrella review, a 2025 *J Physiol* muscle-protein-synthesis study, a 2025/26 MSSE randomized trial and a 2025 NSCA review all point the same way. The only phase effect on performance is "trivial" (McNulty 2020, early follicular). The "follicular-phase-based training is superior" claim rests mainly on small, poorly controlled trials (Sung 2014 and similar). The defensible approach is consistent, progressive training regulated by RPE/RIR, plus optional symptom logging that adjusts a session when she actually feels bad, not according to the calendar.

### Cited Findings
- **McNulty et al. 2020, *Sports Medicine*** (systematic review and network meta-analysis, co-authored with Elliott-Sale): exercise performance "might be trivially reduced during the early follicular phase" compared with all other phases. 73 studies had enough data for the network meta-analysis (78 included overall; verify). — [RGU repository PDF](https://rgu-repository.worktribe.com/preview/940995/MCNULTY%202020%20The%20effects%20of%20menstrual%20(VOR%20_%20DATA).pdf); [Semantic Scholar record](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/The-Effects-of-Menstrual-Cycle-Phase-on-Exercise-in-McNulty-Elliott-Sale/94feed7015969fe958c05860ab3c3727f472d00f). I recall the pooled effect as ES ≈ −0.06 (95% CI −0.16 to 0.04), with most included studies rated low quality. This was not visible in snippets, so verify. **[MODERATE evidence that any effect is trivial; low-quality primary studies]**
- **Elliott-Sale et al. 2020, *Sports Medicine*** (oral contraceptive meta-analysis): OC pill use "might result in slightly inferior exercise performance" compared with natural cycles, and performance was consistent across the OC cycle (active pill vs. withdrawal days). — [RGU repository PDF](https://rgu-repository.worktribe.com/preview/941047/ELLIOTT-SALE%202020%20The%20effects%20of%20oral.pdf); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/342939191_The_Effects_of_Oral_Contraceptives_on_Exercise_Performance_in_Women_A_Systematic_Review_and_Meta-analysis). I recall the effect as ES ≈ −0.04 (trivial) and the advice as an individualized approach (verify). A published comment by Lei et al. challenged both 2020 papers, and the authors replied. — [Authors' reply, Sports Med](https://www.researchgate.net/profile/Kelly-Mcnulty/publication/347694013_Reply_to_Comment_on_The_Effects_of_Menstrual_Cycle_Phase_on_Exercise_Performance_in_Eumenorrheic_Women_A_Systematic_Review_and_Meta-Analysis_and_The_Effects_of_Oral_Contraceptives_on_Exercise_Performa/links/60338eeda6fdcc37a842bb76/Reply-to-Comment-on-The-Effects-of-Menstrual-Cycle-Phase-on-Exercise-Performance-in-Eumenorrheic-Women-A-Systematic-Review-and-Meta-Analysis-and-The-Effects-of-Oral-Contraceptives-on-Exercise-Performa.pdf)
- **Colenso-Semple, D'Souza, Elliott-Sale & Phillips 2023, *Frontiers in Sports and Active Living* 5:1054542** (umbrella review, McMaster): "Current evidence shows no influence of women's menstrual cycle phase on acute strength performance or adaptations to resistance exercise training." The authors say it is "premature to conclude that short-term fluctuations in reproductive hormones appreciably influence acute exercise performance or longer-term strength or hypertrophic adaptations." They specifically address the claim that follicular-phase-based training (higher frequency and volume in the follicular phase) is superior, and they note "poor and inconsistent methodological practices" in the literature. — [Frontiers full text](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fspor.2023.1054542/full); [PMC10076834](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10076834); [PubMed 37033884](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37033884/) **[MODERATE–STRONG for "no meaningful effect"]**
- **Sung et al. 2014, *SpringerPlus* 3:668** (often cited by Sims-style "follicular-loading" advice): follicular-phase-based training "was superior to luteal phase-based training" for isometric knee-extension strength and the diameter of rectus femoris, vastus intermedius and vastus lateralis. One summary table gives quadriceps diameter change of 0.57 ± 0.54 cm (follicular) vs. 0.39 ± 0.38 cm (luteal). The large SDs suggest imprecision. — [SpringerPlus article](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/2193-1801-3-668) **[WEAK: small sample; I recall ~20 women in a single-leg design (verify)]**
- **Sakamaki-Sunaga et al. 2016** (phase-dependent training frequency, 14 women): "no major differences among the different training protocols with regard to muscle hypertrophy and strength." — [PubMed 26554551](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26554551/)
- **Kissow et al. 2022, *Sports Medicine*** ("Effects of Follicular and Luteal Phase-Based Menstrual Cycle Resistance Training on Muscle Strength and Mass"): its conclusion, as surfaced, is that follicular-phase training "may be superior to luteal phase-based training in terms of enhancing muscle strength and mass." It is based on very few studies. Search tools disagreed on whether the paper is a systematic review or a narrower analysis. — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-022-01679-y); [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Effects-of-Follicular-and-Luteal-Phase-Based-Cycle-Kissow-Jacobsen/6f221598bada9aaecf265a66a3a95d8e2831a24d) **[WEAK; tentative pro-follicular signal]**
- **Randomized controlled trial (MSSE; DOI 10.1249/MSS.0000000000004031; online 2025/26):** "Menstrual Cycle Phase Does Not Influence Training-Induced Muscle Hypertrophy or Strength." It used a randomized unilateral design in 24 eumenorrheic women over three consecutive cycles. Emphasizing training in the follicular or the luteal phase gave no advantage, and "MC phase-based RET does not yield superior strength or hypertrophy gains compared to traditional consistent (fixed-volume) training" when volume is matched. **Training volume** was significantly associated with hypertrophy and strength. — [Ovid/MSSE](https://www.ovid.com/jnls/acsm-msse/fulltext/10.1249/mss.0000000000004031~menstrual-cycle-phase-does-not-influence-training-induced); [Semantic Scholar PDF](https://pdfs.semanticscholar.org/8e96/b3d004a5f606a0cab2fcb112db0228feea1e.pdf) **[MODERATE; the best-controlled direct test to date]**
- **Colenso-Semple et al. 2025, *J Physiol*:** found "a significant effect of exercise but no effect of cycle phase or interaction" on muscle protein synthesis or whole-body myofibrillar proteolysis after resistance exercise. — [J Physiol (Wiley)](https://physoc.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1113/JP287342); [ScienceDaily press release, Mar 2025](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2025/03/250303141152.htm); [Commentary: "Menstrual cycles and macrocycles: Science, not socials, is doing the heavy lifting" (PMC11870042)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11870042/)
- **NSCA *Strength & Conditioning Journal*, Dec 2025:** "evidence for periodizing strength and/or endurance training according to menstrual cycle phases to optimize female athlete performance is lacking." — [SCJ](https://journals.lww.com/nsca-scj/fulltext/2025/12000/evidence_for_periodizing_strength_and_or_endurance.4.aspx)
- **ACSM (Van Every & D'Souza):** resistance-training adaptations are driven by consistent training, sufficient effort and progressive overload, not by phase alignment. — [ACSM article](https://acsm.org/?p=27047)
- **Stronger By Science**, "Should females be training based on menstrual cycle phase?": the science is "quite weak," with little direct data on hypertrophy from cycle-synced vs. non-synced training. — [Stronger By Science](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/training-menstrual-cycle-phase/)
- **Dissenting/positive reviews:** a 2026 *Retos* systematic review says phase-based periodization "was shown to optimize outcomes" but that "further investigations are needed regarding muscle strength gains." — [Retos 2026](https://www.revistaretos.org/index.php/retos/article/download/119754/85480/527454). A 2024 meta-analysis in *Sports* (MDPI) also addresses maximal strength across phases — [Sports 2024;12(1):31](https://www.mdpi.com/2075-4663/12/1/31) (its conclusion was not retrieved).
- **Methodology:** phase verification is a core problem. A 2025 *Sports Medicine* paper is titled "Why We Must Stop Assuming and Estimating Menstrual Cycle Phases in Laboratory and Field-Based Sport Related Research." — [Sports Med 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-025-02189-3). The Colenso-Semple umbrella review likewise flags poor methodological practice — [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fspor.2023.1054542/full)
- **Perceived readiness:** studies do report phase differences in *perceived* readiness to train in female athletes. — [J Coaching & Sports Science](https://journal.foundae.com/index.php/jcss/article/view/882)
- **Hormonal contraception and fitness:** a 2023 meta-analysis on cardiorespiratory fitness across the cycle and hormonal contraceptive use exists. — [PubMed 37484400](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37484400/) (details not retrieved)

### Inferences
- **Honest bottom line:** for a 22-year-old who wants muscle, cycle phase is not a programming variable worth encoding. Volume, effort and progressive overload drive adaptation regardless of phase, and this holds whether she cycles naturally or uses an OC pill (performance across the OC cycle is stable).
- **Low-risk, defensible way to account for the cycle (symptom-based autoregulation):**
  1. Optional daily check-in: energy, sleep, cramps/pain, mood, GI symptoms, bleeding heaviness, each on 1–5.
  2. Keep the planned exercises, and let RPE/RIR targets set the load. On "bad symptom" days, allow −1 set per exercise, swap high-skill or heavy-axial lifts for machine or supported variants, or move the session ±1–2 days. Never force a deload because of the calendar.
  3. Do not prescribe more volume in the follicular phase or less in the luteal phase by default. A tentative signal exists (Sung 2014, Kissow 2022), but the 2025/26 RCT did not replicate it.
  4. Use cycle tracking mainly as a **health marker** (cycle length and regularity, missed periods) to flag LEA/REDs (see section 5).
- **Hormonal contraception:** no adjustment is needed. The OC meta-analysis effect is trivial and it does not change the hypertrophy plan.

### Gaps
- **Thompson et al. 2020** (*Sports Med*, "The effect of the menstrual cycle and oral contraceptives on acute responses and chronic adaptations to resistance training: a systematic review") was not retrieved. I recall that it found tentative support for higher follicular-phase training frequency and no clear impairment from OC use (verify).
- **Elliott-Sale et al. 2021** (*Sports Med*, "Methodological considerations for studies in sport and exercise science with women as participants: a working guide") was not retrieved. Its key standard, as I recall it, is to verify phase with hormone measurement and ovulation testing rather than counting days (verify).
- The exact pooled effect sizes and confidence intervals for McNulty 2020 and Elliott-Sale 2020 were not visible in retrieved snippets.
- The full author list of the MSSE RCT was not confirmed. The search tool could not confirm Colenso-Semple as an author; she is the author of the separate *J Physiol* MPS study.
- No direct trials of hormonal-IUD or implant users on hypertrophy were found.

## 3. Which of Sims' claims have drawn criticism, and what is the counter-evidence?

### Takeaway
The claims most at odds with the evidence are: (a) women should **never** train fasted; (b) protein is needed in a tight **30-minute** window; (c) training and fuelling should follow **cycle phase**; and (d) **luteal heat and hydration** effects big enough to change prescriptions. Direct trials in women show fasted and fed training give similar body-composition outcomes. Thermoregulatory differences between phases are small (≈0.2 °C at rest, ≈0.05 °C after exercise). Phase-based periodization has not held up in newer RCTs. Her structural critique is legitimate: women are under-represented in research. Her specific prescriptions are often extrapolations from small studies, sometimes her own.

### Cited Findings
**Fasted training**
- Schoenfeld, Aragon, Wilborn, Krieger & Sonmez 2014 (*JISSN* 11:54): 20 young women on a hypocaloric diet for 4 weeks did 1 h steady-state cardio 3×/week, fasted or fed. Both groups lost weight and fat mass, with no difference between groups. "Body composition changes associated with aerobic exercise in conjunction with a hypocaloric diet are similar regardless whether or not an individual is fasted prior to training." — [PMC4242477](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4242477/); [JISSN/Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12970-014-0054-7) **[MODERATE; short, small, cardio-only]**
- A 6-week HIIT study in overweight women found no fasted-vs-fed group differences. — [Holly Dunn review of fasted training in women](https://hollydunn.co.uk/articles/fasted-training-in-women)
- Meta-analysis (2017): minimal changes in body mass and composition after aerobic training in either the fasted or the fed state. — [Hackett & Hagstrom, J Funct Morphol Kinesiol (MDPI)](https://www.mdpi.com/2411-5142/2/4/43)
- Meta-analysis of acute bouts (2025): fasted exercise was associated with larger glucose and insulin increases and larger falls in free fatty acids, i.e., acute metabolic differences, not body-composition outcomes. — [ScienceDirect 2025](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2405457725000622)
- Sims' newsletter claims fasting harms active women's hormones, but it relies on an unnamed study. Search found no source linking fasted *exercise* to kisspeptin or cortisol harm in women. The stronger evidence concerns overall energy restriction (e.g., falls in T3 when menstruating women restrict energy), not meal timing. — [Sims newsletter](https://www.drstacysims.com/newsletters/articles/posts/fasting-for-active-women-risks); [Physiologically Speaking review](https://www.physiologicallyspeaking.com/p/what-science-says-about-fasted-exercise)
- Grade: "fasted training harms women" is **[WEAK/EXPERT OPINION]**. "Fasted vs. fed doesn't change fat loss" is **[MODERATE]**. The legitimate concern underneath Sims' claim is chronic low energy availability, which is real (see section 5).

**Protein timing ("within 30 minutes")**
- Sims' *ROAR* advice of 20–25 g within 30 min is widely repeated. Even a Huberman-site summary flags the "30 minutes" attribution as unverified, and her later guidance loosened to ~35 g within 45 min. — [Huberman AI summary](https://ai.hubermanlab.com/s/0WSWccjm); [Goodreads ROAR quotes](https://www.goodreads.com/work/quotes/46061205)
- The ISSN position stand she co-authored frames protein mainly as a **daily total** (1.4–2.2 g/kg), with the upper end for the luteal phase and menopause. — [ISSN 2023](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/pdf/10.1080/15502783.2023.2204066); [ESNL summary](https://exerciseandsportnutritionlab.com/issn-position-stand-female-athletes/)
- Grade: daily total protein ≈1.6–2.2 g/kg is **[STRONG]**, with protein spread across 3–5 meals. A strict 30-min post-workout window is **[WEAK]**. Evidence that women need a tighter window than men is absent; I found no female-specific trial supporting it.

**Luteal-phase heat and hydration**
- Giersch et al. 2020 (*J Sci Med Sport* 23(12):1134–1140; meta-analysis of 9 studies): core temperature was ~0.18 °C higher before exercise in the luteal vs. follicular phase, but only ~0.05 °C higher after exercise, and 4 studies found no difference. There was no phase difference in sweat rate, mean skin temperature or exercise heart rate. The evidence is "limited." — [ScienceDirect abstract](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S144024402030058X); [EvidenceStrong summary](https://evidencestrong.com/menstrual-cycle-exercising-and-thermoregulation-in-heat/)
- A 2024 *Psychoneuroendocrinology* study found that hydration did not differ between phases before or after exercise, and that plasma volume and haemoglobin were maintained pre- to post-exercise. — [ScienceDirect 2024](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0306456524002547)
- A 2023 *Sports Medicine* meta-analysis covers heat adaptation in females, and a 2021 paper documents female under-representation in exercise thermoregulation research. — [Heat adaptation for females](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-023-01831-2); [Female (under)representation](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40798-021-00334-6)
- Grade: a luteal resting core temperature rise of ~0.2–0.3 °C is **[MODERATE]**. That this rise needs special hydration or sodium protocols in ordinary gym training is **[WEAK]**, and it is irrelevant to an indoor hypertrophy program.

**Cycle-phase-based carbohydrate and protein manipulation**
- Phase-specific carbohydrate intakes (0.45 vs. 0.35 g/lb/h) and +20 g luteal protein come from *ROAR* and its secondary summaries. — [Debbie Potts ROAR summary](https://debbiepotts.net/roar-like-a-female-athlete/); [Recall summary](https://www.recall.it/summary/fitness/dr-stacy-sims-female-specific-exercise-and-nutrition-for-health-performance-and-longevity). No phase-specific MPS difference exists: Colenso-Semple 2025 *J Physiol* found no phase effect on MPS or proteolysis after resistance exercise. — [J Physiol](https://physoc.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1113/JP287342)
- Grade: phase-based macro shifts for a gym-goer are **[WEAK/EXPERT OPINION]**. They are low-harm if total intake is adequate.

**General "cycle syncing" and influencer framing**
- Kirsty Elliott-Sale called the cycle-syncing narrative "quite superficial" (Aug 2026 interview). — [Tyla](https://www.tyla.com/womens-health/menstrual-cycle-syncing-workouts-exercise-diet-171668-20260805)
- Asker Jeukendrup's mysportscience: "No evidence to adapt training to the phase of the menstrual cycle." — [mysportscience](https://www.mysportscience.com/post/no-evidence-to-adapt-training-to-the-phase-of-the-menstrual-cycle)
- *Triathlete*: "The case against training based on your menstrual cycle." — [Triathlete](https://www.triathlete.com/training/the-case-against-training-based-on-your-menstrual-cycle?scope=anon)
- A *J Physiol* commentary is titled "Science, not socials, is doing the heavy lifting." — [PMC11870042](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11870042/)

**Where Sims' critique is valid**
- Women are under-represented. A 2021 audit of six major sport and exercise science journals found only about 6% of studies were done on women alone. — [cited in Sims-related summary, Turnpaugh](https://turnpaughhwc.com/train-smarter-as-a-woman/) (secondary; the primary audit was not retrieved). Thermoregulation research shows the same under-representation. — [Sports Med Open 2021](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40798-021-00334-6)

### Inferences
- For the program: **never-fasted is a fine default but not a rule.** If the user prefers morning training without breakfast, a small pre-session snack (≈15–25 g protein ± some carbohydrate) costs little and may help session quality. There is no evidence of harm to muscle outcomes in women from occasionally training fasted when daily energy and protein are adequate.
- **Protein timing:** encode "protein-containing meal within ~1–2 h before and/or after training" and per-meal doses of ~0.3–0.4 g/kg. Daily total is the priority, and the 30-min window should not be a hard constraint.
- **Heat/hydration:** no luteal-specific rules are needed for indoor lifting. Generic hydration advice is enough.

### Gaps
- I did not locate a peer-reviewed paper that critiques Sims by name. Criticism appears in commentaries, podcasts and journalism aimed at "cycle syncing" generally. Researchers such as Colenso-Semple, Phillips and Elliott-Sale publicly disagree with phase-based prescriptions, but no formal rebuttal of *ROAR* or *Next Level* was found.
- Not re-retrieved here, but standard references the report writer may want: Schoenfeld, Aragon & Krieger 2013 (*JISSN*, protein timing meta-analysis: the timing effect largely disappears when total protein is matched); Morton et al. 2018 (*BJSM*, ~1.6 g/kg/day plateau for resistance-training gains, upper 95% CI ~2.2 g/kg). Verify before citing.
- The "kisspeptin" mechanism sometimes attributed to Sims' anti-fasting stance could not be sourced.

## 4. Positions of comparable experts: where they agree and disagree with Sims

### Takeaway
There is broad consensus with Sims that women should lift heavy, prioritize protein and avoid chronic under-fuelling. The academic physiologists (Elliott-Sale, Colenso-Semple/Phillips, the McNulty group, Nuckols/Stronger By Science, Jeukendrup) **disagree** with phase-based training and fuelling prescriptions. They treat the cycle as a health marker and favor individual symptom management. On sex differences, meta-analyses show women gain muscle at the same *relative* rate as men and show greater relative upper-body strength gains, which undercuts any idea that women need fundamentally different hypertrophy programming. Practitioners such as Lyon (protein) and Contreras (glutes, volume) align on high protein and possibly slightly higher volume tolerance in women. Those are expert opinions.

### Cited Findings
**Kirsty Elliott-Sale (Manchester Metropolitan University; menstrual cycle and contraception methodology)**
- She argues that the cycle is "a vital health marker, not a tool to measure sport performance." A woman's menstrual cycle "does not solely dictate how they train, compete, or fuel or determine if and when they get injured." — [MMU news](https://www.mmu.ac.uk/news-and-events/news/story/menstrual-cycle-vital-health-marker-not-tool-measure-sport-performance)
- Her advice is to track symptoms and address them rather than redesign training around the phase. — [Tyla interview, Aug 2026](https://www.tyla.com/womens-health/menstrual-cycle-syncing-workouts-exercise-diet-171668-20260805)
- She is co-author of the 2025 UEFA consensus (women's football): tracking supports well-being by identifying menstrual irregularities, managing symptoms and improving education and autonomy, but any link to performance or injury "remains unclear." — [BMJ Open SEM, Sep 2025 (DOAJ)](https://doaj.org/article/c864dfcf85c440d7879ccff06db042c9)
- She co-authored the McNulty 2020 and OC 2020 meta-analyses and the Colenso-Semple 2023 umbrella review (see section 2).

**Lauren Colenso-Semple and Stuart Phillips (McMaster)**
- Their 2023 umbrella review found no influence of phase on acute strength or adaptations, and called it "premature" to conclude otherwise. — [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fspor.2023.1054542/full)
- In 2025 they found no phase effect on MPS or proteolysis. Press coverage framed the result as debunking the belief that women build more muscle by timing workouts to their cycle. — [J Physiol](https://physoc.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1113/JP287342); [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2025/03/250303141152.htm)
- The disagreement with Sims is direct: they reject phase-based training and fuelling. They agree with her on lifting and protein.

**Greg Nuckols / Stronger By Science, and the sex-difference meta-analyses**
- Roberts, Nuckols & Krieger 2020 (*JSCR* 34(5):1448–1460; PROSPERO-registered): there was no significant sex difference in hypertrophy (12 outcomes from 10 studies) or lower-body strength. Women had a significantly **larger relative upper-body strength** gain. Most subjects were untrained. — [JSCR full text](https://journals.lww.com/nsca-jscr/fulltext/2020/05000/Sex_Differences_in_Resistance_Training__A.30.aspx); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/340216433_Sex_Differences_in_Resistance_Training_A_Systematic_Review_and_Meta-Analysis) **[MODERATE–STRONG]**
- Refalo, Nuckols et al. (*PeerJ*, Bayesian meta-analysis; 2025): males gain more **absolute** muscle size, but **relative** increases are similar between sexes. — [PeerJ 19042](https://peerj.com/articles/19042/); [SportRxiv preprint](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/400)
- A *Scientific Reports* 2021 paper reports that "Resistance training induces similar adaptations of upper and lower-body muscles between sexes." — [Nature Sci Rep](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-021-02867-y)
- In older adults: a 2020 *Sports Med* meta-analysis on sex differences in strength and size adaptations. — [Sports Med 2020](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-020-01388-4) (details not retrieved). Also a 2026 narrative review: [Sport Sci Health 2026](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11332-026-01650-8)
- Stronger By Science on phase-based training: "quite weak" science. — [SBS](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/training-menstrual-cycle-phase/)
- Nuckols discusses sex differences in strength training on a podcast. — [Health Scientist Podcast ep. 6](https://the-health-scientist-podcast.pinecast.co/episode/2de9facbc3d44f2c/ep-06-greg-nuckols-sex-differences-in-strength-training)

**Brad Schoenfeld**
- He is lead author of the only well-known fasted-vs-fed cardio RCT conducted entirely in young women. It found no difference, which contradicts Sims' "never fasted" stance on body-composition grounds. — [PMC4242477](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4242477/)

**Gabrielle Lyon ("muscle-centric medicine")**
- She recommends ~30–50 g of high-quality protein per meal, with the first and last meals of the day at 40–50 g. Her per-meal leucine target is ~2.5 g, and she has cited ~8–9 g leucine/day. — [Huberman Lab episode](https://www.hubermanlab.com/episode/dr-gabrielle-lyon-how-to-exercise-eat-for-optimal-health-longevity); [The Lyon Protocol PDF](https://drgabriellelyon.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/The-Lyon-Protocol-Dr.-Gabrielle-Lyon.pdf); [WHOOP podcast 224](https://www.whoop.com/us/en/thelocker/podcast-224-protein-and-skeletal-muscle-the-keys-to-longevity-with-dr-gabrielle-lyon/)
- Daily target: ~1 g protein per lb of **ideal** body weight, per an AI-generated Huberman summary that needs verification. Other sources quote 1.2–1.6 g/kg (up to 1.8 g/kg). She recommends resistance training 3–4×/week. — [Huberman AI summary](https://ai.hubermanlab.com/s/gA4GZrSf); [Doctor's Kitchen podcast](https://www.thedoctorskitchen.com/podcasts/317-how-to-support-muscle-health-for-longevity-and-improving-metabolism-dr-gabrielle-lyon) **[EXPERT OPINION]**
- She agrees with Sims on high protein and a "protein-first" approach. She differs in emphasizing per-meal dose and leucine threshold over timing relative to workouts, and she says little about cycle phase.

**Bret Contreras (glute training)**
- On Huberman Lab he says research on sex differences is mixed, but he believes women "recover better and can handle somewhat more volume." — [Huberman Lab, Bret Contreras](https://www.hubermanlab.com/episode/build-ideal-physique-bret-contreras)
- Glute volume and frequency: training glutes ~2×/week may be enough. — [Glute frequency article](https://bretcontreras.com/your-optimal-training-frequency-for-the-glutes-part-i-exercise-type/). He estimated the maximum recoverable glute volume at ~36–45 sets/week when split properly; women's optimum may be slightly above men's, "but not double." — [Perform podcast transcript](https://performpodcast.com/bret-contreras-optimize-training-glute-development/). In a 2016 post he suggested <30 weekly glute sets for people who find high volume too much. — [Optimal glute program](https://bretcontreras.com/how-to-design-an-optimal-glute-training-program/). He also has older observational posts on training women. — [Training Women](https://bretcontreras.com/training-women/); [12 Observations From Training Women](https://bretcontreras.com/12-observations-from-training-women/) **[EXPERT OPINION; numbers inconsistent across posts]**
- He agrees with Sims that women should lift heavy. He differs by favoring higher volume and a wider rep-range menu (hypertrophy-centric), whereas Sims emphasizes heavy, low-rep, "less is more" work for midlife women.

**Asker Jeukendrup (mysportscience)**
- "No evidence to adapt training to the phase of the menstrual cycle." — [mysportscience](https://www.mysportscience.com/post/no-evidence-to-adapt-training-to-the-phase-of-the-menstrual-cycle)
- He also questions whether low energy availability can be measured. — [mysportscience LEA](https://www.mysportscience.com/post/can-we-measure-low-energy-availability)

### Inferences
- **Consensus core for the generator:** progressive heavy resistance training, adequate total protein (~1.6–2.2 g/kg/day; per-meal ~0.3–0.4 g/kg or ~25–40 g), adequate energy, consistency. On these points Sims, Lyon, Contreras and the academic physiologists all agree.
- **Main disagreement:** whether cycle phase should change training or fuelling. The academic consensus as of late 2026 says no, apart from symptom management.
- **Sex-specific programming tweaks with some support:** women's greater relative upper-body strength gains (Roberts 2020), and possibly better recovery and volume tolerance (Contreras opinion; Nuckols has discussed women's fatigue resistance). A generator could set slightly higher default volume ceilings or shorter rest for women, but there is no need for different rep ranges or exercise selection.

### Gaps
- **Emma Ross (The Well HQ):** two searches returned nothing usable. My understanding, not verified this session, is that she co-founded The Well HQ, co-authored *The Female Body Bible* (2023), and advocates individual cycle and symptom tracking and education over rigid phase-syncing. This needs a source before it is reported.

- **Brad Schoenfeld:** no specific statement on women's programming was found beyond the fasted-cardio trial.

## 5. RED-S / low energy availability and iron: what should a program for a young woman watch for?

### Takeaway
The 2023 IOC REDs consensus treats low energy availability (LEA) as a continuum from adaptable to problematic. Health problems become likely at ≤30 kcal/kg FFM/day, but that figure is not used as a universal diagnostic cutoff. Diagnosis relies on the REDs CAT2 symptom and risk tool. For a young woman trying to build muscle, the practical red flags are missed or irregular periods, persistent fatigue, recurrent injuries or stress fractures, stalled progress despite good training, and low ferritin. Iron deficiency is common in active women. Ferritin below about 30–35 µg/L is the usual "stage 1" flag in sports medicine.

### Cited Findings
- **IOC REDs consensus 2023 (Mountjoy et al., *BJSM* 57:1073–1097):** LEA "exists on a continuum between adaptable and problematic LEA." LEA with health implications is at ≤30 kcal/kg FFM/day, and 30–45 kcal/kg FFM/day is described as reduced EA, for example during deliberate fat or body-mass loss. The 30 kcal/kg FFM/day threshold "is not applied universally." — [IOC/BJSM PDF](https://stillmed.olympics.com/media/Documents/Athletes/Medical-Scientific/Consensus-Statements/REDs/BJSM-IOC-consensus-statement-on-Relative-Energy-Deficiency-in-Sport-REDs.pdf); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/374201706_2023_International_Olympic_Committee's_IOC_consensus_statement_on_Relative_Energy_Deficiency_in_Sport_REDs)
- The 30 kcal/kg FFM/day cutoff came from short-term laboratory studies in sedentary women. — [NEDA-hosted copy of consensus](https://www.nationaleatingdisorders.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/RED.S-Consensus.-September-2023.-British-Journal-of-Medicine.pdf); [Gatorade female athlete chapter](https://performancepartner.gatorade.com/content/resources/pdfs/sports-nutrition-hydration-female-athletes-ch-9.pdf?v=2)
- **REDs CAT2** (Clinical Assessment Tool v2) supports detection and diagnosis by accumulated severity and risk stratification. Its indicators include low RMR (<30 kcal/kg FFM/day) or an RMR ratio <0.90. — [IOC REDs CAT2](https://stillmed.olympics.com/media/Documents/Athletes/Medical-Scientific/Consensus-Statements/REDs/IOC-REDs-CAT-V2.pdf)
- Energy availability cannot be measured accurately enough in the field to diagnose LEA, so diagnosis relies on symptoms and risk factors. — [mysportscience](https://www.mysportscience.com/post/can-we-measure-low-energy-availability). A 2024 *Sports Medicine* paper asks whether REDs is a valid "syndrome" at all. — [Sports Med 2024](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-024-02108-y)
- **Iron staging (sports medicine; Peeling 2007, summarized in Sim et al. 2019 *EJAP*):** Stage 1 iron depletion is ferritin <35 µg/L with Hb >115 g/L and transferrin saturation >16%. Stage 2 is ferritin <20 µg/L. Stage 3 (anaemia) is ferritin <12 µg/L. Other cutoffs in use are 35, 30, 20 and 12 µg/L. — [Imulab evidence summary](https://imulab.co/pages/ferritin-levels-for-athletes); [Dr Antti, iron deficiency in athletes](https://drantti.com/iron-deficiency/) (secondary; primary sources are Peeling 2007 and Sim 2019)
- **Swiss sports-medicine consensus:** ferritin <15 µg/L means empty stores and 15–30 µg/L means low stores. 30 µg/L is the suggested cutoff for athletes over 15. — [Dr Antti summary](https://drantti.com/what-is-ferritin/) (secondary)
- With a criterion of <35 ng/mL, one study found suboptimal iron status in **52% of female athletes** vs. 15% of male athletes. Review estimates for female cohorts run ~15–35%, with some up to 60%. — [Stanford FASTR biomarkers](https://fastr.stanford.edu/news/biomarkers/); [Imulab summary](https://imulab.co/pages/ferritin-levels-for-athletes) (secondary)
- **Screening frequency:** annually for asymptomatic athletes without a history of deficiency, and every 3–6 months for high-risk athletes (Sim et al. 2019). The IOC recommends regular iron screening in routine athlete health assessments. Ferritin is an acute-phase protein, so inflammation, including from a hard session in the preceding 24–48 h, can mask deficiency. — [Dr Antti](https://drantti.com/iron-deficiency/); [Triforward iron guide](https://triforward.com/guides/iron-ferritin-triathletes) (secondary)
- Population data suggest physiologically based ferritin thresholds for nonpregnant women may sit above the WHO cutoff of 15 µg/L. — [NHANES III analysis, PMC10472073](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10472073/)
- The ISSN female-athlete stand lists iron among the best-evidenced supplements for females, meaning repletion when deficient, not blanket use. — [ESNL ISSN summary](https://exerciseandsportnutritionlab.com/issn-position-stand-female-athletes/)

### Inferences
- **Generator guardrails for a 22-year-old pursuing lean mass:**
  1. Default to an energy **surplus or maintenance**, not a deficit. If she wants a "lean bulk" or recomposition, cap any deficit at a modest level and keep protein high.
  2. Run a periodic health check-in: cycle regularity (flag no period for ≥3 months, or cycles >35 days), unexplained fatigue, frequent illness, bone pain or stress-fracture history, disordered-eating red flags, dizziness, and performance stalling despite adherence. Any positive answer prompts "see a clinician; consider a REDs CAT2-style assessment."
  3. **Iron:** suggest a ferritin, Hb and transferrin-saturation check at baseline and roughly annually, especially with heavy periods, a vegetarian or vegan diet, or endurance add-ons. Treat <30–35 µg/L as a flag to discuss with a doctor, not a reason to self-supplement high doses.
  4. Hormonal contraception can mask amenorrhea, so for OC users cycle regularity is not a valid LEA signal. Rely on the other symptoms. (This is an inference from how OCs work: withdrawal bleeds are drug-induced. It was not sourced in this session.)
- These guardrails match Sims' legitimate core concern ("women are chronically under-fuelled") while avoiding her weaker mechanistic claims.

### Gaps
- The IOC consensus's specific iron and ferritin text and the full CAT2 indicator list were not retrieved in detail. The iron numbers above come largely from secondary summaries of Peeling 2007 and Sim et al. 2019 (*Eur J Appl Physiol*), which should be checked directly.
- Search did not establish the source for the claim that hormonal contraception masks LEA-related menstrual dysfunction. It is widely stated in REDs literature, but no citation was retrieved this session.
- No evidence was found that specifically targets REDs risk in recreational (non-athlete) young women doing hypertrophy training. Most data come from athletes.
