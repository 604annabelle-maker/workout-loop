# Jump (impact) training dose and frequency for bone in a 22-year-old woman: is once a week enough?

Evidence grades used throughout:
- **Strong**: several RCTs in premenopausal women that agree.
- **Moderate**: one RCT in the target population, or human and animal evidence that agree but is indirect.
- **Weak**: animal-only, cross-sectional, or a single small or low-quality trial.
- **Opinion**: guideline or consensus wording, or my own inference.

Population flags: [PRE] premenopausal women, [POST] postmenopausal women, [ANIMAL] rats, mice or birds, [CHILD] children.

Access caveat: pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov, pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov, link.springer.com, jstage.jst.go.jp and dspace.lboro.ac.uk were all blocked in this session, so I could not read any full text. The numbers below come from abstracts and database records that surfaced in search results. A few numbers could not be checked against the papers, and each of those is flagged.

## 1. Frequency: what weekly frequencies have worked, and is there any evidence for once a week?

### Takeaway
Every effective jump trial in premenopausal women gave impact loading at least 3 days a week, and most gave it daily or near-daily. The one trial that directly compared frequencies in premenopausal women (Bailey & Brooke-Wavell 2010) found that 50 hops 2 days/week did nothing for femoral-neck BMD, 4 days/week helped somewhat and 7 days/week helped most. I found no human trial of once-weekly jumping. The only once-weekly data come from growing rats, where 1 session/week (10 jumps) produced a non-significant gain and 3 or more sessions/week did not. So once a week has no positive evidence for bone, and the indirect evidence points against it.

### Cited Findings

**Human trials, premenopausal women: frequencies that worked**
- **Kato 2006** [PRE]: 36 female college students (mean age 20.7 ± 0.7 y) did 10 maximal vertical jumps/day, 3 times/week, for 6 months. Femoral-neck BMD rose from 0.984 to 1.010 (+2.6%, P < 0.01) with no change in controls. Lumbar spine BMD also rose. Total hip, trochanter and Ward's triangle showed no significant interaction. **Moderate** (one small RCT, but the closest match by age). — [PEDro record](https://search.pedro.org.au/search-results/record-detail/14578); [Peeref listing](https://peeref.com/works/68946688)
- **Bassey 1998** [PRE vs POST]: 50 vertical jumps, 6 days/week, mean jump height 8.5 cm. Premenopausal women gained 2.8% femoral BMD at 5 months (p < 0.001, n = 31) relative to controls. Postmenopausal women showed no difference from controls at 12 months (n = 123) or 18 months (n = 38), whatever their HRT status. A secondary claim of a >4% trochanter gain is unverified. **Moderate.** — [Bassey 1998 record](https://acnpsearch.tweb-dev.unibo.it/singlejournalindex/60643465); [Epistemonikos](https://www.epistemonikos.org/en/documents/b535eaf1de80ac13f7d691ae19c7f9bd0874f722)
- **Vainionpää 2005** [PRE, age 35–40]: 120 women did supervised, progressive high-impact exercise 3 times/week plus a home program for 12 months. Compared with controls:
  - femoral neck: +1.1% vs −0.4%
  - intertrochanter: +0.8% vs −0.2%
  - total femur also improved.
  
  Only 65% of the exercise group and 68% of controls completed the study. **Moderate.** — [PubMed 15221206](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15221206/)
- **Heinonen 1996, Lancet** [PRE]: an 18-month high-impact RCT. Femoral-neck BMD gains were greater in the trained group (snippet: 1.6% vs 0.6%, p = 0.006). The non-weight-bearing distal radius did not change. Vertical jump and predicted VO2 also improved. At the 3.5-year follow-up the bone benefits (Z, CSA) had shrunk and were no longer significant, while the performance gains held. I could not confirm the weekly frequency from the sources I retrieved. **Moderate.** — [PubMed 8918277](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8918277/); [UKK Institute follow-up](https://ukkinstituutti.fi/?p=8266)
- **Winters & Snow 2000, JBMR** [PRE]: jump plus resistance training, 3 times/week. After 6 months of detraining, BMD, strength and power fell significantly back toward baseline. **Moderate.** It shows that the benefit only lasts while the training continues. — [Detraining record](https://bohrium.dp.tech/paper/arxiv/11127215); [Scilit](https://scilit.com/publications/73c58583432c5a6a7afcb3844dde13fb)
- **Tucker 2015** [PRE, age 25–50]: 60 women did 10 or 20 jumps with 30 s rest between jumps, twice daily, for 16 weeks.
  - Jump-20 gained significantly more hip BMD than controls. Jump-10 was marginal.
  - Neither jump group changed significantly from baseline. The difference came from controls losing BMD.
  - Femoral neck and spine showed no group differences.
  - PEDro score 4/10 (no concealment, no blinding, no ITT).
  
  **Weak.** — [PubMed 24460005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24460005/); [PEDro](https://search.pedro.org.au/search-results/record-detail/45237)

**Direct frequency comparison**
- **Bailey & Brooke-Wavell 2010, Bone** [PRE; 61 completers, age 33.6 ± 11.1 y]: 50 multidirectional hops on one randomly chosen leg, assigned to 0, 2, 4 or 7 days/week, over about 6 months. Femoral-neck BMD change in the exercised leg:

  | Days/week | FN BMD change |
  |---|---|
  | 0 | −0.3% |
  | 2 | 0.0% |
  | 4 | 0.9% |
  | 7 | 1.8% (95% CI 0.8–2.8) |

  The group effect was significant (p = 0.015) and compliance was 86.7 ± 10.6%. The authors concluded that "brief, daily hopping exercises increased femoral neck BMD in premenopausal women but less frequent exercise was not effective." The order of the percentages above is my reading of a garbled snippet and should be checked against the full text. **Moderate.** This is the most relevant frequency trial for this question. — [PubMed 20004758](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20004758/); [Loughborough repository](https://dspace.lboro.ac.uk/2134/15541)

**Frequency-threshold research in older women** [POST]
- **Kemmler, EFOPS** (12-year cohort, osteopenic postmenopausal women): about 1.5 to under 2 sessions/week was compared with 2 or more. The authors concluded that "an overall exercise frequency of at least 2 sessions/week may be crucial, even if exercise is applied with high intensity/impact." **Moderate** for older women; indirect for a 22-year-old. — [FAU CRIS: dose-response of exercise frequency](https://cris.fau.de/publications/117839524); [Zendy: Scand J Med Sci Sports 2013](https://zendy.io/title/10.1111/sms.12024)
- **Kemmler, von Stengel & Kohl 2017 review**: minimum effective frequency for lumbar spine and femoral neck BMD of about 2.1–2.5 sessions/week. — [FAU CRIS](https://cris.fau.de/publications/205197211)
- **Conflicting result**: a 2020 meta-analysis in postmenopausal women found larger lumbar-spine BMD changes with fewer than 2 sessions/week than with 2 or more. The authors attributed this to interacting exercise parameters. — [Springer Medicine summary](https://www.springermedicine.com/exercise-frequency-and-fracture-risk-in-older-adults-how-often-i/21943906)

**The only once-weekly data: animal study**
- **Umemura, Nagasawa & Honda 2008** [ANIMAL, growing rats aged 11 weeks]: 10 jumps per session for 8 weeks, at 0, 1, 3, 5 or 7 sessions/week. Gain in fat-free dry tibia weight versus sedentary rats:

  | Sessions/week | Tibia weight gain |
  |---|---|
  | 1 | +7.5% (not significant) |
  | 3 | +12.6% (P < 0.01) |
  | 5 | +12.0% (P < 0.01) |
  | 7 | +19.8% (P < 0.001) |

  Cortical area was larger at 7 sessions/week than at 1. The authors concluded "it is not always necessary to do high-impact exercise every day, although exercising every day does have the greatest effect." These figures come from abstract snippets. **Weak** (animal). — [Springer abstract](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00774-007-0848-7); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/23225227_High_impact_exercise_frequency_per_week_or_day_for_osteogenic_response_in_rats)

**Mechanostat and recovery research** [ANIMAL]
- **Umemura 2016 review**: bone's mechanosensors become less responsive after many repetitions in a session, so returns diminish. Sensitivity takes "several hours or several days" to come back after the last session. **Weak.** — [DOAJ: J Phys Fitness Sports Med 2016](https://doaj.org/article/6bd05782895243588b231b363d385967)
- **Robling, Hinant, Burr & Turner 2002, JBMR** [rat ulna, 16 weeks]: four bouts of 90 cycles with 3 h of recovery between them beat one bout of 360 cycles for bone structure and strength. Turner & Robling report that loaded ulnae were 64% stronger than controls, while aBMD rose only modestly. **Weak.** — [Gwern PDF](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/biology/2002-robling.pdf); [summary](https://fasciaguide.com/research/effects-of-biomechanical-stress-on-bones-in-animals)
- **Turner & Robling 2003, Exerc Sport Sci Rev**: bone "tunes out" after a couple of dozen loading cycles, so there is limited benefit beyond that per day. Short sessions separated by 4–8 h rest are "a more effective osteogenic stimulus than a single sustained session." **Weak to opinion** (an extrapolation from animal work). — [Core.ac.uk PDF](https://core.ac.uk/download/215746207.pdf); [Robling search cluster](https://springerlink.bibliotecabuap.elogim.com/content/pdf/10.1007/BF03026318.pdf)
- **Conflicting animal result**: one group saw no extra bone gain from an interrupted resistance protocol (3–4 h and 10–12 h recovery) in young growing rats. — reported in the [Turner & Robling cluster](https://core.ac.uk/download/215746207.pdf)

### Inferences
- **The plain answer on once a week:** no trial shows that once-weekly jumping builds bone in young women. The closest human evidence (Bailey & Brooke-Wavell: 2 days/week gave 0.0%) and the only once-weekly animal data (W1 not significant) both point to "not enough." **Moderate** confidence that once a week is below the effective dose for BMD.
- **Why frequency matters more than total volume:** the dose cannot be "saved up" for one long session. Bone stops responding after roughly 20–50 loading cycles in a bout (animal), and sensitivity recovers within hours to days. One 100-jump session per week is therefore probably not equivalent to 20 jumps × 5 days. **Weak to moderate.**
- **The effective frequency band:** in premenopausal women it is about 3 days/week (Kato, Vainionpää, Winters & Snow) up to daily (Bassey 6/week, Bailey 7/week, Tucker twice daily). The best-evidenced minimum is 3 days/week with about 10 maximal jumps (Kato).
- **Detraining:** any benefit fades once the loading stops (Winters & Snow; the Heinonen follow-up). Impact work has to be a permanent habit, not a 6-month block.

### Gaps
- I found no human RCT of once-weekly impact loading in any population.
- I could not open the Bailey & Brooke-Wavell full text to confirm the group-to-percentage order, the exact duration or the ground-reaction forces of the hops.
- The results of the twice-daily versus once-daily experiment (D1 vs D2) in Umemura 2008 were not in the snippets.
- I could not confirm the weekly frequency in Heinonen 1996 or the exact BMD effect sizes in Winters & Snow 2000.

## 2. Load characteristics that matter (magnitude, rate, direction, reps, rest)

### Takeaway
Hip BMD gains in premenopausal women track the number of high-acceleration impacts (above about 4 g), and the rate of loading matters as well as the peak. Multidirectional ("odd-impact") and high-impact loading are linked to the strongest femoral necks. Resistance training alone is not. Short rests between individual impacts (about 10–30 s) increase bone's response in animal studies and were used in human protocols.

### Cited Findings
- **Vainionpää 2006, Osteoporos Int** [PRE, n = 64 with accelerometers]: activity producing accelerations above 3.9 g correlated positively with change in hip BMD. The threshold for improving BMD was "less than 100 accelerations per day at levels exceeding 3.9 g." The response was dose- and intensity-dependent. A related report from the same trial put the requirement at about 60 impacts/day, comparable to the same number of daily jumps (it is unclear which paper reports this). **Moderate.** — [Springer Medicine](https://www.springermedicine.com/intensity-of-exercise-is-associated-with-bone-density-change-in-/20631404); [Springer DOI](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s00198-005-0005-x); [BMC Musculoskelet Disord 2009 (DOAJ)](https://doaj.org/article/0ef030b4a8244c998b9fe43e39bb9e85)
- **Heikkinen 2007, J Biomech** [PRE, same trial]: activity with acceleration slopes of at least 1000 m/s³ (and related signal-energy measures) was associated with hip BMD change. Rate of loading matters, not just the peak g. **Moderate to weak.** — [Acceleration slope paper](https://ersearch2.cvtisr.sk/vufind/SummonRecord/FETCH-LOGICAL-c601t-8ab9a504f86f363d8400d50ce19cb4dd522b347da13a2dfcde4d5a95097c6b093?print=1)
- **Nikander and colleagues** [PRE, adult female athletes, n = 255, cross-sectional]:
  - High-impact sports (volleyball, hurdling) and odd-impact sports (squash, soccer, speed skating, step aerobics) had higher femoral-neck aBMD, cross-sectional area and section modulus Z than non-athletes.
  - Weightlifting did not clearly raise Z.
  - The authors concluded that "loadings which arise from high impacts or impacts from atypical loading directions seem particularly effective."
  - In the MRI sub-study, these athletes had about 20% thicker anterior and superolateral femoral-neck cortex.
  - Finite-element fracture loads were 11–26% higher than in controls.
  
  **Moderate to weak** (cross-sectional, so self-selection is possible). — [Femoral neck structure, JBMR](https://bvbvf1.bib-bvb.de/vufind/PrimoRecord/cdi_proquest_miscellaneous_67485778); [Nikander thesis](https://jyx.jyu.fi/jyx/Record/jyx_123456789_20275); [Abe 2018 FE study](https://jyx.jyu.fi/handle/123456789/58878?show=full)
- **Rest between impacts** [ANIMAL]: in birds and mice, inserting a 10 s unloaded rest between each of 100 low-magnitude cycles turned a non-osteogenic stimulus into an osteogenic one (Srinivasan 2002, JBMR). It also triggered bone formation in aged mice (2003) and amplified responses near the threshold (2007). **Weak.** — [Srinivasan 2002](https://data.zhenzhi.com/details/1/898692233); [Aged-skeleton follow-up](https://www.bohrium.com/paper-details/enabling-bone-formation-in-the-aged-skeleton-via-rest-inserted-mechanical-loading/812374172855435265-8180); [J Appl Physiol 2007](https://next.sciencegate.app/document/10.1152/japplphysiol.00507.2006)
- **Human protocols with rest between jumps**: Tucker 2015 used 30 s between single jumps [PRE]. **Weak.** — [PubMed 24460005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24460005/)
- **Reps per session**: effective human protocols ranged from 10 maximal jumps (Kato) to 50 jumps or hops (Bassey, Bailey & Brooke-Wavell). In rats, 10 jumps/day was enough to trigger an osteogenic response (Nagasawa 2008). **Moderate** (human) plus **weak** (animal). — [Kato PEDro](https://search.pedro.org.au/search-results/record-detail/14578); [Nagasawa 2008](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s00774-007-0812-6)
- **Low jumps work in young women but not older women**: Bassey's 8.5 cm jumps helped premenopausal women but not postmenopausal women. Low-moderate jumps may be enough for a young skeleton, but a higher stimulus is likely needed later in life. **Moderate.** — [Bassey 1998](https://acnpsearch.tweb-dev.unibo.it/singlejournalindex/60643465)

### Inferences
- **Prioritise:** (1) a high peak (maximal or near-maximal effort jumps, landings that clearly exceed brisk-walking loads); (2) a fast rate of loading (stiff, crisp ground contacts rather than soft absorbing landings, within safe technique); (3) varied directions (lateral, diagonal, rotational hops); (4) a short rest between single efforts. **Weak to moderate.**
- **Ceiling on reps:** above about 50 impacts per bout the animal data predict diminishing returns. Spreading impacts across days, or across two bouts in a day, is better than adding reps to one bout. **Weak.**

### Gaps
- I did not find a verified source giving ground-reaction-force multiples of body weight for specific exercises (countermovement jump, drop jump, hop, lateral bound, box jump-down vs step-down). The ">3–4 × BW" rule of thumb in the brief could not be sourced in this session.
- I could not verify the claimed 4 g / >4 g threshold wording (the abstract says 3.9 g).
- I could not retrieve the effect sizes from the Martyn-St James & Carroll 2010 meta-analysis of impact modalities in premenopausal women; only its existence is confirmed ([PubMed 20013013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20013013/)).

## 3. Minimum effective dose (and "jump snacks")

### Takeaway
The smallest protocol that has worked in women of about her age is 10 maximal vertical jumps, 3 days/week, for 6 months (Kato 2006, +2.6% femoral neck). Accelerometry suggests that fewer than 100, and perhaps about 60, impacts per day above about 4 g is enough. Guidelines converge on "brief bursts of about 50 impacts on most days." A 1–2 minute home bout 3–7 days/week is better supported than a single weekly gym session.

### Cited Findings
- **Smallest effective human protocol**: 10 maximal vertical jumps/day, 3 days/week, 6 months, in women aged about 20.7 y. Femoral neck +2.6%. **Moderate.** — [Kato 2006, PEDro](https://search.pedro.org.au/search-results/record-detail/14578)
- **Twice-daily small bouts**: 20 jumps twice daily (30 s rest between jumps) beat controls at the hip, while 10 jumps twice daily was only marginal. **Weak.** — [Tucker 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24460005/)
- **Accelerometer threshold**: fewer than 100 accelerations per day above 3.9 g; a related report says about 60 per day, equivalent to the same number of jumps. **Moderate.** — [Vainionpää 2006](https://www.springermedicine.com/intensity-of-exercise-is-associated-with-bone-density-change-in-/20631404); [DOAJ 2009](https://doaj.org/article/0ef030b4a8244c998b9fe43e39bb9e85)
- **Frequency floor for 50 hops**: 2 days/week = 0.0%, 4 days/week ≈ 0.9%, 7 days/week ≈ 1.8%. **Moderate.** — [Bailey & Brooke-Wavell 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20004758/)
- **ESSA 2017 position statement** (Beck, Daly, Fiatarone Singh & Taaffe, J Sci Med Sport): for healthy people preventing osteoporosis, "lifelong exercise including regular, brief, weight-bearing, high-impact exercise and high intensity PRT is recommended." A secondary clinic summary gives about 50 impacts per session, 4–7 days/week, which I could not confirm in the primary paper. **Opinion** (guideline). — [ECU repository](https://ro.ecu.edu.au/ecuworkspost2013/2797); [Wild Physio summary (secondary)](https://wildphysiofitness.au/blogs/physiotherapy/exercise-for-bone-health)
- **UK "Strong, Steady and Straight"** (2018 quick guide; 2022 consensus endorsed by the Royal Osteoporosis Society): weight-bearing or impact exercise on most days of the week, building up to 50 moderate impacts (low-level jumping, jogging, dancing, hopping), plus muscle strengthening 2–3 days/week. **Opinion** (guideline, aimed mainly at people with or at risk of osteoporosis). — [SfE quick guide PDF](https://www.endocrinology.org/media/3590/final-quick-guide_strong-steady-straight_dec18.pdf); [EurekAlert 2022](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/952512); [Endocrinologist feature](https://www.endocrinology.org/endocrinologist/135-spring20/features/strong-steady-and-straight-exercise-recommendations-for-osteoporosis-and-bone-health/)
- **ACSM 2004 position stand** (Kohrt et al.): in adulthood "the primary goal of physical activity should be to maintain bone mass," and whether adults can increase BMD with training "remains equivocal." I could not retrieve its specific frequency and jump-count prescription. **Opinion.** — [ACSM 2004 record](https://bvbvf1.bib-bvb.de/vufind/PrimoRecord/cdi_proquest_miscellaneous_67021260); [Univr PDF](https://docs.univr.it/documenti/OccorrenzaIns/matdid/matdid600016.pdf)
- **Healthy Bones Australia**: its exercise guide is based on the ESSA guidelines and frames the adult goal as maintaining bone and muscle strength. I could not confirm a specific jump count on its site. — [Healthy Bones Australia](https://healthybonesaustralia.org.au/?p=429)

### Inferences
- **Practical minimum effective dose:** about 10–20 maximal-effort jumps, at least 3 days/week (Kato-level), indefinitely. The better-supported target is 20–50 impacts per day on most days (Bassey, Bailey, ESSA and UK guidance). **Moderate.**
- **"Jump snacks"** (10–20 jumps at home, taking under 2 minutes, on non-gym days, or a second bout 4–8 h after the first) are directly modelled on Kato and Tucker. They are better supported than one larger weekly gym session. **Moderate** that 3 or more short bouts/week beat 1 bout/week; **weak** on the exact size of the advantage.
- **Expected effect size:** realistic gains are about 1–3% femoral-neck BMD over 5–12 months in young women. This is modest but meaningful while she is still accruing peak bone mass. Any gain is lost if she stops.

### Gaps
- I found no trial that compared "one gym session" with "short daily home bouts" head to head in young women.
- ESSA's exact impact-dosing numbers come from a secondary source.

## 4. Practical protocol for a gym-going young woman

### Takeaway
Do 10–30 maximal-effort, well-landed jumps with short rests, as a brief block done fresh (after the warm-up, before lifting). Mix vertical and multidirectional or unilateral hops. Repeat this at least 3 days/week; gym days count. Progress from bilateral to unilateral and from vertical to lateral. Most of the specific exercise choices below are opinion built on the loading principles above, because the trials used simple jumps or hops.

### Cited Findings
- **Exercises used in successful trials** [PRE]: maximal vertical countermovement jumps (Kato), low vertical jumps of about 8.5 cm (Bassey), multidirectional single-leg hops (Bailey & Brooke-Wavell), progressive high-impact classes (Vainionpää; Heinonen). — [Kato](https://search.pedro.org.au/search-results/record-detail/14578); [Bassey](https://acnpsearch.tweb-dev.unibo.it/singlejournalindex/60643465); [Bailey](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20004758/); [Vainionpää 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15221206/)
- **Combining jumps with resistance training**: done together 3 times/week, they produced musculoskeletal benefits in premenopausal women that reversed with detraining (Winters & Snow). ESSA and UK guidance both pair impact with progressive resistance training. **Moderate / opinion.** — [Winters & Snow](https://bohrium.dp.tech/paper/arxiv/11127215); [ESSA](https://ro.ecu.edu.au/ecuworkspost2013/2797); [EurekAlert 2022](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/952512)
- **Odd-impact, multidirectional loading** is linked to more robust femoral-neck geometry (Narra 2013; Nikander). **Weak to moderate.** — [Nikander cluster](https://jyx.jyu.fi/jyx/Record/jyx_123456789_20275); [Narra 2013 (Clin Physiol Funct Imaging)](https://zendy.io/title/10.1111/cpf.12022)
- **Safety**: ESSA notes that impact work may need modifying with osteoarthritis or frailty. The UK consensus found "little evidence that physical activity is associated with significant harm" and judged that benefits generally outweigh risks. — [ESSA](https://ro.ecu.edu.au/ecuworkspost2013/2797); [Nursing in Practice / UK consensus](https://www.nursinginpractice.com/clinical/musculoskeletal/encourage-osteoporosis-patients-to-exercise-regularly-say-experts/)

### Inferences (opinion unless graded otherwise)
- **Exercise menu, ranked for bone stimulus per rep** (my inference from the peak, rate and direction principles):
  1. Maximal countermovement jumps landing on the floor. This matches the Kato protocol.
  2. Single-leg hops: forward, back, lateral and diagonal (Bailey-style multidirectional).
  3. Lateral bounds and skater bounds (odd-impact).
  4. Low drop landings or drop jumps from 20–40 cm, once technique is solid.
  5. Tuck jumps and repeated pogo jumps (fast, stiff contacts).
- **Box jumps:** jumping onto a box reduces landing impact. If bone is the goal, the impact comes from the landing, so jumping down (a controlled drop landing) gives the load, while stepping down removes it. Step down when fatigued or tired of box work, or to protect volume on heavy leg days. Jump down deliberately when you want the bone stimulus. **Opinion**; no GRF source was found in this session.
- **Rest:** 10–20 s between single maximal jumps or short clusters of 3–5, and about 60 s between sets. Rest makes each rep higher quality and may help bone sensitivity (animal rest-insertion data; Tucker's 30 s). **Weak.**
- **Landing technique:** land on the forefoot, then let the heel touch, with knees tracking over the toes (no inward collapse) and hips back. The landing should be quiet but not mushy. Stop a set when height drops noticeably or landings get sloppy. **Opinion.**
- **Warm-up:** 3–5 minutes of easy movement, then 2–3 ramp-up jumps at 50–80% effort. **Opinion.**
- **Placement:** after the warm-up and before lifting, while fresh. Jump quality and peak force are better when not fatigued, and impact before squatting does not appear to hurt the lift (no source). On heavy lower-body days, keep the jump block at 10–20 contacts. On upper-body or rest days, a 20–40 contact block or a home "snack" is fine. **Opinion.**
- **Progression over about 8–12 weeks** (**opinion**, loosely modelled on Vainionpää's progressive approach):

  | Weeks | Work |
  |---|---|
  | 1–2 | 10 bilateral countermovement jumps |
  | 3–4 | 15–20 contacts, adding lateral hops |
  | 5–8 | 20–30 contacts, including single-leg hops and low drop landings |
  | 9+ | 30–50 contacts, including multidirectional bounds and drop jumps |

  Increase the volume by no more than about 5–10 contacts per week.

### Gaps
- I found no sourced trial data on box jump-down vs step-down forces, on the interaction of jumps with heavy squats, or on injury rates for jump training in young women.
- Landing technique guidance here is coaching opinion, not from bone trials.

## 5. If she only wants to jump once a week: is it worth doing, and how should she use that session?

### Takeaway
For bone, a single weekly jump session is probably below the effective dose. It has no supporting trial: 2 days/week was ineffective in premenopausal women and 1 day/week was non-significant in rats. Her 2–4 lifting sessions still load bone, but a weekly jump session is still worth doing for power, landing skill and athleticism. Heinonen's high-impact trial improved vertical jump and those gains outlasted the bone gains. The best use of that one session is a short, high-quality, multidirectional block done fresh. Better still, add 10 jumps at the start of every lifting session, which turns "once a week" into 3–4 exposures at almost no cost in time.

### Cited Findings
- **Human evidence**: 50 hops 2 days/week gave 0.0% femoral-neck change in premenopausal women. **Moderate.** — [Bailey & Brooke-Wavell 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20004758/)
- **Animal evidence**: 10 jumps once a week gave +7.5% tibial mass, which was not significant. Three or more sessions per week were significant. **Weak.** — [Umemura 2008](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00774-007-0848-7)
- **Older-adult threshold** [POST]: about 2 sessions/week or more is needed for BMD effects. **Moderate** (indirect). — [Kemmler, FAU CRIS](https://cris.fau.de/publications/117839524)
- **Performance benefits**: high-impact training improved vertical jump in premenopausal women (Heinonen 1996). The performance gains lasted to 3.5 years, while the bone gains became non-significant. **Moderate.** — [PubMed 8918277](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8918277/); [UKK follow-up](https://ukkinstituutti.fi/?p=8266)
- **Diminishing returns within a session** [ANIMAL]: the bone response saturates after about a couple of dozen cycles, so a longer weekly session cannot make up for low frequency. **Weak.** — [Turner & Robling review](https://core.ac.uk/download/215746207.pdf); [Umemura 2016](https://doaj.org/article/6bd05782895243588b231b363d385967)

### Inferences
- **Yes, keep the weekly session**, but describe it honestly as a power, landing-skill and athleticism session with an uncertain bone benefit. **Opinion.**
- **Best use of one session**: 20–40 contacts of high quality across 3–4 jump types, with the multidirectional and single-leg work done first while fresh. Do it before that day's lifting. Adding reps beyond about 40–50 is unlikely to help bone. **Opinion / weak.**
- **Cheapest upgrade:** a 10-jump "primer" (Kato dose) at the start of each of her 2–4 lifting sessions. That alone reaches the 3 days/week Kato frequency, the best-evidenced minimum. **Moderate** (directly matches an effective RCT protocol).

### Gaps
- Nobody has tested whether heavy resistance training 2–4 times a week plus one jump session equals 3 or more jump sessions per week for bone in young women.

## 6. Encodable protocol (synthesis)

### Takeaway
The minimum effective jump dose is about 10 maximal jumps, 3 or more days/week, done permanently. The better dose is 20–50 multidirectional impacts on most days, split into short bouts with short rests. Once a week is a skill or power session, not a bone prescription.

### Cited Findings
- 10 jumps, 3 days/week, 6 months: femoral neck +2.6% in women about 20 y old. **Moderate.** — [Kato 2006](https://search.pedro.org.au/search-results/record-detail/14578)
- 50 hops: 2 days/week did nothing; 7 days/week gave about +1.8%. **Moderate.** — [Bailey & Brooke-Wavell 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20004758/)
- Fewer than 100 (about 60) impacts per day above 3.9 g is enough. **Moderate.** — [Vainionpää 2006](https://www.springermedicine.com/intensity-of-exercise-is-associated-with-bone-density-change-in-/20631404)
- Guideline target: about 50 impacts on most days, plus resistance training 2–3 times a week. **Opinion.** — [UK quick guide](https://www.endocrinology.org/media/3590/final-quick-guide_strong-steady-straight_dec18.pdf); [ESSA](https://ro.ecu.edu.au/ecuworkspost2013/2797)

### Inferences: protocol (opinion built on the graded evidence above)

```
GOAL_TIER:
  once_weekly    = skill/power/landing; bone benefit UNPROVEN (evidence suggests insufficient)
  minimum_bone   = >=3 exposures/week (Kato dose)       [moderate]
  better_bone    = 5-7 exposures/week, 20-50 impacts/day [moderate]

PER EXPOSURE (bone):
  contacts:      10-20 (minimum); 20-50 (better); hard cap ~50 per bout
  effort:        maximal or near-maximal; crisp, fast ground contact
  structure:     singles or clusters of 3-5; rest 10-20 s between singles/clusters; ~60 s between sets
  sets example:  2-4 sets x 5 reps (= 10-20 contacts) or 5 x 5-10 for 25-50
  mix:           >=1 vertical (CMJ) + >=1 multidirectional/unilateral (lateral or diagonal hops, skater bounds)
  split option:  if >30 contacts/day, split into 2 bouts >=4-8 h apart (e.g., AM home + PM gym)

FREQUENCY OPTIONS:
  A (once a week only):   1 gym session, 20-40 contacts; label = power/skill, not bone
  B (minimum bone):       10 CMJ primer at start of each of 2-4 lifting sessions + 1 home snack (10-20 jumps) on a non-gym day -> >=3/week
  C (better bone):        B + home snacks on most remaining days (5-7 exposures/week, 20-50 contacts/day)

PLACEMENT RULES:
  - after 3-5 min warm-up + 2-3 ramp jumps; BEFORE lifting
  - heavy lower-body day: cap 10-20 contacts, bilateral CMJ only
  - upper-body/rest day: 20-40 contacts, include unilateral/multidirectional
  - box jumps: step-down when fatigued; jump-down deliberately as a drop landing when seeking impact
  - stop set if jump height visibly drops or landing quality degrades

PROGRESSION (by week):
  1-2:  10 bilateral CMJ
  3-4:  15-20 contacts; add lateral 2-leg hops
  5-8:  20-30 contacts; add single-leg multidirectional hops, low drop landings (20-30 cm)
  9+:   30-50 contacts; add skater/lateral bounds, drop jumps (30-40 cm)
  rule: +5-10 contacts/week max; progress complexity before volume

MAINTENANCE:
  - continue indefinitely; benefits reverse within ~6 months of stopping [moderate, Winters & Snow]
```

### Gaps
- Specific jump heights, drop-box heights and GRF targets (×BW) could not be sourced in this session. The ">3–4 × BW" heuristic remains unverified here.
- Whether 2–4 heavy lifting sessions a week change the jump frequency she needs is untested.
- Full texts (PubMed/PMC/Springer/J-STAGE/Loughborough) were inaccessible. Several numbers rest on abstracts or search-result summaries and are flagged above.
