# Sex Differences in Muscle Growth Physiology: Young (18-30) Premenopausal Women vs Men

*Scope: what is the same and what is different about hypertrophy and strength adaptation in young women vs men, for a 22-year-old premenopausal woman whose goal is lean muscle. Research date: 1 Nov 2026.*

*Access note for the report writer: in this session every full-text host (PMC, PubMed, PeerJ, Springer, Frontiers, journals.physiology.org, Stronger By Science, SportRxiv, Europe PMC) was refused by the network proxy. All findings below come from web-search result pages that quoted abstracts, tables and journal landing pages, not from reading full texts. Numbers that came from only one search summary, or that conflicted between summaries, are flagged. Evidence grades: **strong** = consistent meta-analytic or multi-study human evidence; **moderate** = a few decent human studies or one meta-analysis with caveats; **weak** = single small study, animal data, or indirect evidence; **opinion** = expert interpretation or my inference.*

## 1. Absolute vs relative hypertrophy and strength gains (women vs men)

### Takeaway
On the **relative** (percent-of-baseline) scale, young women gain muscle at essentially the same rate as men. They gain **upper-body strength faster** in percent terms, at least when untrained. On the **absolute** scale (cm², cm³, kg of muscle), men gain more. That male advantage is small to moderate (SMD about 0.2 overall) and is concentrated in the **upper body**. "Same rules" is accurate for relative hypertrophy. It is misleading if read as "same absolute results" or "same upper-body results".

### Cited Findings
- **Roberts, Nuckols & Krieger 2020 (JSCR 34(5):1448-1460; pre-registered PROSPERO CRD42018094276).** Hypertrophy: 12 outcomes from 10 studies, ES difference = 0.07 ± 0.06, P = 0.31, so no sex difference. Upper-body strength: 19 outcomes from 17 studies, ES = −0.60 ± 0.16, **favouring women**, P = 0.002, I² = 72.1. Lower-body strength: 23 outcomes, ES = −0.21 ± 0.16, P = 0.20 (n.s.), I² = 74.7. Most participants were untrained and protocols varied. The authors suggest untrained women may have a greater capacity for relative upper-body strength gains. **Grade: moderate** (small number of hypertrophy studies, high heterogeneity, mostly untrained) — [PubMed 32218059](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32218059/)
- Refalo et al. 2025 note that Roberts 2020 **did not separate absolute from relative change** in muscle size. This matters: "no difference" in Roberts is mostly a statement about standardized change scores, not raw muscle gained — [Edinburgh Napier listing of Refalo 2025](https://www.napier.ac.uk/research-and-innovation/research-search/outputs/sex-differences-in-absolute-and-relative-changes-in-muscle-size-following-resistance)
- **Refalo, Nuckols et al. 2025 (PeerJ 13:e19042, published 25 Feb 2025; SportRxiv preprint 2023/2024).** Bayesian meta-analysis, 29 studies included of 2,720 screened, healthy adults of mixed ages. **Absolute** hypertrophy favoured males: SMD = 0.19 (95% HDI 0.11 to 0.28), probability of direction 100%. **Relative** hypertrophy was similar: exponentiated lnRR difference = 0.69%. Results were minimally affected by measurement method and **not affected by training experience**. Authors' conclusion: "females have a similar potential to induce muscle hypertrophy as males, particularly when considering relative increases." **Grade: strong** for "relative is similar, absolute modestly favours men" — [PeerJ](https://peerj.com/articles/19042/); [PMC11869894](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11869894/); [DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/dbf856c9d64840b59318c009c4abd626)
- **Refalo 2025 regional and fibre subgroups** (from a search summary of the paper's tables; verify against the full text). Absolute upper-body SMD = 0.30 (0.14 to 0.44). Absolute lower-body SMD = 0.17 (0.056 to 0.29). Relative change: lower body +1.04% (−2.03 to 4.2%), upper body +0.60% (−2.97 to 4.18%), so no relative difference in either region. **Conflict to flag:** another summary of the abstract says absolute hypertrophy "favoured males in upper-body regions, **but not lower-body regions**". That fits a lower-body effect that is small and uncertain, but the quoted lower-body interval excludes zero. Treat the lower-body absolute difference as small and uncertain. **Grade: moderate** — [PMC11869894](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11869894/); [PeerJ](https://peerj.com/articles/19042/)
- **Hubal et al. 2005 (MSSE 37:964-972).** 12-week unilateral elbow-flexor training in a large cohort of men and women. Size change ranged from −2% to +59% (−0.4 to +13.6 cm²) and 1RM change from 0% to +250%. "Despite greater absolute gains in men, **relative increases in strength measures were greater in women** versus men." Men had only a very slight relative size edge, about 20% vs 18% (a secondary blog figure, not verified in the paper). **Variation between individuals dwarfs the average sex difference.** **Grade: moderate** — [UCF STARS record](https://stars.library.ucf.edu/scopus2000/3942/); [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Variability-in-muscle-size-and-strength-gain-after-Hubal-Gordish-Dressman/3e34d553ee58965916ff063b2706aa613e3a86fc); secondary: [Nutrition Tactics](https://www.nutritiontactics.com/muscle-mass-gains-are-highly-variable/)
- **Ivey et al. 2000 (J Gerontol A 55:M641-M648).** Mixed young (21-29) and older (65-75) men and women, MRI quadriceps volume. **Absolute** volume gain was greater in men: 204 ± 20 vs 101 ± 13 cm³ (p < .01). Age did not alter the response, but sex did. The relative change data were not available to me. A follow-up from the same group (Roth et al. 2001) reported significant gains in all age and sex groups with "no significant differences between the groups". **Grade: weak-moderate**; the population is half older adults, so flag it — [Keiser summary of Ivey 2000](https://resources.keiser.com/longevity-research-on-strength-training/strength-and-power-gains/effects-of-age-gender-and-myostatin-genotype-on-the-hypertrophic); [Roth 2001](https://agsjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1046/j.1532-5415.2001.4911233.x)
- **Jones et al. 2021 (Sports Med 51:503-517). OLDER ADULTS (>50 y), so it does NOT describe a 22-year-old.** About 30 studies and more than 1,400 participants. Absolute gains in size and strength favoured men. Relative: women gained more lower-body strength (g = −0.21 [−0.33, −0.10], p = 0.0003), with no difference in relative upper-body strength (g = −0.29 [−0.62, 0.04], p = 0.08) or relative muscle size (g ≈ 0.10, n.s.) — [PubMed 33332016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33332016/); [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2021/01/210107112423.htm)
- A separate 2023 meta-analysis in adults aged 60 and over reported greater **absolute** upper-body (ES 0.81) and lower-body (ES 0.40) strength gains in older men. Older population, so flagged — [search summary citing PubMed 37507092](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37507092/)

### Inferences
- **Same:** the percent growth a young woman can expect from a given program matches a man's. Studies of untrained participants, including Hubal 2005 and the Roberts 2020 strength data, suggest women may out-gain men in **percent strength**, especially upper body. That is probably partly neural and a lower-baseline effect. (Opinion, moderate confidence.)
- **Different:** because women start with less muscle (see Q7), equal percent gains produce fewer absolute cm² or kg. A woman should expect visibly slower absolute change than a male peer on the same program. The Refalo absolute SMD of about 0.19 is "small", but it compounds on a smaller base. (Moderate.)
- The older-adult meta-analyses (Jones 2021; the 2023 meta) should not be used to argue anything specific about a 22-year-old. Their direction (absolute favours men, relative similar) is the same as in young adults, though.
- Individual variability within each sex (Hubal: −2% to +59% size change) is far larger than the mean sex difference. Her own response cannot be predicted from her sex. (Strong.)

### Gaps
- I could not verify the commonly cited Hubal 2005 sample size (585; I recall 342 women and 243 men) or the commonly cited 1RM percentages (women about +64% vs men about +40%). Both are from memory and need checking against the full text.
- Roberts 2020 and Refalo 2025 together contain few studies in **resistance-trained young women**. Refalo found no moderation by training status, but the trained subgroup is small. Whether the female relative strength advantage persists in trained women is not established.
- I could not access the Refalo 2025 forest plots to resolve the lower-body absolute-SMD discrepancy.

## 2. Hormones: testosterone, estrogen, and why women still grow

### Takeaway
Women's circulating testosterone is roughly an order of magnitude lower (commonly cited as about 10-20x), yet their acute muscle-building (MPS) response and their relative hypertrophy match men's. **Strong** evidence shows that acute exercise-induced hormone spikes do not drive hypertrophy in young adults. Lower **resting** testosterone plausibly explains part of women's smaller baseline muscle mass and smaller absolute gains. Estrogen has real and protective roles in muscle: satellite-cell maintenance and possibly damage attenuation. Most of that mechanistic evidence comes from rodents. **I found no good human evidence that estrogen makes young women recover faster between sessions in a way that changes training.**

### Cited Findings
- **West et al. 2012 (J Appl Physiol 112(11):1805-13).** 8 young men vs 8 young women. Despite an about **45-fold** difference in exercise-induced serum testosterone, myofibrillar protein synthesis rose similarly after resistance exercise plus protein, both acutely and at 24 h. Only minor sex differences appeared in anabolic signaling. **Grade: moderate** (small n, but consistent with other studies) — [J Appl Physiol](https://journals.physiology.org/doi/full/10.1152/japplphysiol.00170.2012); [GSSI review](https://www.gssiweb.org/sports-science-exchange/article/sse-121-sex-based-differences-in-muscle-protein-turnover-and-metabolism-in-aging-feeding-and-exercise-responses)
- **West & Phillips 2012 (Eur J Appl Physiol 112(7):2693-2702).** n = 56 young men, 12 weeks of training. Exercise-induced rises in GH, free testosterone and IGF-1 did **not** correlate with lean-mass or leg-press strength gains. Cortisol and GH showed weak correlations with fibre CSA change. Conclusion: "post-exercise elevations in endogenous hormones are relatively poor predictors of resistance training-induced hypertrophy." **Grade: moderate-strong** — [PMC3371329](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3371329/); [PubMed 22105707](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22105707/)
- **West et al. 2010** manipulated acute hormone exposure directly: "exposure of loaded muscle to acute exercise-induced elevations in endogenous anabolic hormones enhances neither muscle hypertrophy nor strength" in young men. **Grade: moderate** — [West & Phillips 2012 context, via search summary](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3371329/)
- **Morton et al. 2016 (J Appl Physiol 121:129-138).** Resistance-trained young men. "Neither load nor systemic hormones determine resistance training-mediated hypertrophy or strength gains"; no systemic hormone shared significant variance with fibre CSA or lean-mass change. **Grade: moderate** — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/303029912_Neither_load_nor_systemic_hormones_determine_resistance_training-mediated_hypertrophy_or_strength_gains_in_resistance-trained_young_men)
- **Caveat on the above:** West & Phillips 2012, Morton 2016 and West 2010 studied **within-male variation in acute spikes**. They do not show that **resting** testosterone (the 10-20x between-sex gap) is irrelevant. Exogenous testosterone clearly causes hypertrophy in a dose-dependent way. **Grade: opinion/inference**, flagged — see Gaps.
- An editorial accompanying West 2012 said MPS rates do not differ between young men and women "in fasted or fed states, at rest or after exercise". **Grade: moderate** — [PubMed 22442029](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22442029/)
- **Estrogen and satellite cells (Collins et al. 2019, Cell Reports, "Estrogen Regulates the Satellite Cell Compartment in Females").** In **female mice**, estrogen deficiency undermined maintenance of muscle stem cells, and loss of ERα signalling in satellite cells caused satellite-cell apoptosis and impaired muscle strength. **Grade: weak** for humans (mouse data; relevance mainly to estrogen-deficient states such as menopause) — [Cell Reports PDF](https://www.cell.com/cell-reports/pdf/S2211-1247(19)30791-0.pdf); [bioRxiv](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/331777.full.pdf)
- **Enns & Tiidus (J Appl Physiol, rats, downhill running).** Estrogen influenced satellite-cell activation and proliferation after damaging exercise. In rats, sex and estrogen attenuated leukocyte infiltration and indirect damage markers. **Grade: weak** (animal) — [J Appl Physiol](https://journals.physiology.org/doi/full/10.1152/japplphysiol.00128.2007)
- **Human data on myogenic response after eccentric damage:** a 2022 human study found **blunted** satellite-cell and inflammatory-gene responses in females after eccentric contractions. A 2022 review (Oxfeldt et al., "Sex Hormones and Satellite Cell Regulation in Women") summarises the field. Another source notes that "estrogen and sex do not significantly influence post-exercise indexes of muscle damage, inflammation, and repair". The human evidence is mixed. **Grade: weak-moderate** — [PMC9099417](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9099417/); [Oxfeldt 2022, Transl Sports Med](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1155/2022/9065923); [PMC9419932 review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9419932/)
- Hunter's review lists estrogen among the factors in women's lower fatigability, alongside fibre type, perfusion and metabolism. **Grade: moderate** for the fatigability difference itself (see Q5), **weak** for estrogen as its specific cause — [Hunter 2014, PubMed 24433272](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24433272/)

### Inferences
- "Women can't build muscle because they lack testosterone" is **wrong** (strong). "Hormones don't matter at all" is **also an overstatement** (opinion). The acute-spike research is about men and within-sex variation. The large difference in resting androgens likely contributes to sex differences in baseline muscle mass, especially upper-body mass, and to absolute gains. It does not appear to limit **relative** hypertrophy in young women.
- Estrogen is best described as **supportive and protective**: maintaining the satellite-cell pool and possibly damping inflammation and damage. It is not a growth accelerator. For a eumenorrheic 22-year-old, this probably matters most as a reason to avoid things that suppress estrogen, such as low energy availability or amenorrhea. (Opinion.)
- **"Does estrogen make young women recover faster?"** Within a session, young women do recover faster and fatigue less (Q5). Between sessions, the best direct human RT data (Nuckols et al. 2025/26, Q5) show **the same recovery rate** despite more work done. Estrogen-specific recovery claims rest mainly on rodent data. (Moderate.)

### Gaps
- I could not access Hansen 2018 (the review on female hormones and muscle), so its specific conclusions are not captured.
- I did not retrieve a source quantifying the female:male testosterone ratio in young adults. The "~10-20x" figure is the commonly cited range, not verified here.
- No human RCT isolates estrogen's effect on hypertrophy in young eumenorrheic women. Menstrual-phase effects on adaptation are outside this note's scope.

## 3. Fibre type and muscle architecture

### Takeaway
Young women have **similar or only slightly higher type I fibre proportions by number**. Because women's type II fibres are **much smaller** relative to men's, type I fibres take up a **larger share of muscle area** in women. All fibre types are smaller in women. This plausibly underlies women's greater fatigue resistance. Refalo 2025 found **type II hypertrophy similar between sexes**, with a possible (uncertain) male advantage in type I growth.

### Cited Findings
- **Staron et al. 2000 (J Histochem Cytochem 48:623-629).** Vastus lateralis of 55 women (21.2 ± 2.2 y) and 95 men (21.5 ± 2.4 y). Apart from type IC, there were **no significant sex differences in fibre-type distribution** (pooled about 41% I, 31% IIA, 6% IIAB, 20% IIB). **All three major fibre types (I, IIA, IIB) had significantly larger CSA in men.** "Type II fibers of males tend to be larger than the Type I, whereas the reverse is true for the female." Summaries disagree on the exact area rank order (men IIA>IIB=I or IIA>I>IIB; women I=IIA>IIB or I>IIA>IIB), so check Table 3. **Grade: moderate** — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/002215540004800506); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/12547655_Fiber_Type_Composition_of_the_Vastus_Lateralis_Muscle_of_Young_Men_and_Women)
- **Haizlip et al. 2015 (Physiology), "Sex-Based Differences in Skeletal Muscle Kinetics and Fiber-Type Composition".** As quoted in a search summary, men's fibres are larger than women's by about 19% (type I), 59% (IIA) and 66% (IIX). The **gap is largest in type II fibres**. **Grade: moderate** (figures from a summary; verify) — [Physiology](https://journals.physiology.org/doi/full/10.1152/physiol.00024.2014)
- **Nuzzo 2024 meta-analysis (Clinical Anatomy), "Sex differences in skeletal muscle fiber types".** Pooled vastus lateralis type I distribution was **48.0% in men vs 50.6% in women**, a small difference. CSA was greater in men for all fibre types. **Grade: strong** for "small difference in number, larger difference in size" — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/ca.24091)
- **Refalo 2025 fibre-type subgroups** (about 8 studies each). Type I absolute SMD = 0.39 (−0.03 to 0.81); relative difference 12.7% (−6.84 to 34.9%), slightly favouring men but with an interval crossing zero. Type II absolute SMD = 0.10 (−0.33 to 0.52); relative −2.29% (−19.2 to 16.7%), similar between sexes. The authors call the type I difference only possible. **Grade: weak** (wide intervals) — [PMC11869894](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11869894/)
- Hunter's review attributes the sex difference in fatigability for slow, low-load tasks to **contractile mechanisms** (fibre type and metabolism) and also points to perfusion. **Grade: moderate** — [Hunter 2014 PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24433272/); [Hunter 2016 Exp Physiol](https://zendy.io/title/10.1113/ep085370)

### Inferences
- The fibre-type story is often overstated as "women are mostly slow-twitch". By number, the difference is about 2-3 percentage points. The meaningful difference is the **area** profile: women's type II fibres are relatively small. (Moderate.)
- **Training implication (weak or opinion):** a higher type I area share is a plausible reason women tolerate more reps per set and shorter rests. It is **not** evidence that women need lighter loads or higher reps to grow. Hypertrophy across load ranges is similar when sets are taken close to failure (other researchers cover this). No study I found shows that women grow better with a different rep range than men.
- Capillarity and perfusion: I found no source with specific young-female vs male capillary density numbers. See Gaps.

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve sex-comparison data on capillary density per fibre or muscle architecture (pennation angle, fascicle length) in young adults.
- Fibre-type evidence is dominated by vastus lateralis biopsies. Upper-body fibre-type comparisons by sex were not found.

## 4. Muscle protein synthesis and protein needs

### Takeaway
**Strong-to-moderate** evidence shows young women and men have **similar** basal and exercise- or protein-stimulated MPS, despite large testosterone differences. Expert reviews conclude any sex differences in protein **requirements** are "minimal, if they exist at all". The protein rules that apply to men, scaled per kg (ideally per kg of lean mass), apply to young women. "Same rules" is **accurate** here.

### Cited Findings
- West et al. 2012: similar myofibrillar FSR in 8 men vs 8 women after resistance exercise plus 25 g whey, immediately and at 24 h, despite about 45-fold differences in exercise-induced testosterone. **Grade: moderate** — [J Appl Physiol](https://journals.physiology.org/doi/full/10.1152/japplphysiol.00170.2012)
- The editorial on West 2012 ("men aren't from Mars and women aren't from Venus") cites general consensus that MPS does not differ between young men and women "in fasted or fed states, at rest or after exercise". **Grade: moderate-strong** — [PubMed 22442029](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22442029/); [J Appl Physiol editorial](https://journals.physiology.org/doi/full/10.1152/japplphysiol.00354.2012)
- GSSI (Sports Science Exchange 121): basal MPS is similar in young men and women. "Despite large differences in sex steroid concentrations, there is scant evidence that women, young or old, respond markedly differently to resistance training in terms of relative hypertrophy or strength gains." The same review describes Smith et al. 2009 as showing relatively small sex-based differences in protein metabolism. **Grade: moderate-strong** — [GSSI SSE 121](https://www.gssiweb.org/sports-science-exchange/article/sse-121-sex-based-differences-in-muscle-protein-turnover-and-metabolism-in-aging-feeding-and-exercise-responses)
- GSSI (Sports Science Exchange 270, "Muscle protein metabolism and protein requirements for female athletes"): sex-based differences in dietary protein needs "are, if they exist at all, minimal". Protein needs do not change substantially, or at all, across the menstrual cycle or with OC use. **Grade: moderate** — [GSSI SSE 270](https://www.gssiweb.org/en/sports-science-exchange/article/muscle-protein-metabolism-and-protein-requirements-for-female-athletes--aligning-science-with-sex-specific-needs)
- A systematic review on protein requirements of premenopausal female athletes notes that most research has been done in men and applied to women. It argues that sex-hormone effects on exercise metabolism are under-studied. **Grade: opinion/review** — [PMC7696053](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7696053/)
- In **older** women, MPS response to exercise appears somewhat impaired vs older men. That finding is flagged as not applicable to a 22-year-old — [GSSI SSE 121](https://www.gssiweb.org/sports-science-exchange/article/sse-121-sex-based-differences-in-muscle-protein-turnover-and-metabolism-in-aging-feeding-and-exercise-responses)

### Inferences
- For MPS and protein, "women build muscle on the same rules as men" is **accurate**. Dose protein per kg body mass (or lean mass). Because women are lighter with less lean mass, absolute gram targets are lower, but the per-kg logic is the same. (Moderate-strong.)
- MPS is an acute marker. Matching MPS does not guarantee matching absolute hypertrophy, because the same percent turnover on a smaller muscle yields fewer absolute grams. This is consistent with Q1. (Inference.)

### Gaps
- I could not access Dreyer et al. 2010 (leg MPS and mTOR signalling after resistance exercise "independent of sex") to quote numbers. It is widely cited as showing no sex difference.
- No leucine-threshold or per-meal-dose study specific to young women was found.
- Mittendorfer's work (higher basal MPS in older women, not young) was not retrieved.

## 5. Fatigability, between-set and between-session recovery, muscle damage, and reps at %1RM

### Takeaway
This is where "same rules as men" is **most misleading**. At the same relative intensity, young women are **less fatigable** for most isometric and slow or moderate-velocity tasks. They lose less force, and their muscles recover faster between contractions and sets. **But** (a) the advantage shrinks for fast, high-velocity contractions; (b) the best direct resistance-training study shows women **recover between sessions at the same rate** as men; and (c) the Nuzzo 2024 meta-regression found **sex had little influence on how many reps can be done at a given %1RM** in a single set to failure. In other words, women's advantage shows up **across multiple sets and sustained efforts**, not in a single set. On muscle damage, women showed **greater immediate relative strength loss but lower CK** after eccentric work, with similar soreness.

### Cited Findings
- **Hunter 2014 review (Acta Physiol 210(4):768-89).** Women are generally less fatigable than men at the same relative intensity. The difference is **task-specific**: it depends on contraction type, speed, intensity, muscle group and environment. Proposed mechanisms include fibre type, metabolism, perfusion and possibly estrogen, and the difference is not simply explained by women's lower absolute strength. **Grade: strong** for isometric/slow tasks — [PubMed 24433272](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24433272/); [PDF copy](https://paulogentil.com/pdf/Sex%20differences%20in%20human%20fatigability%20-%20mechanisms%20and%20insight%20to%20physiological%20responses.pdf)
- **Hunter 2016 (Exp Physiol), "Sex differences in fatigability of dynamic contractions".** Women are usually less fatigable for isometric contractions, but "whether this occurs for dynamic tasks is less clear". Women were less fatigable for low-load, slow-velocity elbow-flexor work, but "the sex difference is diminished when the repeated contractions are relatively high in velocity". Power loss was similar in men and women for fast contractions at a 20% load. **Grade: moderate** — [Zendy, DOI 10.1113/ep085370](https://zendy.io/title/10.1113/ep085370); [Marquette repository](https://epublications.marquette.edu/exsci_fac/84)
- **Fulco et al. 1999.** 9 men vs 9 women **matched for adductor pollicis strength**. Women's MVC force declined about **2-fold slower**, endurance time was **nearly twice as long**, and women recovered faster between contractions. Strength matching shows the effect is not just about lower absolute force. **Grade: moderate** (small n, hand muscle, isometric) — [Scilit record](https://scilit.com/publications/b023a1e1720c6995b4f7e58803c3084d); [BVS record](https://pesquisa.bvsalud.org/bvsms/resource/pt/mdl-10606825)
- **Ansdell et al. (2017, 2019, 2020) knee extensors.** At 30% and 50% MVC intermittent isometric contractions, men lost more force, and the difference was not explained by absolute strength (2017). After exercise normalized to each person's power-duration relationship (critical power), **women showed smaller losses of knee-extensor contractile function** and less loss of voluntary activation after heavy-intensity exercise, with **similar time to task failure** (J Physiol 2020). The difference was associated with less muscle deoxygenation in women (2019 companion study). **Grade: moderate-strong** — [ScienceDirect 2017](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S105064111730233X); [PubMed 32964441](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32964441/); [Mendeley 2019](https://www.mendeley.com/catalogue/6a95e434-75be-30f2-b2b1-862d4720bdca/)
- **Nuckols, Overpeck, Hanson & Battaglini (PeerJ, DOI 10.7717/peerj.20542; accepted 17 Nov 2025, published about Jan 2026). The most directly relevant resistance-training study.** In a multi-set bench-press fatigue protocol, women completed substantially more reps than men (**about 58 vs 30**). Post-training soreness and recovery of estimated 1RM did **not** differ by sex. Conclusion: "females fatigue slower than males during multiple sets of bench press yet appear to recover from training at the same rate in spite of completing a higher relative workload." Rep counts varied widely between individuals (CV 47.8% women, 35.8% men). **Grade: moderate** (single study; sample size not captured) — [PeerJ](https://peerj.com/articles/20542/); [PMC12790778](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12790778/)
- **Amdi et al. 2025 scoping review (Int J Sports Med), "Biological sex differences in fatigue in resistance-trained individuals".** Females "may experience less neuromuscular fatigue and improved recovery following resistance training than males; however, it is unclear whether this applies to resistance-trained individuals." **Grade: moderate (review)** — [PubMed 40112869](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40112869/)
- **Judge & Burke 2010 (IJSPP 5(2):184-196).** 6 male and 6 female athletes plus 4 male and 4 female students, with a bench-press 5RM-estimated 1RM measured at different recovery times. Women's strength did not vary with recovery time. The authors suggested **48 h recovery for men vs 4 h for women**. **Critique:** a 2021 paper says the conclusions were "erroneously based ... on within-sex time effects and not between-sex differences". **Grade: weak** (tiny n, flawed inference). Do **not** use it to claim women need only 4 h between sessions — [Human Kinetics](https://journals.humankinetics.com/view/journals/ijspp/5/2/article-p184.xml); [critique, PMC8618037](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8618037/)
- **Sewright et al. 2008 (MSSE), muscle damage.** 58 women and 42 men did 50 maximal eccentric elbow-flexor contractions. **Women had significantly greater relative strength loss immediately after** and were more likely to be high responders for immediate strength loss. **Men had a larger CK response.** Serum myoglobin and **soreness did not differ**. CK, soreness and strength loss were more tightly correlated in men (r = 0.55-0.59) than women (r = 0.12-0.49). **Grade: moderate** — [SDSU Coaching Science Abstracts](https://coachsci.sdsu.edu/csa/vol141/sewright.htm); [BVS record](https://pesquisa.bvsalud.org/bvsvs/resource/pt/mdl-18202579)
- A 2016 PLOS ONE study found higher CK in men but, "based on isometric torque loss, there is no sex difference in exercise induced muscle damage". **Grade: moderate** — [PLOS ONE PDF, MMU repository](https://e-space.mmu.ac.uk/605457/11/journal.pone.0150848.PDF)
- **Nuzzo, Pinto, Nosaka & Steele 2024 (Sports Med 54:303-321; online Oct 2023), reps at %1RM.** 952 reps-to-failure tests, 7,289 individuals, 452 groups, 269 studies; groups were 66% male and 60% trained. **Correct finding:** "the moderator analysis suggested **little influence of sex, age, or training status** on the REPS ~ %1RM relationship, thus the general main model ... can be applied to all individuals." **Exercise** was the meaningful moderator: more reps on leg press than bench press across loads, so separate tables were built. I could not retrieve the exact sex coefficients (they are in the paper or its OSF supplement); one snippet hints at some sex-related differences without magnitudes. **Grade: strong** for "sex is not a meaningful moderator of single-set reps to failure at a given %1RM" — [Solent Pure](https://pure.solent.ac.uk/en/publications/maximal-number-of-repetitions-at-percentages-of-the-one-repetitio/); [ECU repository](https://ro.ecu.edu.au/ecuworks2022-2026/3100); [PMC10933212](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10933212/); [Nuzzo Substack](https://jameslnuzzo.substack.com/p/the-repetitions-1rm-relationship)

### Inferences
- **Reconciling Nuzzo 2024 with fatigability research:** in a **single set** to failure at X% 1RM, women do not reliably do more reps than men. Across **repeated sets with fixed rest**, women retain performance better (the Nuckols PeerJ bench study, about 58 vs 30 reps). The female advantage therefore appears to be **between-set recovery and fatigue resistance**, not single-set capacity. Do not tell her "women can do more reps at the same %1RM", which is the claim Nuzzo does not support. (Moderate.)
- **Between-session recovery:** the best RT-specific data show **similar** recovery in women and men, even though women did more relative work. "Women recover faster, so they can train each muscle far more often" is **not established**. Judge & Burke 2010 is too weak to support it. (Moderate.)
- **Muscle damage:** women are not uniformly less damaged. CK is lower, but immediate force loss after heavy eccentrics can be **greater**, and soreness is similar. "Estrogen protects women from damage" is mostly a rodent and CK-based story. (Moderate.)
- **Proximity to failure (opinion):** because women fatigue less across sets, a set "at 2 RIR" late in a session may represent a different fatigue state than the same set for a man. Reps-in-reserve self-estimation accuracy by sex was not researched here.

### Gaps
- Sample size of the Nuckols et al. PeerJ study, and whether its 58 vs 30 rep figure came from sets to failure at a fixed %1RM with fixed rest, could not be confirmed.
- **No trial I found tests different rest intervals in women vs men for hypertrophy outcomes.** Rest-interval recommendations for women are extrapolated from acute fatigue data.
- Hunter's 2016 performance-fatigability review in clinical populations and aging, and Ansdell 2019 recovery numbers, were not read in full.
- A 2020 meta-analysis of 23 trials (higher absolute CK in men; trend toward greater relative strength loss in women) appeared in a search summary, but I could not verify its citation, so it is not cited here.
- Kerksick's and Enns & Tiidus's reviews of damage and soreness by sex were not retrieved.

## 6. Hormonal contraception and muscle gains

### Takeaway
Evidence is **mixed and low quality** (small, non-randomized, user vs non-user comparisons). Most controlled training studies with second- or third-generation combined OCs show **no impairment**, and some show a trend toward **greater** hypertrophy or FFM gains in OC users. One larger observational study (Riechman & Lee 2022) found **smaller lean-mass gains** in OC users, driven by users of **more androgenic progestins**. A defensible reading is that typical modern OCs do not meaningfully harm gains, with progestin type an open question. No meaningful human evidence exists for IUDs, implants or injectables. **Grade overall: weak-moderate.**

### Cited Findings
- **Riechman & Lee 2022 (JSCR 36(11):3074-3080).** Women aged 18-29, 38 non-OC vs 34 OC users, 10 weeks of whole-body RT. Non-OC users gained more lean mass: **1.6 kg vs 1.0 kg** (3.5% vs 2.1%). The OC group had lower DHEA, DHEAS and IGF-1 and higher cortisol, before and after training. By progestin androgenicity: **medium-high androgenic progestin 0.3 kg**, low-androgenic 1.1 kg, non-OC 1.6 kg. Observational design; small subgroups. A 2009 conference press release reported different numbers (73 women aged 18-31); rely on the published paper. **Grade: weak-moderate** — [PubMed 33993156](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33993156/); [2009 press coverage](https://www.news-medical.net/news/2009/04/20/48525.aspx)
- **Dalgaard et al. 2019 (Front Physiol).** 10-week supervised leg training, 14 OC users vs 14 non-users. There was a **trend toward greater muscle CSA gain in OC users**, and **type I fibre area increased significantly more** in OC users. Strength gains were similar. **Grade: weak** — [JYX record](https://jyx.jyu.fi/jyx/Record/jyx_123456789_65104)
- **Dalgaard et al. (JSCR 2022; epub 2020), "Influence of Second Generation Oral Contraceptive Use on Adaptations to Resistance Training in Young Untrained Women".** 20 OC vs 18 non-users, 10 weeks, MRI quadriceps. Second-generation OC use "did not significantly improve adaptations", but there was a **trend toward greater FFM gains** in OC users. **Grade: weak** — [AMiner](https://www.aminer.org/pub/5f197c619fced0a24b3bcd19/influence-of-second-generation-oral-contraceptive-use-on-adaptations-to-resistance-training); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/343163086_Influence_of_Second_Generation_Oral_Contraceptive_Use_on_Adaptations_to_Resistance_Training_in_Young_Untrained_Women)
- **Oxfeldt et al. 2020 (J Appl Physiol).** 38 untrained women. Quadriceps fibre CSA, satellite cells per type I fibre and MuRF-1 mRNA all increased **with no between-group difference**. OC users showed a MHC IIx to IIa shift and greater MRF4 mRNA. **Grade: weak-moderate** — [X-MOL listing](https://www.x-mol.com/paper/1316948215239774208); [PubMed 32694286](https://www.pubmed.gov/32694286/)
- **Romance et al. 2019 (Int J Sports Med), "Oral Contraceptive Use does not Negatively Affect Body Composition and Strength Adaptations in Trained Women".** 23 resistance-trained women, 8 weeks. **FFM increased significantly in OC users but not in non-users**. Strength and power gains were similar. **Grade: weak** (n of about 11-12 per group) — [PubMed 31491790](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31491790/)
- **2025 study, "Second-Generation Oral Contraceptive Use Is Associated With Greater Muscle Hypertrophy Following Resistance Training".** 12 weeks; greater hypertrophy in specific muscles in OC users and no strength difference. **Grade: weak** (details not retrieved) — [PubMed 41420473](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41420473/)
- Stronger By Science summary: second- and third-generation combined OCs "don't seem to meaningfully impact either strength gains or hypertrophy outcomes". **Grade: opinion (expert synthesis)** — [Stronger By Science](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/?p=57958)
- A systematic review called the evidence mixed and called for more experimental studies on OC and menstrual-cycle effects on resistance-training responses. **Grade: opinion/review** — [PMC10798924 (review table)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10798924/table/Tab1)

### Inferences
- For a 22-year-old on a typical combined OC: the weight of controlled evidence says **no meaningful penalty**, and possibly a small benefit in FFM or hypertrophy. That is weak evidence, but consistent across Dalgaard 2019, Dalgaard 2022, Oxfeldt 2020, Romance 2019 and the 2025 study. (Weak-moderate.)
- The one negative signal (Riechman & Lee) points specifically at **more androgenic progestins** (for example levonorgestrel-type, second generation). That partly conflicts with Dalgaard 2022 and the 2025 study, which found no harm or a benefit with second-generation OCs. **This conflict is unresolved**, so it should not change contraceptive choice. Contraceptive choice is a medical decision for her clinician. (Opinion.)
- Hormonal IUDs, implants, injectables and progestin-only pills are essentially unstudied for hypertrophy.

### Gaps
- No RCT randomizes women to OC vs none for hypertrophy outcomes, and all studies are user vs non-user with possible self-selection.
- I found no data on hormonal IUD, implant or DMPA effects on RT adaptations.
- I could not verify the specific progestins in each study's OC group.

## 7. Upper body specifically

### Takeaway
The sex difference in muscle mass is **larger in the upper body** (about 40%) than the lower body. Men's **absolute** hypertrophy advantage is clearest in the upper body (Refalo 2025: SMD about 0.30 upper vs about 0.17 lower). On a **relative** basis, upper-body hypertrophy is similar between sexes, and untrained women make **larger relative upper-body strength gains** (Roberts 2020 ES −0.60). This is where "same rules as men" is most misleading for **outcomes**. A woman should expect smaller absolute upper-body change and a larger starting gap, even though her muscles respond the same proportionally.

### Cited Findings
- **Janssen et al. 2000 (J Appl Physiol 89(1):81-88).** Whole-body MRI, 468 men and women aged 18-88. Men had more skeletal muscle in absolute terms (33.0 vs 21.0 kg) and relative to body mass (38.4% vs 30.6%). "Gender differences were greater in the upper (40%) than lower..." body (the lower-body figure was truncated in the snippet; it is commonly cited as about 33%, unverified). Weight and height explained about 50% of SM variance in both sexes. The age range is broad, but the sex difference in distribution applies to young adults. **Grade: strong** — [PubMed 10904038](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10904038/); [Bioblast summary](https://bioblast.at/index.php/Janssen_2000_J_Appl_Physiol)
- **Refalo 2025:** absolute hypertrophy favoured men more in the upper body (SMD 0.30, 0.14-0.44) than the lower body (SMD 0.17, 0.056-0.29). The abstract-level summary says "upper-body ... but not lower-body". Relative change: upper +0.60% and lower +1.04% difference, both with intervals spanning zero. **Grade: moderate** — [PMC11869894](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11869894/)
- **Roberts 2020:** upper-body **strength** gains favoured women (ES −0.60 ± 0.16, P = 0.002). Lower-body strength showed no significant difference (ES −0.21, P = 0.20). **Grade: moderate** — [PubMed 32218059](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32218059/)
- **Hubal 2005 (elbow flexors):** men had greater absolute size gains and women greater relative strength gains, with a slight male edge in relative size (about 20% vs 18%, secondary source). **Grade: moderate** — [UCF STARS](https://stars.library.ucf.edu/scopus2000/3942/)
- A 2021 study titled "Resistance training induces similar adaptations of upper and lower-body muscles between sexes" exists. I could not read its details, so it is title-level only. **Grade: weak** — [PMC8648816](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8648816/)

### Inferences
- **Mechanism vs outcome:** the **mechanism** (relative response of upper-body muscle to training) looks the **same**. The **outcome** (absolute upper-body muscle gained, and the baseline gap) is **different**. (Moderate.)
- Women's large relative upper-body strength gains in untrained studies are likely partly **neural** and novelty-driven, from a low baseline. They are not evidence of faster upper-body hypertrophy. (Opinion.)
- **Practical (opinion):** for a lean-muscle goal, it is reasonable to give the upper body **at least equal and possibly greater** weekly hard-set volume than "default" women's programs, which often bias toward lower body and glutes. This is because her starting gap is largest there and absolute progress will be slowest. There is no direct trial showing extra upper-body volume helps women more than men.

### Gaps
- No trial compares upper-body volume dose-response by sex.
- Whether women's upper-body relative strength advantage persists in trained women is unknown.
- The Janssen lower-body percentage could not be verified from accessible text.

## 8. What this means in practice: which programming variables should plausibly differ for a young woman

### Takeaway
**Most of the "rules" really are the same**: progressive overload, sets taken reasonably close to failure, sufficient weekly hard sets, a wide effective load range, and per-kg protein. The **reasonable sex-specific adjustments** are modest and mostly rest on acute fatigability data rather than long-term hypertrophy trials:
- shorter rest between sets is likely tolerable, with less performance drop-off;
- possibly more sets per session;
- emphasis on the upper body;
- realistic expectations for **absolute** (not relative) gains.

None of these has a direct sex-by-variable hypertrophy RCT behind it. Below is a variable-by-variable grade.

### Cited Findings
- Relative hypertrophy is the same in women and men, with absolute gains modestly lower: **strong** — [Refalo 2025](https://peerj.com/articles/19042/)
- Acute MPS response is the same, and protein needs differ minimally if at all: **moderate-strong** — [West 2012](https://journals.physiology.org/doi/full/10.1152/japplphysiol.00170.2012); [GSSI SSE 270](https://www.gssiweb.org/en/sports-science-exchange/article/muscle-protein-metabolism-and-protein-requirements-for-female-athletes--aligning-science-with-sex-specific-needs)
- Women fatigue less across multiple sets: in bench press, about 58 vs 30 reps in a multi-set protocol. **Moderate** — [Nuckols et al. PeerJ](https://peerj.com/articles/20542/); [Hunter 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24433272/); [Ansdell 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32964441/)
- Between-session recovery is similar in women and men: **moderate** (one direct RT study) — [Nuckols et al. PeerJ](https://peerj.com/articles/20542/)
- Single-set reps at a given %1RM do not meaningfully differ by sex: **strong** — [Nuzzo 2024](https://pure.solent.ac.uk/en/publications/maximal-number-of-repetitions-at-percentages-of-the-one-repetitio/)
- The fatigue advantage shrinks for fast, high-velocity contractions: **moderate** — [Hunter 2016](https://zendy.io/title/10.1113/ep085370)
- A 2026 narrative review, "Sex differences in resistance training: a brief narrative review" (Sport Sciences for Health), exists. Its contents were not accessible — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11332-026-01650-8)

**Variable-by-variable (each row's grade applies to the "should it differ for her?" claim):**

| Variable | Should it differ for a young woman? | Grade | Basis |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Rest between sets** | Plausibly **shorter rests work** (for example about 60-120 s on many exercises) with less performance loss than men experience. This does not mean shorter rests are *better* for growth. | Weak-moderate | Fatigability data (Hunter, Fulco, Ansdell, Nuckols PeerJ). No sex-by-rest hypertrophy trial found. |
| **Rep range / load** | **Same.** Use the standard reps-at-%1RM tables. No evidence women need lighter or higher-rep work to grow. | Strong (reps-%1RM); moderate (no sex-by-load hypertrophy difference) | Nuzzo 2024; Refalo 2025 type II hypertrophy similar between sexes. |
| **Volume per session** | Possibly **more sets per session are tolerable** before performance drops. Whether more volume yields more growth for women specifically is untested. | Weak (opinion) | Lower within-session fatigue; Nuckols PeerJ. |
| **Weekly volume / dose-response** | **Same** framework (count hard sets per muscle). No evidence of a different dose-response curve. | Moderate (absence of evidence) | Relative hypertrophy similar; no sex-moderated dose-response data found. |
| **Frequency** | **Same.** "Women recover in 4 h" (Judge & Burke) is not credible. The direct RT data show similar between-session recovery. | Moderate | Nuckols PeerJ; Judge & Burke critique. |
| **Proximity to failure** | **Same** principle. Note that women may need to be pushed harder in later sets because they fatigue less, and an RIR estimate made early may drift. | Weak/opinion | Fatigability data; no sex-specific RIR-accuracy data retrieved. |
| **Exercise selection / emphasis** | **Prioritize upper body** at least equally, given the about 40% baseline gap and the larger absolute male advantage there. | Weak-moderate (inference from Janssen, Refalo) | No direct trial. |
| **Eccentric-heavy or novel work** | Expect possibly **greater immediate strength loss** after unaccustomed heavy eccentrics, with similar soreness. Introduce gradually. | Weak-moderate | Sewright 2008. |
| **Protein** | **Same** per-kg targets. No adjustment needed for cycle phase or OC. | Moderate-strong | GSSI reviews; West 2012. |
| **Hormonal contraception** | No programming change. Combined OCs are not shown to impair gains; one study flags androgenic progestins. A medical decision, not a training lever. | Weak-moderate | Q6. |

### Inferences
- **Where "same rules as men" was accurate:** the mechanisms and drivers of hypertrophy (mechanical tension, effort, overload, volume), MPS response, protein needs, the reps-%1RM relationship, relative (percent) hypertrophy, and between-session recovery. (Strong-moderate.)
- **Where it was misleading:**
  1. **Absolute outcomes:** women gain fewer cm² or kg, especially in the upper body. "Same rules" can imply "same results", which is false.
  2. **Fatigue profile:** women are measurably more fatigue-resistant across sets. Rest periods, and how hard later sets feel, can reasonably differ.
  3. **Starting point:** a roughly 40% upper-body muscle-mass gap means the upper body deserves deliberate emphasis.
  4. **Hormonal context** (estrogen status, contraception, energy availability) is a female-specific consideration, even if typical OCs are not a problem.
  5. **Muscle-damage profile** differs in pattern (CK vs force loss), not simply "women are protected".

  She was right that the line was oversimplified. (Opinion, grounded in the cited evidence.)
- **Best one-line replacement (opinion):** "Women build muscle by the same mechanisms and grow by the same percentage as men. They start smaller (especially the upper body), gain less in absolute terms, and fatigue less across sets, so the principles are shared but expectations, rest and emphasis can reasonably differ."

### Gaps
- No RCT compares sex-by-rest-interval, sex-by-volume or sex-by-frequency effects on hypertrophy in young adults.
- Menstrual-cycle-phase-based programming was out of scope for this note. Other researchers may cover it.
- Smith-Ryan's and Elliott-Sale's reviews were not retrieved, and the Stronger By Science "Do men and women build muscle and strength differently?" article could not be read ([URL](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/sex-differences-muscle-strength/)).
