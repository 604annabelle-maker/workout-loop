# Women-Specific Programming for Lean Muscle (Premenopausal, ~18-30 y): What Differs From a Generic Hypertrophy Template, and Who Says So

Scope and method notes (read first):
- Population: healthy, premenopausal women ~18-30. Anything derived from peri/post-menopausal or older samples is flagged **[MENOPAUSE-DERIVED]**.
- Grades used on every claim: **[Strong]** = multiple consistent meta-analyses and/or position stands; **[Moderate]** = one good meta-analysis or several consistent RCTs, with limits; **[Weak]** = few small or acute studies, indirect evidence; **[Opinion]** = coach/expert view without direct trial support.
- Research date: 1 Nov 2026. Full-text fetches were blocked in this session (pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov, acsm.org, frontiersin.org and mennohenselmans.com did not resolve), so findings below come from abstracts, repository records and search-engine summaries of primary papers. Where only a secondary summary was available, this is stated.
- Note on Stacy Sims: she is first author of the 2023 ISSN female-athlete position stand cited below. Its premenopausal recommendations match general sports-nutrition ranges; its menopause-specific add-ons are flagged.

## Baseline: Do young women respond to resistance training differently from men at all?

### Takeaway
Relative muscle growth is essentially the same in women and men; men gain slightly more in absolute terms, mostly in the upper body. Relative upper-body strength gains favour women (at least when untrained). The evidence does not support a different overall template for women. [Strong]

### Cited Findings
- Roberts, Nuckols & Krieger 2020 (JSCR, pre-registered meta-analysis): hypertrophy did not differ by sex (12 outcomes, 10 studies; ES = 0.07, P = 0.31, I² = 0). Relative upper-body strength gains favoured women (19 outcomes, 17 studies; ES = -0.60, P = 0.002, I² = 72%). Lower-body strength did not differ (ES = -0.21, P = 0.20). Authors suggest untrained women have a higher capacity to increase upper-body strength. [Strong for hypertrophy parity; Moderate for the upper-body strength effect, given high heterogeneity and mostly untrained samples] — [PubMed 32218059](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32218059/)
- Refalo et al. 2025 (PeerJ, Bayesian meta-analysis, 29 studies, healthy adults 18-45, same training in both sexes; Nuckols co-author): absolute muscle-size gains slightly favoured males (SMD = 0.19, 95% HDI 0.11-0.28), but relative gains were similar. Absolute upper-body (not lower-body) hypertrophy favoured males. Type I fibre growth slightly favoured males; Type II similar. Training experience did not moderate results. Conclusion: "females have a similar potential to induce muscle hypertrophy as males (particularly when considering relative increases)". [Strong] — [PeerJ 19042](https://peerj.com/articles/19042); [Napier repository](https://napier-repository.worktribe.com/output/4166718/sex-differences-in-absolute-and-relative-changes-in-muscle-size-following-resistance-training-in-healthy-adults-a-systematic-review-with-bayesian-meta-analysis)
- Colenso-Semple, D'Souza, Elliott-Sale & Phillips 2023 (umbrella review) note the field's historic bias toward studying men, partly from worry about hormonal fluctuations, and conclude cycle phase does not meaningfully change strength or hypertrophy outcomes. [Moderate] — [Frontiers / PMC10076834](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10076834)

### Inferences
- Because relative growth matches and fibre-type data mostly match, the "male-derived" hypertrophy template (volume, effort, load range, progression) is a valid starting point for a 22-year-old woman. Any women-specific changes are fine-tuning, not a different system.
- The upper-body result (lower absolute gains, larger relative strength gains in untrained women) is the strongest data-based reason to make sure upper-body work is not under-dosed in women's programs.

### Gaps
- Few trials in well-trained women; Refalo et al. call for work in highly trained participants.
- No trial found that randomises women (only) to "female-adapted" versus standard templates.

## Rest intervals: do women need less rest between sets?

### Takeaway
Acute studies show women keep their reps better across sets at the same relative load and rest, so they can often use the shorter end of normal rest ranges without losing volume. No long-term trial shows that shorter rest builds more muscle in women, and the hypertrophy meta-analysis argues against going much below ~60 s for anyone. [Weak for any sex-specific rest rule]

### Cited Findings
- Ratamess et al. 2012a (JSCR 26(7):1817-26): 3 sets of up to 10 reps at 75% 1RM bench press with 1, 2 or 3 min rest. Women performed more reps than men at every rest length; men lost more bar velocity and power across sets. Authors: "shorter RIs may suffice in women to attain a specific volume." [Weak: acute, single exercise] — [UCF STARS record](https://stars.library.ucf.edu/scopus2010/4301); [IUCC record](https://cris.iucc.ac.il/en/publications/the-effects-of-rest-interval-length-on-acute-bench-press-performa/)
- Ratamess et al. 2012b (JSCR 26(11):2929-38; 22 trained men and women, ≥1 y training): women did significantly more reps than men at all rest lengths on bench and incline press; changing rest on the first exercise affected only the first set of one later exercise in women. [Weak] — [IUCC record](https://cris.iucc.ac.il/en/publications/the-effects-of-rest-interval-length-manipulation-of-the-first-upp/)
- A 2012 commentary speculated that men may need longer rest than women because of the fatigability difference. This is speculation, not a finding. [Opinion] — [search summary of Ratamess-related commentary](https://cris.iucc.ac.il/en/publications/the-effects-of-rest-interval-length-manipulation-of-the-first-upp/)
- Judge & Burke 2010 (IJSPP 5(2):184-96): a between-session test, not between-set rest. After a 5-set bench session, estimated 1RM fell at 4 h and 24 h in men only; women showed no drop at any recovery time. Authors suggested 48 h for men and 4 h for women "may optimize strength development". Very small sample (reported as 4-6 per sex in secondary summaries; not verified). [Weak] — [PubMed 20625191](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20625191/)
- Singer et al. 2024 (Frontiers in Sports and Active Living; Bayesian meta-analysis, 9 studies, 19 measurements; Schoenfeld and Swinton co-authors): a small hypertrophy benefit for rest >60 s, possibly from preserved volume load; no appreciable extra benefit beyond 90 s. Whole-body estimates were near zero. Training to failure did not change the rest effect. Sex was not reported as a moderator in the summaries found. [Moderate for the general >60 s finding; not sex-specific] — [Frontiers abstract](https://frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2024.1429789/abstract)
- Hunter 2014 review (sex differences in fatigability): women are usually less fatigable than men in sustained or repeated contractions at the same relative intensity and recover force faster. The difference is task-dependent (larger in elbow flexors than ankle dorsiflexors) and is thought to come from fibre type and neural factors. [Moderate as physiology; indirect for programming] — [Hunter review PDF](https://paulogentil.com/pdf/Sex%20differences%20in%20human%20fatigability%20-%20mechanisms%20and%20insight%20to%20physiological%20responses.pdf)
- Amdi, Fyfe, Yoon, Nuckols & Refalo 2025 (Int J Sports Med scoping review, resistance-trained people): sex differences in neuromuscular fatigue were generally small or absent; some evidence of greater male fatigability during or right after training, mainly in complex free-weight lifts. [Moderate that the difference shrinks in trained lifters] — [Thieme abstract](https://thieme-connect.de/products/ejournals/abstract/10.1055/a-2563-9449); [AUT repository](https://openrepository.aut.ac.nz/items/7a0f0ee6-6e7d-4781-98a4-e87760d2c420/full)
- Amdi et al. 2021 (Sports): women recovered lifting velocity faster after a fixed-volume squat protocol, but no sex difference appeared after a protocol matched to 4-6RM. [Weak] — [PMC8618037](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8618037/)
- Nuckols, Overpeck, Hanson & Battaglini 2026 (PeerJ 14:e20542) tested sex differences in fatigue and recovery with a protocol closer to real practice. The abstract's results could not be retrieved. A Layne Norton/Biolayne video summary reports women did roughly twice as many total reps in a multi-set bench protocol, recovered velocity faster between sets, and that relative hypertrophy is similar when volume is equated. [Unverified; treat as Weak until the paper is read] — [PeerJ 20542](https://peerj.com/articles/20542); [Biolayne video summary](https://www.pulse.bot/fitness/video/women-should-train-completely-differently-than-men-educational-video-biolayne-84c88c1e-30af-4cfa-9e91-1d89a0e8c076/)
- Popular articles give specific numbers such as "45-60 s for women vs 90-120 s for men". These figures were not traceable to a trial. [Opinion, unsupported] — [secondary summary in search results](https://fitnessvolt.com/women-should-train-differently/)

### Inferences
- A defensible women-specific rule is to rest by readiness, not by a fixed shorter clock: if reps hold, a woman can use the low end of normal ranges (about 60-90 s on isolation and machine work, about 2 min on heavy compounds). Do not cut below ~60 s, because the only hypertrophy meta-analysis favours >60 s.
- The fatigue advantage shrinks near maximal loads and in trained lifters (Amdi 2021, 2025), so heavy compound sets still deserve full rest.

### Gaps
- No long-term RCT compares rest lengths in women for hypertrophy.
- Could not confirm whether Singer 2024 tested sex as a moderator, or the results of Nuckols et al. 2026 (full text blocked).

## Volume and frequency: do women tolerate or benefit from more weekly sets or higher frequency?

### Takeaway
Many respected coaches (RP/Israetel, Contreras, Nippard) say women can handle somewhat more volume and frequency because they fatigue less and recover faster. This is a reasonable extrapolation from acute fatigue data, but no trial shows women gain more muscle from more volume than men do. Treat it as permission to progress toward the higher end of normal ranges if recovery allows, not as a requirement. [Opinion / Weak]

### Cited Findings
- ACSM 2026 position stand (overview of 137 systematic reviews, >30,000 participants): train all major muscle groups at least twice a week; for hypertrophy aim for higher weekly volume, about 10 sets per muscle group. No sex-specific volume recommendation appeared in the summaries found. [Strong for the general dose] — [EurekAlert release](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1119943); [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/03/260319074552.htm); [Newswise](https://www.newswise.com/articles/acsm-unveils-landmark-2026-resistance-training-guidelines-first-update-in-17-years)
- Dr Mike Israetel (RP), as summarised by BoxRox: women can handle higher per-session volume and recover faster, allowing higher frequency per muscle group. He reportedly acknowledges his knowledge here is "largely theoretical". [Opinion] — [BoxRox summary](https://www.boxrox.com/?p=192197)
- RP analysed over 1 million logged workouts in its app and found women self-select programs shifted toward the lower body. This describes preference, not what is optimal. [Descriptive only] — [BoxRox](https://www.boxrox.com/?p=250604)
- Bret Contreras (Huberman Lab and other interviews, secondhand summaries): train each muscle at least twice weekly, possibly three times for advanced lifters; women often prefer 3 lower-body plus 2 upper-body days; women recover slightly faster and tolerate more volume; glutes can be trained more often. A BoxRox article notes there is no evidence that training glutes many times a week is better; this is his belief. [Opinion] — [Huberman Lab episode](https://www.hubermanlab.com/episode/build-ideal-physique-bret-contreras); [BoxRox on glute frequency](https://www.boxrox.com/?p=176424)
- Jeff Nippard: sex differences are "slight variations and tweaks to an already sound fundamental training philosophy"; most differences come from muscle mass and body fat, not sex per se; a summary of his 12-study video says women can do more sets and reps per workout and more workouts per week because of recovery differences. [Opinion, informed by evidence review] — [Nippard video transcript](https://rosetta.to/u/jeffnippard/men-vs-women-sex-differences-in-training-science-explained-12-studies); [BoxRox summary](https://www.boxrox.com/?p=172526)
- Menno Henselmans (secondhand summary of his Bayesian Bodybuilding view): women can handle higher volume and should use higher reps, tied to fatigue resistance. His primary article could not be fetched. [Opinion, unverified] — [interview page](https://mennohenselmans.com/gender-differences-jeff-nippard/); [secondary summary](https://www.adaymag.com/2015/07/03/bayesian-body-building.html)
- The data underneath the claim are acute fatigue and recovery studies (Ratamess 2012; Judge & Burke 2010; Hunter 2014), and the trained-lifter review finds those differences small (Amdi 2025). [Weak as a basis for volume prescription] — [Amdi 2025](https://thieme-connect.de/products/ejournals/abstract/10.1055/a-2563-9449)
- Relative hypertrophy is the same in both sexes under the same training, and Refalo et al. found no effect of training experience, which argues against women needing more volume to grow. [Moderate, indirect] — [PeerJ 19042](https://peerj.com/articles/19042)

### Inferences
- Start a 22-year-old woman at the evidence-based dose (about 10+ hard sets per muscle per week, each muscle 2x/week) and add sets only if performance keeps rising and recovery is good. That respects the coach consensus without assuming it.
- Higher frequency is mainly a practical tool (spreading sets so each session stays good quality), which fits women's preferences for more lower-body days.

### Gaps
- No RCT found that compares volume dose-response by sex, or tests "women-only higher volume" templates.
- Greg Nuckols' own long-form opinion on women's volume could not be retrieved; his published work is listed under the coaches section.

## Rep ranges and load: do women benefit from more reps or a wider rep range?

### Takeaway
The largest dataset shows sex has little effect on how many reps people can do at a given %1RM, so women do not need different load tables or a different rep range. The usual broad hypertrophy range (moderate loads, sets taken close to failure) applies. Women's fatigue advantage shows up mainly across sets, not within a single set. [Moderate]

### Cited Findings
- Nuzzo et al. 2024 (Sports Medicine 54:303-321; meta-regression of 952 reps-to-failure tests, 7,289 people, 269 studies): little influence of sex, age or training status on the reps-to-%1RM relationship; exercise type was the main moderator (bench press and leg press need their own tables). [Moderate-Strong] — [Solent repository](https://pure.solent.ac.uk/en/publications/maximal-number-of-repetitions-at-percentages-of-the-one-repetitio/); [SportRxiv preprint](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/291); [Nuzzo's own summary](https://jameslnuzzo.substack.com/p/the-repetitions-1rm-relationship)
- Ratamess 2012a/b found women did more total reps across multiple sets at 75% 1RM, mainly because they lost less from set to set. [Weak] — [UCF STARS](https://stars.library.ucf.edu/scopus2010/4301)
- ACSM 2026: heavier loads (≥80% 1RM, 2-3 sets) for strength; about 10 weekly sets per muscle for hypertrophy; training to momentary failure, equipment type and complex periodization did not consistently change outcomes for average healthy adults. No sex-specific load advice appeared in summaries. [Strong] — [ACSM pronouncement slide deck](https://acsm.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Pronouncement-ppt-deck_resistance-training-ps.pdf); [EurekAlert](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1119943)
- Henselmans (secondhand) recommends higher reps for women, based on fatigue resistance. [Opinion, unverified] — [secondary summary](https://www.adaymag.com/2015/07/03/bayesian-body-building.html)

### Inferences
- Use the same rep ranges as any hypertrophy program. If a woman finds high-rep sets (e.g., 15-30) less punishing than men do, that is a reasonable way to add volume on isolation work, but it is a comfort choice, not a requirement.
- Because reps at %1RM do not differ by sex, RIR/RPE-based loading works the same way for women.

### Gaps
- Full text of Nuzzo 2024 not accessed; exact sex-moderator estimates not confirmed.

## Exercise selection and emphasis: glutes, lower body, upper body, pull-ups, pelvic floor

### Takeaway
Hip thrusts and squats grow the glutes about equally; squats also grow quads and adductors more. Compound lifts give most of the result, with isolation work as optional extra. Upper body is the area where women start furthest behind in absolute terms and improve fastest in relative strength, so it deserves full, not token, volume. Heavy lifting appears safe for the pelvic floor of healthy young women without children in the short term; leakage is common among competitive lifters and is treated with pelvic floor training, not by stopping lifting. [Moderate for glute data; Weak for pelvic floor]

### Cited Findings
- Plotkin et al. 2023 (Frontiers in Physiology; untrained college-aged adults, hip thrust n = 18 vs back squat n = 16, 9 weeks, volume-equated, MRI): similar gluteal hypertrophy (hip thrust had a small non-significant edge at some sites); squat produced more quadriceps and adductor growth; each group got stronger at its own lift; deadlift transfer similar. Higher glute EMG in hip thrust did not predict more growth. Mixed-sex sample. [Moderate] — [PubMed 37877099](https://www.pubmed.gov/37877099/); [Frontiers full text](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2023.1279170/full)
- Barbalho et al. 2018 (Sports; trained women): adding single-joint exercises to a multi-joint program gave no additional strength benefit; arm circumference changes were similar (1.47% vs 1.58%). [Weak-Moderate] — [DOAJ record](https://doaj.org/article/b3d05d8d876e422aa77c37ec932dd616)
- Barbalho et al. 2020 (Int J Sports Med 41(5):306-310; trained women): multi-joint and multi-joint plus single-joint groups had larger increases in gluteus maximus, quadriceps and pectoralis major than a single-joint-only group. [Weak-Moderate] — [Thieme](https://thieme-connect.de/products/ejournals/html/10.1055/a-1082-1126)
- Caution: Barbalho et al. 2020 in JSCR (untrained young women, single-joint added to multi-joint) is listed as RETRACTED; do not cite it. Several Barbalho papers have been questioned. [Source integrity flag] — [library record showing retraction](https://bvbvf1.bib-bvb.de/vufind/PrimoRecord/cdi_proquest_miscellaneous_2042234000); [Solent abstract](https://pure.solent.ac.uk/en/publications/the-influence-of-adding-single-joint-exercise-to-a-multi-joint-re/)
- Upper body: absolute upper-body hypertrophy favours men (Refalo 2025), and relative upper-body strength gains favour women (Roberts 2020, ES = -0.60). [Moderate] — [PeerJ 19042](https://peerj.com/articles/19042); [PubMed 32218059](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32218059/)
- Pelvic floor, prevalence: Skaug et al. 2022 (JSCR 36(10):2800-07) surveyed Norwegian national-level female powerlifters and Olympic weightlifters. Reported urinary incontinence prevalence differs by source (50.0% in the library catalogue abstract; 41.7% of 180 respondents in press coverage); the catalogue also lists 80% anal incontinence and 23.3% pelvic organ prolapse symptoms. [Moderate that leakage is common in competitive lifters; figures need checking] — [British Library record](https://eld.bl.uk/catalog/100167694155_0x000001); [press coverage](https://uk.style.yahoo.com/wee-yourself-just-rock-incontinence-100011945.html)
- Pelvic floor, acute safety: Skaug, Engh & Bø crossover study in healthy, nulliparous, strength-trained women found no immediate effect on pelvic floor muscle function after heavy squats and deadlifts at 75-85% 1RM versus rest; authors call heavy lifting "well tolerated by the PFM in short-term" and say long-term effects and loads above 85% need study. [Weak: acute, small] — [ICS 2023 abstract](https://www.ics.org/2023/abstract/282)
- Bø and colleagues 2024 pilot in nulliparous power- and weightlifters with stress incontinence: cites international consensus that pelvic floor muscle training is first-line treatment and has no side effects; only one athlete improved in the pilot. [Weak for efficacy in lifters; Strong that PFMT is first-line in general] — [Int Urogynecol J 2024](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s00192-024-05801-8)

### Inferences
- For a 22-year-old focused on glutes and legs, pairing a squat or lunge pattern with a hip thrust or bridge covers glutes and quads; picking based on preference and joint comfort is fine.
- Pull-ups: no specific trial was found, but the upper-body findings support progressive vertical-pull work (assisted pull-ups, negatives, lat pulldown) as a reasonable emphasis. [Inference]
- Pelvic floor: leakage during heavy lifts is a symptom to address (pelvic-health physio, PFMT, bracing and breathing technique), not a reason to avoid heavy training in a healthy nulliparous woman.

### Gaps
- No trial found specific to pull-up programming in women.
- No long-term data on heavy lifting and pelvic floor outcomes in young women; Skaug 2022 exact prevalence figures unconfirmed.
- Sohee Lee and Meg Squats/Stronger U published positions could not be found in this pass.

## Hormonal contraception and the menstrual cycle in program design

### Takeaway
Leading women's-physiology researchers (Elliott-Sale, Colenso-Semple, Phillips, McNulty) agree that phase-based "cycle syncing" is not supported: phase effects on performance are trivial, and cycle phase does not change muscle protein synthesis or long-term gains. Oral contraceptive use does not change hypertrophy or strength gains. The defensible approach is individual: track the cycle and symptoms, and adjust a session when symptoms are bad. [Moderate]

### Cited Findings
- McNulty, Elliott-Sale et al. 2020 (Sports Medicine 50(10):1813-27; 73 studies, Bayesian and network meta-analysis): performance may be trivially lower in the early follicular phase (ES -0.06, 95% CrI -0.16 to 0.04); evidence quality low; authors recommend a personalised approach based on each woman's own response rather than general phase-based prescriptions. [Moderate] — [RGU repository](https://rgu-repository.worktribe.com/output/940959); [Northumbria record](https://nrl.northumbria.ac.uk/id/eprint/43759)
- Colenso-Semple et al. 2023 umbrella review (with Elliott-Sale and Phillips): phase does not meaningfully affect strength or resistance-training adaptations; commonly cited pro-phase-training studies are "seriously limited" and should be read cautiously; literature has poor and inconsistent cycle verification. [Moderate] — [PMC10076834](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10076834); [MMU PDF](https://e-space.mmu.ac.uk/631857/1/Colenso-Semple%20et%20al%20no%20MC%20phase%20effects.pdf)
- Colenso-Semple et al. 2025 (J Physiol 603(5):1109-21; 12 women, late follicular vs mid-luteal, hormonally verified, stable isotopes): resistance exercise raised muscle protein synthesis, but cycle phase did not change MPS or myofibrillar breakdown; no anabolic advantage to training in a particular phase. [Moderate: well-controlled but small, acute] — [McMaster record](https://experts.mcmaster.ca/scholarly-works/3492640); [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2025/03/250303141152.htm)
- Elliott-Sale et al. 2020 (Sports Medicine; 42 studies, 590 participants): OC users may perform slightly worse than naturally cycling women, but the group effect is likely trivial; performance stable across the pill cycle; 83% of studies moderate to very low quality; no blanket advice for or against the pill; individualise. [Moderate] — [NTU repository](https://irep.ntu.ac.uk/id/eprint/41198)
- Nolan, McNulty, Manninen & Egan 2023 (Sports Medicine; 8 studies, 325 participants, all oral pills): OC use had no significant effect on hypertrophy (0.01, 95% CI -0.11 to 0.13), strength (0.10, -0.08 to 0.28) or power (-0.04). Findings do not cover implants, IUDs or injections. [Moderate for pills; no data for other methods] — [PMC10798924](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10798924/)
- Dr Lauren Colenso-Semple (interviews): women do not need an entirely different rulebook. [Opinion from a researcher in the field] — [The Proof interview](https://theproof.com/science-based-exercise-tips-for-women-dr-lauren-colenso-semple/)
- Dr Emma Ross (The Well HQ, former English Institute of Sport head of physiology): advocates tracking the cycle and understanding symptoms to build strategies to manage them; says exercise is a powerful way to manage symptoms. No primary statement found endorsing or rejecting phase-based periodisation. [Opinion] — [Health Club Management profile](https://www.healthclubmanagement.co.uk/health-club-management-features/HCM-People-Dr-Emma-Ross-Baz-Moffat-Dr-Bella-Smith-Founders-The-Well/35042)

### Inferences
- Program the same plan all month. On days with bad cramps, fatigue or heavy bleeding, use the existing autoregulation tools (lower RPE target, drop a set, swap exercises). This needs no phase-specific rules.
- Pill use is not a reason to change the program.
- Losing periods while training and dieting is a health warning sign (see energy section), not something to train around.

### Gaps
- No resistance-training data for hormonal IUDs, implants, injections or rings.
- Emma Ross's exact written guidance (The Well HQ site, *The Female Body Bible*) could not be retrieved.

## Energy, recovery and health: energy availability, iron, sleep, eating enough, protein, creatine

### Takeaway
The most solid women-specific health priority is avoiding low energy availability (REDs). After that come protein at about 1.6 g/kg/day (range 1.4-2.2), iron screening because iron deficiency is common in young active women, protecting sleep, and creatine as a safe, evidence-based option. These are long-established and are not trends. Several popular "female-specific" nutrition rules come from menopause research. [Strong for LEA and protein; Moderate for iron and creatine; Weak for sleep magnitude]

### Cited Findings
- IOC 2023 REDs consensus (Mountjoy et al., BJSM 57:1073-97): low energy availability is a spectrum from adaptable to problematic; prolonged or severe problematic LEA leads to REDs; low carbohydrate availability adds to the risk. The 2023 statement no longer uses an energy-availability number as a diagnostic criterion. [Strong] — [PubMed 37752011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37752011/); [Areta-group commentary](https://mysportscience.com/post/can-we-measure-low-energy-availability)
- The often-quoted 30 kcal/kg fat-free mass/day cutoff came from short lab studies in non-athletic women; its use as a threshold is widely debated. [Moderate as context] — [mysportscience](https://mysportscience.com/post/can-we-measure-low-energy-availability); [Vidal summary](https://www.vidal.fr/actualites/30846-deficit-energetique-amateurs-ou-elites-de-nombreux-sportifs-a-risque.html)
- ISSN 2023 position stand, Nutritional Concerns of the Female Athlete (Sims, Kerksick, Smith-Ryan and others): the primary consideration is adequate energy intake and energy availability, with attention to meal timing around exercise; protein in the mid-to-upper range of 1.4-2.2 g/kg/day for women at all stages of menstrual function; caffeine, iron and creatine have the most evidence for women; sex-specific supplement evidence is limited. [Strong for energy and protein ranges; position stand] — [PubMed 37221858](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37221858/)
- The same ISSN stand's advice of a ~10 g essential-amino-acid protein bolus around exercise to overcome anabolic resistance is for peri- and post-menopausal athletes. **[MENOPAUSE-DERIVED: not needed for a 22-year-old]** — [PubMed 37221858](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37221858/)
- Morton et al. 2018 (BJSM 52:376-84; 49 studies, 1,863 people): protein beyond ~1.62 g/kg/day gave no further gains in fat-free mass with resistance training; the lead author's release says supplementation works equally in women. Stronger by Science notes the breakpoint is uncertain and probably somewhat higher; secondary sources cite an upper confidence bound near 2.2 g/kg. [Strong for ~1.6 g/kg as a floor-target] — [BJSM abstract](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/52/6/376.abstract); [McMaster release](https://science.mcmaster.ca/protein-supplements-work-to-a-point/); [Stronger by Science critique](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/?p=61316)
- Creatine: Smith-Ryan, Cabre, Eckerson & Candow 2021 (Nutrients 13(3):877, narrative review) say women are understudied, describe possible benefits across menstrual, pregnancy, postpartum and menopausal stages, and cite a systematic review finding no adverse gastrointestinal, kidney, liver or heart effects in women. The review reports women's endogenous creatine stores as 70-80% lower than men's (likely reflecting total muscle mass; interpret cautiously). [Moderate for safety; Weak-Moderate for women-specific benefit] — [PMC7998865](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7998865/)
- The high-dose creatine finding (0.3 g/kg/day) with muscle and bone benefits in that review is from post-menopausal women. **[MENOPAUSE-DERIVED]** — [PMC7998865](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7998865/)
- Iron: in 2,749 NCAA Division I athletes, 30.9% of women had iron deficiency (ferritin <20 ng/mL) and 2.2% iron-deficiency anaemia. [Moderate] — [University of Washington poster](https://nutr.uw.edu/wp-content/uploads/2019/10/Finan.MNTPoster_MA.pdf)
- A college team physician's statement notes symptoms can start when ferritin falls below about 30 ng/mL, even before anaemia, and aims to keep athletes above ~40 ng/mL; cutoffs vary between sources. [Weak / expert opinion] — [Carleton team physician statement](https://athletics.carleton.edu/sports/2019/7/17/team-physician-statement-on-iron-status-screening.aspx?id=6017)
- Periodic-screening guidance for elite athletes supports considering iron screening in women because low iron status is common. [Moderate] — [Bahr et al. 2019, J Sci Med Sport](https://nih.brage.unit.no/nih-xmlui/bitstream/handle/11250/2833269/Bahr+JSciMedSport+2019.pdf?sequence=1)
- Sleep: Lamon et al. 2021 (Physiological Reports; 13 young adults, 7 men and 6 women, crossover): one night of total sleep deprivation cut muscle protein synthesis by 18%, raised cortisol 21% and lowered testosterone (22-24%); sex differences were not resolved. [Weak: small, acute, total deprivation] — [PMC7785053](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7785053/)

### Inferences
- For a 22-year-old building lean muscle: eat at maintenance or a small surplus, hit about 1.6 g/kg/day protein (up to ~2.2 is harmless and covers uncertainty), and avoid long aggressive cuts. Missed or irregular periods during training should prompt a check on energy intake.
- Iron: a ferritin check is a sensible, low-cost step if there is fatigue, heavy periods, or a plant-based diet.
- Creatine monohydrate at a standard daily dose is a safe, long-used option; the women-specific benefit claims for young women are less established than for men or older women.

### Gaps
- No source in this pass gave a women-specific calorie surplus for muscle gain; the "maintenance or small surplus" advice is inference.
- Standard creatine dose and sleep-duration targets were not verified in this pass.
- Could not confirm the IOC's exact iron-screening recommendation wording.

## Long-established consensus documents: what do they say about young women specifically?

### Takeaway
The major consensus documents (ACSM 2009 and 2026, WHO 2020) give one set of resistance-training recommendations for healthy adults, without separate prescriptions for women. Women-specific consensus content sits in nutrition and health documents (ISSN 2023 female athlete, IOC REDs 2023), not in training-variable guidance. [Strong]

### Cited Findings
- ACSM 2009 position stand, Progression Models in Resistance Training for Healthy Adults (Med Sci Sports Exerc 41(3):687-708): for hypertrophy, moderate loads, multiple-set higher-volume programs, and roughly 1-2 min rest; secondary summaries disagree (one says 2-3 min). Recommendations are for healthy adults in general. [Strong as long-running consensus; rest wording unverified] — [PubMed 19204579](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19204579/); [full text via SFU](https://sfu.ca/~ryand/kin343/ACSMresistance.pdf); [IDEA Fit summary](https://www.ideafit.com/progression-models-in-resistance-training-for-healthy-adults)
- ACSM 2026 position stand (Resistance Training Prescription for Muscle Function, Hypertrophy, and Physical Performance in Healthy Adults: An Overview of Reviews; first update in 17 years, developed with McMaster): any resistance training helps; at least 2x/week across all major muscle groups; ~10 sets/muscle/week for hypertrophy; ≥80% 1RM for strength; failure, machines vs free weights and complex periodisation did not consistently matter for average adults; individualise for adherence. No sex-specific prescription found in the summaries. [Strong] — [EurekAlert](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1119943); [ACSM slide deck](https://acsm.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Pronouncement-ppt-deck_resistance-training-ps.pdf); [2 Minute Medicine](https://www.2minutemedicine.com/landmark-acsm-mcmaster-guidelines-simplify-resistance-training-for-longevity/)
- WHO 2020 Guidelines on Physical Activity and Sedentary Behaviour (Bull et al., BJSM): recommend regular muscle-strengthening for all age groups. The adult wording (moderate or greater intensity, all major muscle groups, 2 or more days a week) is from the guideline as widely reported; the search snippet did not display the adult line, so verify. [Strong] — [UEA research portal record](https://research-portal.uea.ac.uk/en/publications/world-health-organization-2020-guidelines-on-physical-activity-an/)
- ISSN 2023 and IOC 2023 (above) are the main consensus documents with women-specific content, and that content is about energy, protein, iron and supplements. [Strong] — [PubMed 37221858](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37221858/); [PubMed 37752011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37752011/)

### Inferences
- The absence of sex-specific training variables in ACSM 2009 and 2026, with 17 years of research between them, is itself evidence that the field has not found differences large enough to write into guidance.

### Gaps
- UK Chief Medical Officers' 2019 guidelines (understood to recommend strengthening on at least 2 days a week) were not verified in this pass.
- No current NSCA position statement specific to training women was found; NSCA's older women-specific statements were not retrieved.
- Full text of ACSM 2026 not accessed; could not confirm whether it discusses sex as a moderator.

## Credible coaches and researchers focused on young / premenopausal women: positions, evidence quality, agreement

### Takeaway
Researchers who study premenopausal women (Elliott-Sale, Colenso-Semple, Smith-Ryan, Nuckols, Refalo) converge on "same principles, small tweaks, individualise for symptoms and health". Coaches (Israetel/RP, Contreras, Henselmans, Nippard) add opinions that women can use more volume, more frequency, shorter rest and higher reps; these rest mainly on acute fatigue data. The main disagreement is how big the tweaks should be, not whether women need a different system.

### Cited Findings
- **Dr Kirsty Elliott-Sale** (Manchester Metropolitan; menstrual cycle and OC meta-analyses): phase and OC effects on performance are trivial at group level; recommends individualised approaches over blanket rules. Evidence quality: Moderate (meta-analyses on low-quality primary data). — [McNulty 2020](https://rgu-repository.worktribe.com/output/940959); [Elliott-Sale 2020](https://irep.ntu.ac.uk/id/eprint/41198)
- **Dr Lauren Colenso-Semple** (McMaster; with Stuart Phillips): no phase-based training needed; cycle phase does not change MPS; women do not need a different rulebook. Evidence quality: Moderate (umbrella review plus controlled MPS study). — [Umbrella review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10076834); [J Physiol 2025](https://experts.mcmaster.ca/scholarly-works/3492640); [interview](https://theproof.com/science-based-exercise-tips-for-women-dr-lauren-colenso-semple/)
- **Dr Abbie Smith-Ryan** (UNC; ISSN co-author): creatine is safe and useful for women across the lifespan; adequate energy and protein are priorities. Evidence quality: Moderate for safety; much of the women-specific benefit evidence is in older women. — [Smith-Ryan 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7998865/); [ISSN 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37221858/)
- **Dr Emma Ross** (The Well HQ): track cycle and symptoms, build strategies to manage symptoms, use exercise to help symptoms. Evidence quality: Opinion / applied practice. — [Health Club Management](https://www.healthclubmanagement.co.uk/health-club-management-features/HCM-People-Dr-Emma-Ross-Baz-Moffat-Dr-Bella-Smith-Founders-The-Well/35042)
- **Greg Nuckols** (Stronger by Science; co-author Roberts 2020, Refalo 2025, Amdi 2025, Nuckols 2026): his research shows similar relative hypertrophy, larger relative upper-body strength gains in women, and smaller fatigue differences in trained lifters. Evidence quality: Strong-Moderate (his published meta-analyses). — [Roberts 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32218059/); [Refalo 2025](https://peerj.com/articles/19042); [Amdi 2025](https://thieme-connect.de/products/ejournals/abstract/10.1055/a-2563-9449)
- **Bret Contreras**: train each muscle 2-3x/week; women often prefer 3 lower + 2 upper days; glutes can be trained frequently; women recover slightly faster. Evidence quality: Opinion; his co-authored Plotkin 2023 trial found hip thrust and squat grew glutes similarly. — [Huberman Lab](https://www.hubermanlab.com/episode/build-ideal-physique-bret-contreras); [Plotkin 2023](https://www.pubmed.gov/37877099/)
- **Dr Mike Israetel / RP**: women tolerate more per-session volume and recover faster, so can train muscles more often; self-described as largely theoretical. Evidence quality: Opinion. — [BoxRox](https://www.boxrox.com/?p=192197)
- **Menno Henselmans**: (secondhand) women should use higher reps, more volume, possibly more dietary fat, less explosive work. Evidence quality: Opinion, primary text not retrieved. — [interview page](https://mennohenselmans.com/gender-differences-jeff-nippard/); [secondary summary](https://www.adaymag.com/2015/07/03/bayesian-body-building.html)
- **Jeff Nippard**: differences are slight tweaks; mostly explained by muscle mass and body fat; same relative growth potential; sells an 8-week, 6-day Women's Specialization Program (structure per third-party listing). Evidence quality: Opinion informed by literature review. — [Nippard video](https://rosetta.to/u/jeffnippard/men-vs-women-sex-differences-in-training-science-explained-12-studies); [program page](https://jeffnippard.com/products/womens-specialization-program)
- **Layne Norton / Biolayne** (not on the original list; surfaced in search): when volume is equated, relative hypertrophy is similar, so fully separate programming is not warranted. Evidence quality: Opinion summarising trials. — [Biolayne video summary](https://www.pulse.bot/fitness/video/women-should-train-completely-differently-than-men-educational-video-biolayne-84c88c1e-30af-4cfa-9e91-1d89a0e8c076/)

### Inferences
- Agreement: same core principles; no cycle syncing; OC not a reason to change training; relative growth the same; individualise.
- Disagreement or uncertainty: how much extra volume or frequency women "should" do (coaches yes, researchers say unproven); rest (acute data supports shorter, hypertrophy data says not below ~60 s); higher reps (Henselmans yes; Nuzzo finds no sex difference in reps at %1RM).

### Gaps
- Sohee Lee and Meg Squats / Stronger U: no primary statements found in this pass.
- Henselmans' and Nuckols' own long-form articles on women could not be fetched.

## Synthesis: defensible women-specific adjustments, and popular claims that are not supported

### Takeaway
For a healthy 22-year-old woman, use a standard evidence-based hypertrophy template and apply a small number of tweaks. The strongest women-specific items concern health and nutrition (energy availability, protein, iron), not sets and reps.

### Cited Findings
Defensible adjustments (each graded):
1. Keep the core template the same: all major muscles at least 2x/week, about 10+ hard sets per muscle per week, a broad load range taken close to failure, progressive overload. **[Strong]** — [ACSM 2026](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1119943); [Refalo 2025](https://peerj.com/articles/19042)
2. Do not periodise by cycle phase. Track the cycle and symptoms, and on bad-symptom days use normal autoregulation (lower RPE, fewer sets, swap exercises). **[Moderate]** that phase-based planning adds nothing; **[Opinion]** for the symptom-day method, which is low risk. — [Colenso-Semple 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10076834); [McNulty 2020](https://rgu-repository.worktribe.com/output/940959); [Colenso-Semple 2025](https://experts.mcmaster.ca/scholarly-works/3492640)
3. No programming change for oral contraceptive use. **[Moderate]** (pills only) — [Nolan 2023](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10798924/)
4. Rest by readiness; women may use the lower end of normal ranges (~60-90 s isolation, ~2 min heavy compounds) if reps hold, but not below ~60 s for hypertrophy. **[Weak]** — [Ratamess 2012](https://stars.library.ucf.edu/scopus2010/4301); [Singer 2024](https://frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2024.1429789/abstract)
5. Volume: start in the standard range; progress toward the higher end only if recovery and performance allow. **[Weak / Opinion]** — [Israetel via BoxRox](https://www.boxrox.com/?p=192197); [Amdi 2025](https://thieme-connect.de/products/ejournals/abstract/10.1055/a-2563-9449)
6. Give the upper body full direct volume (horizontal and vertical pressing and pulling, with a pull-up progression), because women start with less absolute upper-body muscle and show larger relative upper-body strength gains. **[Moderate]** for the data; **[Weak]** for the "emphasise" recommendation. — [Roberts 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32218059/); [Refalo 2025](https://peerj.com/articles/19042)
7. Glutes and legs: base on compound squat/lunge/hinge patterns plus a hip thrust or bridge if preferred; both grow glutes similarly; squats add quads and adductors. **[Moderate]** — [Plotkin 2023](https://www.pubmed.gov/37877099/)
8. Heavy lifting is appropriate for healthy nulliparous women; if leakage occurs, refer to pelvic-floor muscle training and a pelvic-health physio rather than avoiding load. **[Weak]** for safety (short-term data); **[Strong]** that PFMT is first-line for stress incontinence. — [Skaug/Engh/Bø ICS 2023](https://www.ics.org/2023/abstract/282); [Bø 2024](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s00192-024-05801-8)
9. Eat enough: avoid chronic low energy availability; treat missed periods as a red flag. **[Strong]** — [IOC REDs 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37752011/); [ISSN 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37221858/)
10. Protein about 1.6 g/kg/day (range 1.4-2.2). **[Strong]** — [Morton 2018](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/52/6/376.abstract); [ISSN 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37221858/)
11. Consider a ferritin check (fatigue, heavy periods, plant-based diet). **[Moderate]** — [NCAA iron data](https://nutr.uw.edu/wp-content/uploads/2019/10/Finan.MNTPoster_MA.pdf)
12. Creatine monohydrate is a safe, optional addition. **[Moderate]** for safety; women-specific benefit in young women **[Weak-Moderate]**. — [Smith-Ryan 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7998865/)
13. Protect sleep (acute deprivation lowers muscle protein synthesis). **[Weak]** for magnitude — [Lamon 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7785053/)

Popular claims that are NOT supported:
- "Train hard in the follicular phase, back off in the luteal phase" (cycle syncing). Contradicted by meta-analyses and the 2025 MPS study. **[Moderate against]** — [Colenso-Semple 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10076834); [Colenso-Semple 2025](https://experts.mcmaster.ca/scholarly-works/3492640)
- "The pill blunts muscle gains." Not supported for oral pills. **[Moderate against]** — [Nolan 2023](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10798924/)
- "Women build muscle much more slowly than men / can't build much muscle." Relative gains are the same. **[Strong against]** — [Refalo 2025](https://peerj.com/articles/19042)
- "Women need a different %1RM-to-reps table or a different rep range." Sex has little effect on reps at %1RM. **[Moderate against]** — [Nuzzo 2024](https://pure.solent.ac.uk/en/publications/maximal-number-of-repetitions-at-percentages-of-the-one-repetitio/)
- "Women need much more volume (e.g., double) than men." No trial support; based on acute fatigue extrapolation. **[Unsupported; Opinion only]** — [BoxRox on Israetel](https://www.boxrox.com/?p=192197)
- Fixed rest rules such as "45-60 s for women vs 90-120 s for men." Not traceable to trials; hypertrophy meta-analysis favours >60 s. **[Unsupported]** — [Singer 2024](https://frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2024.1429789/abstract)
- "Glutes need 3-5 sessions a week." No evidence that very high glute frequency is better. **[Opinion only]** — [BoxRox on Contreras](https://www.boxrox.com/?p=176424)
- "Heavy lifting will damage a young woman's pelvic floor." Not supported short-term in healthy nulliparous women, though leakage is common in competitive lifters. **[Weak; mixed]** — [ICS 2023](https://www.ics.org/2023/abstract/282); [Skaug 2022](https://eld.bl.uk/catalog/100167694155_0x000001)
- Menopause-derived rules applied to 22-year-olds: EAA/protein bolus to beat "anabolic resistance", high-dose (0.3 g/kg/day) creatine for muscle and bone. **[MENOPAUSE-DERIVED; not shown for young women]** — [ISSN 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37221858/); [Smith-Ryan 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7998865/)
- "Isolation work is essential for women's shape." In trained women, adding single-joint work to compound training added little; one supporting JSCR paper is retracted. **[Weak against]** — [Barbalho 2018](https://doaj.org/article/b3d05d8d876e422aa77c37ec932dd616); [retraction record](https://bvbvf1.bib-bvb.de/vufind/PrimoRecord/cdi_proquest_miscellaneous_2042234000)

### Inferences
- The defensible list is short on training variables and long on health basics. That matches the consensus documents, which give women-specific guidance only for energy, protein, iron and supplements.
- The coach-driven tweaks (more volume, shorter rest, higher reps) are low-risk if applied through autoregulation, so they can be offered as options rather than rules.

### Gaps
- No RCT tests a "women-adapted" hypertrophy template against a standard one in young women.
- Full texts for several key papers (ACSM 2026, Singer 2024, Nuzzo 2024, Nuckols 2026, Colenso-Semple 2023) were not accessible in this session; numbers come from abstracts and summaries.
- Sohee Lee and Meg Squats/Stronger U positions not located.
