# Training to Failure and Proximity to Failure for a 22-Year-Old Woman Building Lean Muscle

Researched 1 November 2026. Evidence grades used throughout: **[Strong]** (several meta-analyses or consistent RCTs), **[Moderate]** (one meta-analysis or a few consistent RCTs, small samples), **[Weak]** (single small study, secondary report or indirect evidence), **[Opinion]** (expert or coach opinion, or my own reasoning). "M-only" marks findings that come from men only.

Method note for the report writer: this session's network blocked full-text access to PMC, PubMed, Springer, SportRxiv and Stronger By Science. Every number below comes from abstracts, repository records or search-result summaries of them. I could not read results tables, so I did not get the per-RIR slope coefficients from Robinson 2024 (see Gaps). Where a number came only from a secondary source (blog, press release), I say so.

## 1. Dose-response: how does proximity to failure affect hypertrophy and strength, and how much is lost at 1, 2, 3, 4+ reps in reserve?

### Takeaway
Strength gains barely depend on how close to failure you train. Hypertrophy improves as sets end closer to failure, but the gain from going all the way to failure (0 RIR) rather than stopping at 1–2 RIR is trivial to small. The meaningful losses come from stopping far from failure (roughly 4+ RIR), especially with light loads. So "don't train to failure" is too blunt. The accurate version is "train close to failure on almost every working set; actual failure is optional, adds a little at most, and is best kept to safe exercises."

### Cited Findings
**Meta-analyses**
- **Robinson et al. 2024, Sports Medicine 54(9):2209–2231** (series of meta-regressions; RIR estimated for each study's protocol). Conclusion: "Strength gains were similar across a wide range of RIR, while muscle hypertrophy improves as sets are terminated closer to failure." Only hypertrophy was meaningfully affected by RIR. **[Moderate]** for hypertrophy, **[Strong]** for strength (agrees with the other meta-analyses below). — [Springer abstract](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-024-02069-2); [SportRxiv preprint](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/295)
- In the Robinson preprint, the best-fitting model for hypertrophy was **linear** (multi-level meta-regression on standardized mean change). A second analysis on response ratios also showed hypertrophy improving closer to failure. The authors note the overall model fit was modest, and that "considering the RIR estimation procedures used... the exact relationship between RIR and muscle hypertrophy and strength remains unclear." **[Moderate]** — [SportRxiv preprint PDF](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/download/295/699/654); [Semantic Scholar record](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Exploring-the-Dose%E2%80%93Response-Relationship-Between-to-Robinson-Pelland/21e9295d1d3c1f07fe73d9d9796410ecae4a508f)
- The university press release on Robinson 2024 says strength is similar "whether you stop far from failure or very close to it," while muscle size benefits from training closer to failure. It links stopping 0–5 reps short of failure to good hypertrophy while limiting injury risk. This is a press summary, not the paper. **[Weak]** — [MedicalXpress](https://medicalxpress.com/news/2024-07-bigger-muscles-failure-strength.html); [EurekAlert](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1053119)
- One secondary source reports that Robinson covered 55 hypertrophy and 67 strength studies. I could not confirm these counts. — [Search summary citing GetFitCraft](https://getfitcraft.com/science/training-to-failure-vs-reps-in-reserve)
- **Refalo et al. 2023, Sports Medicine 53(3):649–665** (15 studies). A "trivial advantage" for set failure over non-failure for hypertrophy: **ES = 0.19 (95% CI 0.00–0.37), p = 0.045**, across any definition of failure. Subgroups using strict momentary failure showed no clear advantage. Higher velocity-loss thresholds (closer to failure) "do not always elicit greater muscle hypertrophy," pointing to a possible **non-linear** relationship. **[Moderate]** — [AUT repository](https://openrepository.aut.ac.nz/items/78679612-6ff4-4d00-95d1-7920d072a760); [PMC full text](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9935748/)
- A letter to the editor (Fonseca et al. 2023) questioned some sample sizes used in Refalo 2023's effect-size calculations and urged caution in generalizing. — [AUT repository record / search summary](https://openrepository.aut.ac.nz/items/78679612-6ff4-4d00-95d1-7920d072a760)
- **Grgic et al. 2022, Journal of Sport and Health Science** (15 studies, all young adults). No significant difference between failure and non-failure for strength (**ES = −0.09; 95% CI −0.22 to 0.05**) or hypertrophy (**ES = 0.22; 95% CI −0.11 to 0.55**). A secondary summary reports a significant hypertrophy advantage for failure in the *trained* subgroup (**ES = 0.15; 95% CI 0.03–0.26**), but that subgroup rested on only about 2 studies. **[Moderate]** (trained subgroup **[Weak]**). — [DOAJ record](https://doaj.org/article/dc36ba7ccd994798964f66b93bd8f662); [Sci-Sport summary](https://sci-sport.com/en/training-to-failure-or-not-impact-on-hypertrophy-and-strength/)
- **ACSM 2026 Position Stand** (overview of 137 systematic reviews, >30,000 participants). Training to momentary failure "did not consistently impact training outcomes"; hypertrophy was enhanced by higher volume (≥10 sets/week) and eccentric overload. **[Strong]** (carried over from the sibling notes) — [Newswise/ACSM release](https://www.newswise.com/articles/acsm-unveils-landmark-2026-resistance-training-guidelines-first-update-in-17-years)

**Key trials (0 RIR vs 1–2 RIR)**
- **Refalo et al. 2024, J Sports Sci 42(1):85–101.** Resistance-trained adults (**12 men, 6 women**), within-subject (one leg per condition), 8 weeks, 2 sessions/week, leg press + leg extension, failure vs 2 RIR (leg press) and 1 RIR (leg extension). Quadriceps thickness gain averaged across rectus femoris and vastus lateralis: **FAIL 0.181 cm (HDI 0.119–0.243) vs RIR 0.182 cm (HDI 0.115–0.247)**, i.e. identical. Volume was similar. Failure consistently caused more acute neuromuscular fatigue. **[Moderate]** — [PubMed 38393985](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38393985/); [Taylor & Francis full text](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/02640414.2024.2321021)
- **Hermann et al. 2025, Med Sci Sports Exerc ("Without Fail")**. 42 resistance-trained men **and women**, randomized: one set to failure vs one set at 2 RIR, 9 exercises, 2×/week, 8 weeks. Several hypertrophy measures "tended to favor FAIL" but "absolute differences between conditions were generally modest." Strength and local muscular endurance gains were similar. No sex breakdown reported in what I could access. **[Moderate]** — [PubMed 40249908](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40249908/); [SportRxiv preprint](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/484); [Biolayne summary](https://biolayne.com/reps/issue-37/almost-there-does-2-rir-match-failure-for-muscle-and-strength-gains-in-single-set-training/)
- **Santanielo et al. 2020, Biology of Sport** (trained individuals, within-subject). Failure and non-failure "similarly effective in promoting increases in muscle mass, pennation angle, fascicle length, strength and activation." A blog claim that non-failure grew ~40% more muscle could not be verified. **[Moderate]** (the abstract I retrieved did not state the participants' sex) — [PubMed 33343066](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33343066/)
- **Nóbrega et al. 2018, JSCR.** High- and low-intensity training to "volitional interruption" (stopping when the person chose to, before failure) was "equally effective" for muscle mass, strength and pennation angle as training to failure. **[Moderate]** (participants' sex not stated in the retrieved text) — [ResearchGate record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/311788235_Effect_Of_Resistance_Training_To_Muscle_Failure_Versus_Volitional_Interruption_At_High-_And_Low-Intensities_On_Muscle_Mass_And_Strength)
- **Carroll et al. 2019, IJSPP 14:46–54** (15 well-trained men, 10 weeks, 3×/week). Repetition-maximum training (sets to failure, n = 8) vs relative-intensity training with sets stopped short (n = 7). Weekly volume load did not differ, but "training strain" was higher in the failure group. The non-failure group improved vertical jump, rate of force development and maximal strength more. A preprint citing the companion paper reports effect sizes favoring non-failure for vastus lateralis fiber hypertrophy: **type I g = 0.27, type II g = 0.99**. **[Weak–Moderate]**, M-only, small n. — [Sacred Heart repository](https://digitalcommons.sacredheart.edu/pthms_exscifac/14); [Henselmans review (opinion)](https://mennohenselmans.com/skeletal-muscle-fiber-adaptations-resistance-training-repetition-maximums-relative-intensity/); [SportRxiv preprint citing Carroll](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/download/343/705/647)

**Velocity-loss studies (proxy for proximity to failure; not volume-matched)**
- **Pareja-Blanco et al. 2017, Scand J Med Sci Sports** (22 young men, squat, 8 weeks). 40% velocity loss (VL40, closer to failure) gave more vastus lateralis and intermedius hypertrophy than VL20. VL20 gave similar squat strength and larger jump gains (**CMJ +9.5% vs +3.5%**) with **40% fewer reps**. Only VL40 lost type IIX fibers. **[Moderate]**, M-only. — [Zendy record](https://zendy.io/title/10.1111/sms.12678); [PDF via paulogentil.com](https://paulogentil.com/pdf/Effects%20of%20velocity%20loss%20during%20resistance%20training%20on%20athletic%20performance,%20strength%20gains%20and%20muscle%20adaptations.pdf)
- **Pareja-Blanco et al. 2020, Scand J Med Sci Sports** (64 resistance-trained young men, bench press, 8 weeks; VL0/15/25/50%). VL50 gained significantly more pectoralis major cross-sectional area than VL0 (p < .05). Strength gains did not differ between groups "despite the wide differences in the total volume." Higher VL "allowed for a greater volume load which maximized muscle hypertrophy." **[Moderate]**, M-only. — [idUS repository](https://idus.us.es/handle/11441/139806); [Zendy record](https://zendy.io/title/10.1111/sms.13775)
- **Rodiles-Guerrero et al. 2022, IJSPP** (n = 50). Only VL50 increased cross-sectional area significantly; VL25 gave the largest 1RM gains. **[Weak]**, M-only per search summary. — [UPO repository](https://rio.upo.es/entities/publication/6a5a4a7c-5d53-4025-a896-6236e97054f9/full)

### Inferences
- **Strength:** proximity to failure barely matters for strength. Several studies (Carroll 2019; Pareja-Blanco 2017) even show non-failure doing as well or better for strength and power, because it leaves her fresher. **[Strong]**
- **Hypertrophy at 0 vs 1–2 RIR:** essentially equal (Refalo 2024: 0.181 vs 0.182 cm), or a small edge for failure (Refalo 2023 ES 0.19; Hermann 2025 "modest"). Failure is worth at most a small bonus. **[Moderate]**
- **At 3 RIR:** probably a small loss per set versus 0–1 RIR. Robinson's linear model implies a gradual, not cliff-like, decline. **[Weak]** (inferred from the model shape without the slope numbers)
- **At 4–5+ RIR:** the loss is likely meaningful, and with light loads such sets may produce little growth (see Q2). Treat sets at ≥4 RIR as not counting fully toward hypertrophy volume. **[Opinion]** based on Robinson and Lasevicius.
- **Linear or not?** Unresolved. Robinson's best fit was linear. Refalo 2023 suggested non-linearity, with a flattening near failure. One practical reading fits both: each rep closer to failure helps a little, but the last rep (1 RIR → 0 RIR) buys little and costs the most fatigue. **[Weak]**
- **Verdict on "you shouldn't train to failure":** partly wrong as a blanket rule. She *should* train close to failure (about 0–3 RIR) on nearly all working sets. True failure is optional, gives at most a small hypertrophy bonus, and fits isolation/machine exercises and light high-rep work, not heavy barbell compounds. **[Moderate]**

### Gaps
- I could not retrieve Robinson 2024's slope coefficients (change in effect size per RIR), credible intervals, or load and training-status moderator results. Springer, PMC and PubMed were blocked in this session. So I cannot give an exact "% lost per rep in reserve." Getting the precise numbers needs the full text (DOI 10.1007/s40279-024-02069-2).
- Vieira et al. 2021 (JSCR 35(4):1165–1175) and Lacerda et al. 2020 (JSCR): I could not retrieve their results.
- No source mapped velocity-loss percentages to RIR for the squat or bench press in what I could access.
- Most trials last 6–10 weeks. No long-term (>6 months) failure vs non-failure trial was found.

## 2. Does it depend on load? Do light loads need to go to failure?

### Takeaway
Yes. With heavy loads (about ≥70–80% 1RM, roughly ≤10–12 reps), stopping a few reps short loses little or nothing. With light loads (about 30% 1RM, 20–30+ reps), sets must go to or very near failure to work. Light loads stopped well short of failure produced no significant growth.

### Cited Findings
- **Lasevicius et al. 2022 (epub 2019), JSCR 36(2):346–351.** 25 untrained **men**, unilateral knee extension, 8 weeks, 3 sets, volume equated across conditions. Quadriceps cross-sectional area: high load (80% 1RM) to failure **+8.1%**, high load non-failure **+7.7%**, low load (30% 1RM) to failure **+7.8%**, low load non-failure **+2.8% (not significant)**. Strength: about **+33–34% with high load vs +16–18% with low load**. The authors write that with low loads, "a high level of effort seems to have greater importance than total training volume," while for high loads, "muscle failure does not promote any additional benefits." **[Moderate]**, M-only, single study. — [PubMed 31895290](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31895290/); [USP repository](https://repositorio.usp.br/item/003078250)
- **Schoenfeld et al. 2017, JSCR** (21 studies, ≤60% vs >60% 1RM, **all sets to momentary failure**, ≥6 weeks). Hypertrophy was similar between conditions; maximal strength favored heavy loads: "muscle hypertrophy can be equally achieved across a spectrum of loading ranges." **[Strong]** — [PubMed 28834797](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28834797/)
- **Nóbrega et al. 2018** found low-intensity training to volitional interruption about as effective as training to failure. This partly conflicts with Lasevicius 2022. One likely reason is that self-chosen stopping points with light loads may still be very close to failure. **[Moderate]**; conflict noted. — [ResearchGate record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/311788235_Effect_Of_Resistance_Training_To_Muscle_Failure_Versus_Volitional_Interruption_At_High-_And_Low-Intensities_On_Muscle_Mass_And_Strength)
- **Halperin et al. 2022**: RIR predictions were more accurate with heavier loads and when the target was ≤12 reps rather than >12. — [PubMed 34542869](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34542869/)
- Between-person variation in reps-to-failure at a given %1RM is larger at lower %1RM (Nuzzo et al. 2024, 269 studies). — [PMC10933212](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10933212) (carried over from sibling notes)

### Inferences
- For light work (bands, very light dumbbells, 20–30+ reps, many bodyweight sets), the rule should be **0–1 RIR (true failure is acceptable and often necessary)**. Light sets are also the hardest to judge by feel (Halperin; Nuzzo), so stopping "a few short" risks being far short. **[Moderate]**
- For moderate-to-heavy loads (about 6–12 reps), **1–3 RIR** keeps essentially all the hypertrophy. **[Moderate]**
- This matters for a woman who might default to light "toning" weights (see Q4). Light weights *can* build muscle, but only if she pushes them to failure. **[Moderate]**

### Gaps
- No load × proximity trial in women was found. Lasevicius 2022 is men only.
- The exact %1RM or rep count below which failure becomes necessary is not established. "≤30–40% 1RM / >20 reps" is an inference from the tested conditions.

## 3. Does it depend on exercise type (free-weight compound vs machine/isolation)?

### Takeaway
No trial directly compares failure on compounds vs isolation for hypertrophy. Expert and coaching consensus, plus the fatigue data, supports keeping heavy free-weight compounds 1–3 RIR and allowing failure mainly on machines, cables and isolation exercises. The injury argument is plausible but I found no injury data.

### Cited Findings
- Refalo 2024 noted that the effect of proximity to failure on muscle-specific hypertrophy "may also depend on other factors (e.g., exercise selection, order, and subsequent musculature targeted)." **[Weak]** — [Taylor & Francis](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/02640414.2024.2321021)
- Hermann 2025: participants estimated RIR more accurately on bench press than on squat. — [PubMed 40249908](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40249908/)
- Remmert et al. 2023 (trained and untrained men and women, single- and multi-joint exercises): accuracy was better closer to failure and in later sets; sex and experience had no significant effect. — [PubMed 37036795](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37036795/)
- Coaching sources: 0–2 RIR for most isolation and machine work and 1–3 RIR for compounds. Constant 0–1 RIR on big compounds "can stall progress due to excessive fatigue, form breakdown, and joint stress." Failure suits "stable, low-risk exercises like machine presses, leg extensions, or cable work." **[Opinion]**, low-quality secondary source. — [Reshape blog](https://reshape-website-hthehqceckbmfpgk.francecentral-01.azurewebsites.net/blog/how-to-use-rir-for-hypertrophy-and-strength)
- RP-style hypertrophy mesocycles (as described by third parties): 4–6 weeks of building, RIR starting around 3 and working down to about 0 by the final week, then a mandatory deload. **[Opinion]** (not from RP's own materials) — [Arvo summary](https://arvo.guru/resources/methods/rp-training); [BodySpec summary](https://www.bodyspec.com/blog/post/renaissance_periodization_principles_and_guide)
- Coaching sources recommend stopping at "technical failure" (form starts to slip) rather than grinding failure on barbell lifts. **[Opinion]** — [RPE calibration tips](https://myrpecalculator.pages.dev/blog/rpe-calibration-tips)
- Failure on squats and bench press also produces the largest acute velocity and jump losses and the slowest recovery (Morán-Navarro 2017; see Q5). — [SpringerMedizin](https://www.springermedizin.de/time-course-of-recovery-following-resistance-training-leading-or/15095268)

### Inferences
- **Heavy free-weight compounds** (barbell squat, deadlift, RDL, bench, overhead press, barbell row): 1–3 RIR, no planned failure. These lifts carry the highest systemic fatigue, the hardest bail-outs and the most technique demand, and the hypertrophy bonus from failure is small. **[Opinion]** backed by fatigue data **[Moderate]**
- **Machine and cable compounds** (leg press, hack squat, chest press, lat pulldown, cable row, Smith machine): 1–2 RIR; final set may go to 0 RIR. **[Opinion]**
- **Isolation** (curls, raises, extensions, leg curls, calf raises, glute kickbacks): 0–1 RIR; failure on the last set is fine. **[Opinion]**, consistent with Refalo 2024, which used leg extension at 1 RIR vs failure with no difference.

### Gaps
- I found **no study measuring injury rates or technique breakdown** when training compounds to failure vs short of failure. The safety rule rests on expert opinion and fatigue physiology, not injury data.
- I could not retrieve primary statements from Helms, Nuckols, Schoenfeld, Israetel or Nippard. Only third-party summaries of RP's approach and a Henselmans review page appeared. Stronger By Science pages were blocked.

## 4. Women specifically

### Takeaway
Women respond to failure vs non-failure training the same way men do in the few trials that included them. A 10-week trial in 89 young women found failure added no overall advantage. Women judge RIR about as accurately as men, but trained women on their own tend to choose loads far too light, so the bigger risk for a 22-year-old woman is *under*-training, not over-training. Women's greater fatigue resistance is real for isometric and slow tasks, but no trial shows they should train closer to failure than men.

### Cited Findings
**Failure trials including women**
- **Martorelli et al. 2017, Eur J Transl Myol 27(2)** — the only women-only failure trial found. **89 active young women**, elbow flexors (biceps curl), 70% 1RM, 2×/week, 10 weeks. Three groups: to failure; not to failure, volume equalized; not to failure. Strength rose similarly in all groups with no significant between-group differences. Peak torque at 180°/s improved in both non-failure groups but not in the failure group. Muscle thickness rose **~17.5%** (failure) and **~8.5%** (volume-equalized non-failure) at 10 weeks, with no significant change in the plain non-failure group. Conclusion: training to failure "does not provide additional strength and muscle hypertrophy gains in young women." **[Moderate]** (one study, one muscle). — [PubMed 28713535](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28713535/); [PAGEPress](https://www.pagepressjournals.org/index.php/bam/article/view/6339)
- Refalo 2024 (6 of 18 participants women) and Hermann 2025 (mixed sex, n = 42) both included women; neither reported a sex-specific analysis in the material I could access. — [Taylor & Francis](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/02640414.2024.2321021); [PubMed 40249908](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40249908/)
- A published 2026 protocol for a within-participant randomized trial in **untrained young women** will compare training to concentric failure with adding eccentric-only reps after concentric failure (10 weeks, strength and hypertrophy). No results yet. — [PMC12828313](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12828313/)

**RIR accuracy and sex**
- **Halperin et al. 2022, Sports Med 52(2):377–390** (scoping review + exploratory meta-analysis). People **under-predicted reps to failure by 0.95 reps on average (95% CI 0.17–1.73)**, with very high heterogeneity (I² = 97.9%). Accuracy improved slightly closer to failure (β = −0.025, 95% CI −0.05 to 0.0014), with heavier loads, in later sets and for targets of ≤12 reps. Training status did not meaningfully change accuracy. No sex analysis was reported in the abstract. **[Moderate]** — [PubMed 34542869](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34542869/); [SportRxiv preprint](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/67)
- **Refalo et al. 2024, JSCR** (bench press, **12 trained women, 12 trained men**): no significant sex or experience effect on intraset RIR accuracy; **mean absolute error 0.65 ± 0.78 reps**. **[Moderate]** — [ResearchGate PDF](https://www.researchgate.net/profile/Martin-Refalo-2/publication/375687905_Accuracy_of_Intraset_Repetitions-in-Reserve_Predictions_During_the_Bench_Press_Exercise_in_Resistance-Trained_Male_and_Female_Subjects/links/65d9591fadc608480ae7db13/Accuracy-of-Intraset-Repetitions-in-Reserve-Predictions-During-the-Bench-Press-Exercise-in-Resistance-Trained-Male-and-Female-Subjects.pdf)
- **Remmert et al. 2023, JSCR** (trained and untrained men and women, machine single- and multi-joint exercises): sex, experience and experience with RIR ratings did not significantly affect accuracy. One exception reported by a search summary: a sex effect on the cable triceps pushdown at 3 RIR with 50% 1RM. **[Moderate]** — [PubMed 37036795](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37036795/)
- A bench press study comparing Smith machine and free weights reported valid RIR estimates in **75% of women vs 100% of men (Smith)** and **75% vs 93.3% (free weight)**, without a significant sex effect. **[Weak]** — [PMC11556642](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11556642/)
- Jukic et al. 2024 (46 trained people, 15 women; velocity-based RIR models): women tended to have slightly larger prediction errors with lighter loads. **[Weak]** — [Physiological Reports](https://physoc.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.14814/phy2.15955)
- Reps-to-failure at a given %1RM is not clearly moderated by sex (Nuzzo et al. 2024, 7,289 people, 269 studies). **[Strong]** — [PMC10933212](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10933212) (carried over from sibling notes)

**Do women under-train?**
- **Ratamess et al. 2008, JSCR 22(1):103–111** (46 resistance-trained women): self-selected intensity averaged **~42% 1RM when training alone vs ~51% with a personal trainer**. **[Moderate]** — [PubMed 18296962](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18296962/)
- **Glass & Stanton 2004** (novices): self-selected intensities of **42–57% 1RM**, similar for men and women. **[Moderate]** — [SPONET record](https://sponet.de/sponet/Record/4008734?lng=en)
- Dias and colleagues: trainer-supervised women selected higher loads (about **49–59.5% 1RM**) than unsupervised women. **[Weak]** (secondary summary) — [ResearchGate PDF](https://www.researchgate.net/profile/Marcelo-Ricardo-Dias/publication/308699625_The_Influence_of_a_Personal_Trainer_on_Self-Selected_Loading_during_Resistance_Exercise/links/59e8f343a6fdccfe7fa8afd2/The-Influence-of-a-Personal-Trainer-on-Self-Selected-Loading-during-Resistance-Exercise.pdf)

**Fatigue resistance and recovery**
- Hunter's review work: women are usually less fatigable than men in isometric tasks, with differences that depend on the task. Less is known for dynamic work; for slow-velocity and low-load dynamic tasks, contractile mechanisms explain the sex difference. **[Moderate]** — [PMC4111134](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4111134); [Senefeld et al. 2018](https://digitalcommons.mtu.edu/kip-fp/90)
- **Judge & Burke 2010, IJSPP 5(2):184–196**: after a heavy, low-rep bench press session, men's estimated 1RM was reduced at 4 h and 24 h, while women showed no strength difference at any recovery time. The authors suggested 48 h of recovery for men and 4 h for women. A blog summary gives the sample as about 20 people. **[Weak]** — [PubMed 20625191](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20625191/); [FGCU record](https://scholarscommons.fgcu.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/The-Effect-of-Recovery-Time-on/99385802658806570)
- A study matching men and women for strength and training experience found different recovery time courses after resistance training that were "not explicable by differences in muscle strength, RT performance, experience, muscle damage or fatigability." **[Weak]** — [PMC6206044](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6206044)
- A quasi-experimental study of trained women and men training to concentric failure found similar effort and discomfort in both sexes, but **only women** showed a drop in mood and a rise in negative affect and fatigue. **[Weak]** — [ScienceDirect abstract](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1360859220301091)

### Inferences
- The same proximity-to-failure rules apply to women as to men. The only women-only trial (Martorelli 2017) agrees with the mixed and male data: failure adds no overall advantage over hard non-failure sets of matched volume, and *low-effort* non-failure training falls behind. **[Moderate]**
- Women don't need to be told to avoid failure for safety or hormonal reasons; no evidence found supports that. The realistic risk for a 22-year-old woman training alone is stopping too far from failure with loads that are too light (Ratamess 2008; Glass & Stanton 2004), plus a general ~1-rep under-prediction of RIR (Halperin 2022). So her "2 RIR" may actually be 3–4 RIR until she calibrates. **[Moderate]**
- Women's fatigue resistance means she may complete more reps than expected at a given load in the last sets, and may recover between sets and sessions somewhat faster. This supports allowing the same or slightly shorter rest on isolation work. It does **not** support a rule that women need more failure sets; no trial tested that. **[Weak]**
- Failure may feel worse psychologically for some women (one quasi-experiment). Keep failure sets few and purposeful for adherence. **[Weak]**

### Gaps
- Apart from Martorelli 2017 (elbow flexors only, ultrasound), I found **no women-only RCT** of failure vs non-failure, and no trial reporting a sex × proximity interaction.
- I could not retrieve results from a 2025 study on RIR accuracy across loads, proximity, sexes and exercises. — [EFSUPIT PDF (not read)](https://efsupit.ro/images/stories/november2025/Art%20262.pdf)
- No study tested whether women need *more reps* (or a different RIR target) to reach equal stimulus. The reps–%1RM data suggest not.
- The menstrual cycle × failure question was not researched here; sibling notes advise against cycle-based periodization.

## 5. Recovery and fatigue costs of failure

### Takeaway
Failure training costs clearly more fatigue and recovery time than stopping 1–2 reps short, for little or no extra growth. It also feels consistently worse. That cost is highest on heavy multi-joint lifts and lowest on small isolation exercises. No health or injury harm was documented, but no study measured it.

### Cited Findings
- **Morán-Navarro et al. 2017, EJAP 117(12):2387–2399** (10 resistance-trained men; bench press and full squat; 3×10 to failure with a 10RM load vs 6×5 and 3×5 with the same load). Failure caused significantly larger acute drops in jump height and bar velocity than the volume-matched 6×5. Non-failure protocols recovered faster, by 24–48 h. Ammonia, growth hormone and creatine kinase showed "a markedly different course of recovery." Conclusion: training to failure "considerably increases the time needed for the recovery of neuromuscular function and metabolic and hormonal homeostasis." **[Moderate]**, M-only, n = 10. — [SpringerMedizin](https://www.springermedizin.de/time-course-of-recovery-following-resistance-training-leading-or/15095268); [Universidad de Murcia record](https://portalinvestigacion.um.es/documentos/5d92fd9b299952412b477a2c)
- Refalo 2024: failure "consistently induces higher levels of acute neuromuscular fatigue" vs 1–2 RIR, for the same hypertrophy. **[Moderate]** — [Taylor & Francis](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/02640414.2024.2321021)
- **Refalo et al. 2025, Eur J Sport Sci** (same 12 men + 6 women cohort): perceived discomfort and perceived exertion were higher in the failure condition throughout. Failure stayed as unpleasant at week 8 as at week 1, while discomfort at 1–2 RIR fell slightly over time. An earlier paper from the group found perceptual responses become more negative closer to failure, which "may influence... adherence." **[Moderate]** — [AUT repository](https://openrepository.aut.ac.nz/items/4dfe7224-1903-4456-ab55-2c13a264560e); [Nutrition Evidence summary](https://nutrition-evidence.com/article/522604/the-effect-of-proximity-to-failure-on-perceptual-responses-to-resistance-training)
- Carroll 2019: "training strain" was higher in the failure group, with poorer strength and power outcomes. **[Weak–Moderate]**, M-only — [Sacred Heart repository](https://digitalcommons.sacredheart.edu/pthms_exscifac/14)
- Pareja-Blanco 2017: VL20 matched VL40's strength gains with 40% fewer reps and better jump gains. **[Moderate]**, M-only — [Zendy](https://zendy.io/title/10.1111/sms.12678)

### Inferences
- Every failure set "spends" recovery that could otherwise go to an extra hard set. Weekly volume (sets at ≤3 RIR) is the stronger hypertrophy lever (ACSM 2026). So a plan that sacrifices sets or frequency to fit failure training is a bad trade. **[Moderate]**
- After a session with failure on a muscle's lower-body compounds, allow about 48 h or more before training that muscle hard again. After failure only on isolation exercises, 48 h is likely enough. **[Opinion]** based on Morán-Navarro's 24–48 h gap.
- Higher discomfort without extra results is a real adherence cost for a non-competitive 22-year-old. **[Moderate]**

### Gaps
- Morán-Navarro is men only, n = 10. I found **no recovery-time-course study of failure vs non-failure in women**. Pareja-Blanco 2020 was not reviewed for recovery, and I found no health or injury data.
- No study measured joint pain or injury incidence when training to failure.

## 6. Advanced techniques that reach failure (drop sets, rest-pause, myo-reps, lengthened partials past failure)

### Takeaway
Drop sets and rest-pause produce about the same hypertrophy as traditional sets, often in less time, but with higher perceived effort. They are time-savers for the last set of isolation or machine exercises, not upgrades. Partials past failure are viable, but the evidence is thin and mostly about calves.

### Cited Findings
- **Sødal et al. 2023, Sports Medicine – Open** (6 studies): no significant difference in hypertrophy between drop sets and traditional sets; drop sets are useful for those with limited training time. (The task brief cited "Coleman 2023"; the 2023 meta-analysis found is by Sødal et al.) **[Moderate]** — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40798-023-00620-5); [PMC10390395](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10390395/)
- A later drop-set meta-analysis: hypertrophy comparable to traditional training in less time, with higher RPE and lactate, and uncertainty about whether that extra acute fatigue matters long-term. **[Moderate]** — [PMC13043944](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13043944/)
- A meta-analysis of advanced training systems in recreationally trained adults: advanced systems "did not collectively enhance muscle hypertrophy beyond traditional multiple-set protocols, although rest-pause training may offer a modest advantage"; drop-set effects were small and not significant. **[Moderate]** — [PMC12922048](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12922048/)
- RCT in resistance-trained **males** with volume equalized: rest-pause and drop sets gave similar hypertrophy to traditional sets; rest-pause gave slightly larger strength gains. **[Moderate]**, M-only — [Applied Physiology, Nutrition and Metabolism](https://cdnsciencepub.com/doi/abs/10.1139/apnm-2021-0278)
- Partials past failure (calves): van den Tillaar et al. 2025 (SportRxiv preprint; 4 sets of unilateral Smith machine calf raises to momentary failure, 2×/week, 8 weeks) concluded both initial partials and past-failure partials are viable for medial gastrocnemius hypertrophy. A 2025 study of trained lifters reportedly found lengthened partials beat past-failure partials for calves. Both are small and partly secondhand. **[Weak]** — [SportRxiv preprint](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/download/542/1162/1086); [BarBend](https://barbend.com/train-past-failure-for-the-thickest-calves)
- A 2025 within-person trial in 30 resistance-trained people reportedly found equal biceps and triceps thickness gains from lengthened partials and full range of motion. **[Weak]** (secondhand) — [Planfit summary](https://planfit.ai/en/blog/lengthened-partials)
- Stronger By Science has a drop-set review (not readable in this session). — [SBS: Can You Drop (Set) Weight for Gains?](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/drop-sets/)

### Inferences
- Use drop sets, rest-pause or myo-reps only on the **last set** of an isolation or stable machine exercise, mainly to save time. Count a drop set as roughly one extra hard set, not several. **[Opinion]**
- Don't use them on free-weight barbell compounds. **[Opinion]**
- Lengthened partials past failure are an optional finisher for calves or biceps and need not be a default. **[Weak]**

### Gaps
- I found no myo-rep-specific hypertrophy trial.
- I found no trials of advanced techniques limited to women.
- The "Coleman 2023" drop-set paper named in the brief could not be confirmed; the 2023 meta-analysis I found is Sødal et al.

## 7. Calibration: the AMRAP test for learning what 1–2 RIR feels like

### Takeaway
RIR estimates are off by about 1 rep on average, mostly by under-predicting (people think they are closer to failure than they are). Accuracy improves with practice, with heavier loads, closer to failure and in later sets. Taking an occasional set to true failure on a safe exercise and comparing predicted with actual reps is a reasonable way to calibrate. The method itself comes from coaching practice rather than trials, but the supporting principles have evidence behind them.

### Cited Findings
- Average under-prediction of reps to failure: **0.95 reps (95% CI 0.17–1.73)**. Accuracy is better close to failure, with heavier loads, in later sets and with ≤12-rep targets. **[Moderate]** — [PubMed 34542869](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34542869/)
- Hermann 2025: RIR estimation accuracy **improved over the 8-week intervention**, especially on bench press, and was better on bench than squat. **[Moderate]** — [PubMed 40249908](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40249908/)
- Trained women and men: mean absolute bench press RIR error **0.65 ± 0.78 reps**, with no sex difference. **[Moderate]** — [Refalo et al. JSCR 2024 PDF](https://www.researchgate.net/profile/Martin-Refalo-2/publication/375687905_Accuracy_of_Intraset_Repetitions-in-Reserve_Predictions_During_the_Bench_Press_Exercise_in_Resistance-Trained_Male_and_Female_Subjects/links/65d9591fadc608480ae7db13/Accuracy-of-Intraset-Repetitions-in-Reserve-Predictions-During-the-Bench-Press-Exercise-in-Resistance-Trained-Male-and-Female-Subjects.pdf)
- A study titled "Repetitions in Reserve Is a Reliable Tool for Prescribing Resistance Training Load" tested RIR in novices (findings not retrieved). — [PubMed 36135029](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36135029/)
- Coaching practice: an occasional AMRAP at a fixed load, or the athlete calls her RIR at the target rep and then keeps going to a hard, safe limit. Do this sparingly, because a true max-rep set is fatiguing, and preferably on a low-stakes isolation exercise. **[Opinion]** — [RPE calibration tips](https://myrpecalculator.pages.dev/blog/rpe-calibration-tips); [Stronger By Science RIR article (title only; not readable)](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/reps-in-reserve/)

### Inferences
Encodable calibration protocol **[Opinion]**, grounded in the Halperin and Hermann findings:
1. **When:** week 1 of each training block (every 4–6 weeks), and whenever she's new to an exercise.
2. **Where:** machine or isolation exercises only (e.g., leg press, chest press machine, lat pulldown, leg extension, curl). Never barbell squat, deadlift or bench without safeties.
3. **How:** on the last working set, at the rep where she believes she has 2 left, she logs "predicted RIR = 2" and the rep count, then continues to failure (the first rep she cannot complete with good form). Error = actual reps after the call − 2.
4. **Interpretation:** if the error is ≥ +2 (she had 4+ left), her RIR targets are too conservative. Raise the load ~5–10% or add 2+ reps. If the error is within ±1, she's calibrated.
5. **Rep-overshoot rule (no failure needed):** if she beats the top of the prescribed rep range by ≥2 reps at the prescribed RIR, increase the load next session.
6. Because accuracy is worst far from failure and at high reps, prescribe light/high-rep sets as "to failure" rather than "stop at 2 RIR."

### Gaps
- No study validated the "AMRAP to calibrate RIR" method as an intervention. It's coaching practice supported by indirect evidence (accuracy improves with exposure and near failure).
- I found no data on how quickly women specifically improve RIR accuracy.

## 8. Bottom line: encodable rules by exercise class, failure-set limits, and changes across a block

### Takeaway
For a 22-year-old woman training for lean muscle: take nearly every working set **close to failure (0–3 RIR, usually 1–2)**, treat **true failure as an optional tool** for the last set of isolation and stable machine exercises and for all light high-rep work, and **never plan failure on heavy free-weight compounds**. Move gradually from about 3 RIR to 0–1 RIR across a 4–6-week block, then deload. This corrects the earlier "don't train to failure" advice: the problem was never failure itself, but failure in the wrong place or every set.

### Cited Findings
(Supporting evidence is summarized from Q1–Q7; each rule's grade is in the table.)
- Strength is insensitive to RIR over a wide range; hypertrophy improves closer to failure. — [Robinson 2024](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-024-02069-2)
- 0 RIR ≈ 1–2 RIR for hypertrophy, with more fatigue at failure. — [Refalo 2024](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/02640414.2024.2321021)
- Failure gives a small to modest hypertrophy edge in single-set training. — [Hermann 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40249908/)
- Light loads need failure; heavy loads don't. — [Lasevicius 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31895290/)
- Failure in young women added no overall advantage over volume-matched non-failure. — [Martorelli 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28713535/)
- Failure slows recovery by 24–48 h on squat/bench. — [Morán-Navarro 2017](https://www.springermedizin.de/time-course-of-recovery-following-resistance-training-leading-or/15095268)
- Women training alone choose about 42% 1RM. — [Ratamess 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18296962/)
- Failure does not consistently change outcomes; volume does. — [ACSM 2026](https://www.newswise.com/articles/acsm-unveils-landmark-2026-resistance-training-guidelines-first-update-in-17-years)
- RP-style blocks run from about 3 RIR down to about 0 RIR, then deload. **[Opinion]** — [Arvo RP summary](https://arvo.guru/resources/methods/rp-training)

### Inferences
**Rules by exercise class** (all **[Opinion]** syntheses unless a grade is noted; the evidence basis is shown in the last column)

| Exercise class | Examples | Default RIR (working sets) | Failure (0 RIR) allowed? | Failure-set cap | Basis |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Stop at the first clear slowdown or form change | **No** (never planned). | Machine / cable compound | Leg press, hack squat, chest press, lat pulldown, seated cable row, Smith machine, hip thrust machine | 1–2 RIR | **Yes**, on the **last set** of the exercise, mainly in the final 1–2 weeks of a block | ≤1 per exercise per session | Refalo 2024 used leg press [Moderate]; fatigue [Moderate] |

**Counting rules [Opinion]**
- A set counts as a full hypertrophy "hard set" only if it ends at ≤3 RIR. Sets at 4–5 RIR count as about half a set. Sets at ≥5 RIR, or light sets not taken near failure, count as 0.
- Weekly failure-set budget: no more than about **1/3 of a muscle's weekly hard sets** at 0 RIR, and only on isolation, machine or light exercises. In practice: **≤1 failure set per exercise, ≤2–3 per muscle per session.**
- Don't put failure sets on a muscle within about 48 h before a heavy compound session for that muscle (e.g., no failure leg extensions the day before squats). [Opinion based on Morán-Navarro 2017]

**Changes across a 5-week block plus deload [Opinion; modeled on RP-style progression]**

| Week | Heavy compounds | Machine compounds | Isolation / light | Failure sets |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 (calibration) | 3 RIR | 2 RIR | 1–2 RIR | Calibration AMRAP on 1–2 machine/isolation exercises only |
| 2 | 2–3 RIR | 2 RIR | 1 RIR | None required |
| 3 | 2 RIR | 1–2 RIR | 0–1 RIR | Last isolation set may go to failure |
| 4 | 1–2 RIR | 1 RIR | 0–1 RIR | Last set of each isolation and machine exercise may go to failure |
| 5 (optional peak) | 1–2 RIR (never 0) | 0–1 RIR | 0 RIR last set | Same as week 4 |
| Deload | 4–5 RIR, about half the sets | 4 RIR | 3–4 RIR | None |

**Autoregulation triggers [Opinion]**
- If load or reps on an exercise drop for 2 sessions in a row, or she feels unrecovered, add 1 RIR across the board and remove failure sets for a week.
- On bad-readiness days (sleep, illness, menstrual symptoms), add 1–2 RIR rather than skipping. The sibling notes recommend this symptom-based adjustment over cycle-based periodization.
- Rep-overshoot rule: beating the top of the rep range by ≥2 reps at the target RIR → increase the load next session.

**Women-specific adjustments [Weak–Opinion]**
- No different RIR targets for women; the evidence supports the same rules.
- Build in the calibration AMRAP and the rep-overshoot rule, because unsupervised women tend to pick loads that are too light and everyone under-predicts RIR by about 1 rep.
- Rest periods can stay the same or be slightly shorter on isolation work (women's fatigue resistance). Don't add failure sets to "compensate" for fatigue resistance.

### Gaps
- The exact failure-set caps, the block RIR schedule and the half-set counting rule are expert-style syntheses, not trial-tested values. They are consistent with the evidence above but should be labeled as opinion in the program.
- I found no long-term (≥6 months) data in women on any of these rules, and no injury data to quantify the risk of failure on compound lifts.
