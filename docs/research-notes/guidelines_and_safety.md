# Jump training for bone at 22: what the guidelines say, and how to do it safely

Evidence grades used on every claim: **[Strong]** = official guideline wording, or a meta-analysis or large RCT body that agrees with it; **[Moderate]** = individual RCTs, smaller meta-analyses, or guideline content seen only through reliable secondary summaries; **[Weak]** = observational data, small or pilot studies, or secondary or commercial summaries; **[Opinion]** = an expert's or practitioner's view, or my own inference.

Method note: full-text fetching was blocked in this session for pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov, theros.org.uk and bones.nih.gov ("host not in allowed network domains"). All findings below come from web-search retrieval of abstracts, guideline excerpts and secondary summaries. Where exact guideline wording could not be confirmed, this is flagged.

## Q1. Which established guidelines recommend impact or jump training for women aged 18–30, and which target only children or older women?

### Takeaway
Impact or jumping is **not** a menopause-only recommendation. The main adult bone guideline (ACSM 2004) lists "activities that involve jumping" in its prescription for **adults**. Archived NIH guidance tells adults to gradually add skipping and jumping. ESSA 2017 (Australia) recommends brief high-impact exercise plus high-intensity resistance training as lifelong prevention for healthy adults, and the ROS (UK) advises building up to about 50 moderate impacts a day. The general physical-activity guidelines (WHO 2020, UK CMO 2019) name "bone-strengthening" explicitly only for children and adolescents. For adults they recommend muscle strengthening on 2 or more days a week. The UK CMO links that strengthening to bone health but does not, as far as I could confirm, prescribe jumping for adults. No guideline singles out "women aged 18–30" as its own group. Young adult women fall under "adults", and the specific wording on women is aimed either at youth (peak bone mass) or at postmenopausal women and people with osteoporosis.

### Cited Findings

**ACSM 2004 Position Stand, "Physical activity and bone health" (Kohrt, Bloomfield, Little, Nelson, Yingling; Med Sci Sports Exerc 36(11):1985–1996)**
- Adult prescription, wording confirmed by several reproductions of the position stand: Mode = "weight-bearing endurance activities (tennis; stair climbing; jogging, at least intermittently during walking), activities that involve jumping (volleyball, basketball), and resistance exercise (weight lifting)". Intensity: moderate to high, in terms of bone-loading forces. Frequency: weight-bearing endurance activities 3–5 times a week and resistance exercise 2–3 times a week. Duration: 30–60 min/day of a combination of these. **[Strong; guideline wording]**. Target group: **adults (all adults, not only older adults)**. Sources: [Full text PDF hosted at Univ. Verona](https://docs.univr.it/documenti/OccorrenzaIns/matdid/matdid600016.pdf); [bibliographic record](https://cir.nii.ac.jp/crid/1570291226282093824?lang=en)
- ACSM frames the adult goal as **preserving** bone. It states that whether adults can **increase** BMD through exercise "remains equivocal" **[Strong as a statement of the 2004 evidence; later meta-analyses (Q3) partly resolve this for premenopausal women]**. Source: [ACSM position stand PDF](https://docs.univr.it/documenti/OccorrenzaIns/matdid/matdid600016.pdf)
- For children and adolescents, ACSM recommends impact activities "such as gymnastics, plyometrics, and jumping" plus moderate-intensity resistance training **[Moderate; seen via secondary summary, exact text not retrieved]**. Source: [eOrthopod summary of ACSM guidance](https://eorthopod.com/?p=50110)
- ACSM adds that programmes for **older** adults should also include balance and fall-prevention work. That addition targets older people, not young adults **[Strong]**. Source: [ACSM PDF](https://docs.univr.it/documenti/OccorrenzaIns/matdid/matdid600016.pdf)

- The archived NIAMS "bone exercise packet" says most adults should begin with weight-bearing exercise and "gradually add some skipping and jumping activity". It also says short high-impact bouts can be enough, with 5–10 minutes daily possibly sufficient **[Moderate; official but archived, with exact wording seen via search excerpt]**. Target: adults generally. Source: [NIAMS bone exercise packet (2008–09 web archive)](https://webarchive.library.unt.edu/eot2008/20090508021841/http:/www.niams.nih.gov/Health_Info/Bone/Bone_Health/Exercise/bone_exercise_packet.asp)

**ESSA 2017 position statement (Beck, Daly, Singh, Taaffe; J Sci Med Sport 20(5):438–445). Note: it was published in JSAMS, not BJSM.**
- Evidence from animal and human trials shows that bone responds positively to impact activities and to high-intensity progressive resistance training (PRT). Impact may need modifying in the presence of osteoarthritis or frailty, and adequate calcium and vitamin D are required **[Strong; abstract-level]**. Sources: [ECU repository record](https://ro.ecu.edu.au/ecuworkspost2013/2797); [SciSpace summary](https://typeset.io/papers/exercise-and-sports-science-australia-essa-position-2k4mh04cza)
- For **healthy adults aiming to prevent osteoporosis**, the statement is summarised as recommending "lifelong exercise including regular, brief, weight-bearing, high-impact exercise and high intensity PRT". Programmes for people with low bone mass are built on moderate-to-high-impact weight-bearing activity, high-intensity PRT and balance training **[Moderate; via secondary summaries because the full text is paywalled]**. Sources: [SciSpace summary](https://typeset.io/papers/exercise-and-sports-science-australia-essa-position-2k4mh04cza); [PMC review summarising ESSA](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5968984)
- Examples of impact named in ESSA-derived materials: jumping, hopping, skipping, bounding, drop jumps. One practitioner summary cites about 3 sets of 8 reps at high intensity, framed for osteoporosis prevention and management **[Weak; physio blog paraphrase]**. Source: [Wild Physio summary](https://wildphysiofitness.au/blogs/physiotherapy/exercise-for-bone-health)

**BHOF/NOF peak bone mass position statement (Weaver et al., Osteoporos Int 2016;27(4))**
- Scope: **children and adolescents**, to help them reach maximal peak bone mass "early in life". The statement is a systematic review that updated the 2000 NOF statement **[Strong as to scope]**. Sources: [PubMed record 26856587](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26856587/); [News-Medical: "NOF provides evidence-based guidance to help children and adolescents…"](https://www.news-medical.net/news/20160210/NOF-provides-evidence-based-guidance-to-help-children-and-adolescents-attain-optimal-bone-health.aspx)

**Female Athlete Triad / IOC REDs consensus (2023)**
- These are not exercise-prescription documents. Their bone message is that low energy availability (LEA) suppresses ovulation and estrogen, shifting bone turnover toward resorption. The results are low BMD and a raised risk of bone stress injury (BSI), and recovery of bone takes years **[Strong; consensus content via secondary summaries]**. Sources: [ISAKOS "Bone Health and Females" 2025](https://isakos.com/GlobalLink/Newsletter/2025-Volume-3/Bone-Health-and-Females); [AMSSM listing of IOC REDs consensus](https://learn.amssm.org/AssetListing/Best-Practice-Case-Studies-1088/Case-113-Asset-3-IOC-Consensus-Statement-Relative-Energy-Deficiency-in-Sport-4884)
- In more than 200 elite athletes assessed with the IOC REDs CAT2 tool, athletes with REDs had more amenorrhoea, more bone stress injuries and lower BMD. Those in the "orange" category had higher odds of later BSI **[Moderate]**. Source: [BJSM 2025;59(1):24](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/59/1/24)

### Inferences
- **[Opinion]** "Jumping is a menopause recommendation" is a misreading. The oldest mainstream adult bone guideline (ACSM 2004) puts jumping in the **adult** prescription. Postmenopausal-specific and osteoporosis-specific guidance (ROS Strong, Steady and Straight; HBA's physio quick reference; LIFTMOR-based programmes) **adds** to that baseline by working out how to make impact safe for fragile bone. It is not where the idea started.
- **[Opinion]** No guideline creates a separate "18–30" category. Young women fall under "adults", and the youth-targeted documents (WHO, BHOF) cover the growth years, which end just before her age. She sits in a transition zone: past the period of fastest bone gain, but still able to consolidate and gain modestly (see Q3).

## Q2. How did jump training become linked with menopause (Stacy Sims, LIFTMOR)? Is it menopause-only or a general bone principle?

### Takeaway
The menopause association is recent and comes from popularisation. Stacy Sims promotes short daily jump-landing sessions for **peri- and postmenopausal** women, and LIFTMOR (2018) showed that heavy lifting plus impact was safe and effective in **older women with low bone mass**. The principle that bone responds to brief, high-magnitude, high-rate loading is general across ages. Ironically, the classic jump RCT (Bassey 1998) found that the **same jumping programme raised hip BMD in premenopausal women but not in postmenopausal women**.

### Cited Findings
- **Sims's own claim**: her site describes OSTEO-GAINS "Jump Jump Rest Repeat!" programmes of under 5 minutes a day at home. She says BMD "in peri-menopausal women can be improved with only three to five minutes of bone-stimulating jump exercises per day", and notes that she took part in the trials **[Opinion/Weak as stated; the trial outcomes themselves were not retrieved]**. Source: [Dr Stacy Sims, "Bone health" newsletter article](https://www.drstacysims.com/newsletters/articles/posts/bone-health)
- Secondary summaries of Sims mention her emphasis on stiff-leg landings and multi-directional stress on bone **[Weak; AI-generated or podcast summaries]**. Source: [Momentous: "Maintaining Bone and Muscle Mass Through Menopause and Beyond"](https://www.livemomentous.com/blogs/health-and-science/wonder-women-maintaining-bone-muscle-mass-through-menopause-and-beyond)
- In this search I found no Sims statement restricting jumping to menopause. Her published bone material is aimed at peri- and postmenopausal women **[Weak; absence of evidence]**. Source: [Sims bone-health article](https://www.drstacysims.com/newsletters/articles/posts/bone-health)
- **LIFTMOR RCT (Watson et al., JBMR 2018;33(2):211)**: 101 postmenopausal women (age 65 ± 5) with low bone mass. Eight months of twice-weekly, 30-min supervised high-intensity resistance and impact training (HiRIT: 5 sets × 5 reps at >85% 1RM) versus low-intensity home exercise. HiRIT improved lumbar spine and femoral neck BMD and function, with one minor adverse event (a lower-back spasm) **[Strong for its population: older women with low bone mass]**. Source: [JBMR, Watson et al. 2018](https://academic.oup.com/jbmr/article/33/2/211/7605709)
- LIFTMOR enrolled women with osteopenia or osteoporosis under supervision. Its results may not generalise to unsupervised high-impact training **[Opinion, from a secondary critique]**. Source: [ALLSET Gazette, "Is jumping safe for postmenopausal…"](https://allset.substack.com/p/is-jumping-safe-for-postmenopausal)
- **Bassey et al. 1998 (J Bone Miner Res)**: 50 vertical jumps (mean height 8.5 cm), 6 days a week. Ground reaction force was about 3× body weight in the young women. **Premenopausal women gained 2.8% femoral BMD after 5 months versus controls; postmenopausal women showed no significant benefit** at 12 or 18 months **[Moderate; single RCT]**. Sources: [Bassey 1998 record](https://data.zhenzhi.com/details/1/876293885); [Times Higher Education, "A bounce a day keeps brittle bones away"](https://www.timeshighereducation.com/node/6216)
- ACSM 2004 already included jumping for adults 14 years before LIFTMOR (see Q1) **[Strong]**. Source: [ACSM PDF](https://docs.univr.it/documenti/OccorrenzaIns/matdid/matdid600016.pdf)
- Youth guidance (ACSM children's section; WHO 2020 5–17; BHOF 2016) treats impact as the key bone stimulus during growth **[Strong]**. Sources: [IJBNPA/WHO](https://ijbnpa.biomedcentral.com/track/pdf/10.1186/s12966-020-01037-z); [Moving Medicine/Weaver](https://birmingham.movingmedicine.ac.uk/evidence/paed-obesity-improved-bone-health/)

### Inferences
- **[Opinion]** The menopause link comes from (a) a large, under-served audience (peri- and postmenopausal women losing bone after estrogen falls), (b) LIFTMOR overturning the old belief that women with osteoporosis must avoid heavy or impact loading, and (c) Sims's popular books and podcasts. None of this means jumping is reserved for menopause. In the most direct comparison (Bassey 1998), the premenopausal skeleton responded **better** to jumping.
- **[Opinion]** The more accurate framing is a life-course one. Impact builds bone in childhood and adolescence, consolidates or adds modestly in young adulthood, and slows or partly reverses loss after menopause, where it must be scaled to bone fragility.

### Gaps
- OSTEO-GAINS trial results (Sims's group) were not retrieved, so the size of effect in perimenopausal women is unverified.
- LIFTMOR's exact impact component (from background knowledge, jumping chin-ups with drop landings) was not confirmed in the retrieved abstract.
- I did not trace the publishing history of Sims's claims (e.g., the book *Next Level*, 2022) to primary text.

## Q3. Does jump training actually build bone in women her age? (Supporting trial evidence)

### Takeaway
Yes, modestly. Meta-analyses of RCTs in premenopausal women show that jumping increases femoral neck and trochanter BMD, with smaller or less consistent effects at the spine. Effective protocols are short (10–100 jumps a day) and produce gains of about 0.6–3.4% at the femoral neck over 4–18 months. At 22 she is just past the age at which hip bone mass peaks in most Western data, so gains are possible but smaller than in adolescence.

### Cited Findings
- **Zhao, Zhao & Zhang 2014 (Sports Med)**, a meta-analysis of jumping in premenopausal women: jumping significantly increased femoral neck and trochanter BMD, with less benefit at the lumbar spine **[Moderate; small number of trials (6)]**. Sources: [Springer Medicine](https://www.springermedicine.com/efficiency-of-jumping-exercise-in-improving-bone-mineral-density/22254016); [Bohrium record](https://bohrium.dp.tech/paper/arxiv/24981245)
- **Kelley, Kelley & Kohrt 2013**, a meta-analysis of RCTs of at least 24 weeks of exercise in premenopausal women: "exercise benefits FN and LS BMD in premenopausal women". This covers all exercise, not only jumping **[Moderate–Strong]**. Source: [PMC3563173](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3563173/)
- A review of programme design for premenopausal women: jump-landing protocols of 10–100 jumps a day, 3–7 days a week, lasting 4–18 months, with loads of 2–6 body weights and loading rates above 43 BW/s, produced femoral neck BMD gains of 0.6–3.4% **[Moderate; review, citation details to verify]**. Sources: [Program design considerations for bone health in premenopausal women](https://oalib-perpustakaan.upi.edu/Record/peertech__10_17352_asmp_000015/Description); [ANZBMS abstract](https://anzbms-ifmrs.m.asnevents.com.au/schedule/session/10852/abstract/42702)
- Peak bone timing (US NHANES): mean age at peak femoral neck BMD in women 18.7 y, total hip 19 y, lumbar spine 20.1 y **[Moderate; cross-sectional]**. Source: [Healio 2019, "Peak BMD attainment varies by sex, skeletal site"](https://cm.healio.com/news/endocrinology/20191126/peak-bmd-attainment-varies-by-sex-skeletal-site)
- A longitudinal Canadian study put women's peak total hip mass at age 16–19 and peak lumbar spine mass at age 33–40. Peaks come later in some non-Western cohorts (e.g., Vietnamese women 26–30) **[Moderate; results vary by population]**. Sources: [PMC5101070](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5101070); [Springer Medicine, Vietnamese women](https://www.springermedicine.com/peak-bone-mineral-density-in-vietnamese-women/21836626)
- Some women lose hip BMD in early adulthood despite healthy behaviours **[Weak–Moderate; observational]**. Source: [Healio 2021](https://cm.healio.com/news/endocrinology/20211019/bmd-loss-seen-in-early-adulthood-for-women-despite-healthy-behaviors)
- ROS-linked physiotherapy material: being "under the age of 30 gives you the best chance" of restoring bone losses and rebuilding your bone bank **[Weak; leaflet aimed at people with eating disorders]**. Source: [CSP/CPMH leaflet, Oct 2023](https://cpmh.csp.org.uk/system/files/documents/2023-11/exercise_activity_and_osteoporosis_with_an_eating_disorder_v4_october_2023_0.pdf)
- Belinda Beck (a lead author of the ESSA statement) says benefits take at least 6–12 months of training, and that jumping should be supervised especially in people with osteoporosis **[Opinion/expert]**. Source: [Griffith news](https://news.griffith.edu.au/?p=43912)

### Inferences
- **[Opinion]** For a healthy 22-year-old, jumping is a reasonable way to finish building and then protect hip bone, which is the site most relevant to later fracture. The expected effect is a few percent of BMD, not a transformation. The value lies in entering the menopausal decline with a higher starting point.
- **[Opinion]** Because the hip responds best and the hip peaks earliest, starting impact in the early 20s is if anything **better** timed than starting at 50.

### Gaps
- Kato et al. 2006 (10 jumps a day in young women) could not be retrieved and is not cited.
- Martyn-St James & Carroll's premenopausal impact meta-analysis (J Bone Miner Metab, around 2010) was not retrieved.
- OPTIMA-Ex (women aged 18–30) results were not found.
- I found no RCT showing that jumping in the 20s reduces fracture risk decades later. The link from BMD to future fracture is inferred.

## Q4. Safety considerations for a healthy 22-year-old woman

### Takeaway
For a healthy, non-pregnant, uninjured 22-year-old, low-volume, progressive jump training is low-risk. Three issues matter most. **Pelvic floor**: stress urinary incontinence is common even in nulliparous athletes during high-impact sport, and appears to reflect load exceeding pelvic floor capacity; pelvic floor muscle training is first-line and safe. **Knees and ACL**: young women have high ACL risk, and neuromuscular and landing-technique training roughly halves it. **Overuse of the tendons and shins**: managed through volume caps, rest days and progression gated on landing quality. Clear red flags are low energy availability or missed periods (raised bone stress injury risk), pregnancy, and current lower-limb injury or pain.

### Cited Findings

**Pelvic floor / stress urinary incontinence (SUI)**
- In young nulliparous elite athletes, reported incontinence ranged from 0% (golf) to 80% (trampolinists). It is highest in high-impact sports (gymnastics, track and field, some ball games) **[Moderate; from Nygaard/Bø-era studies]**. Sources: [BMJ Open SEM blog: "Past, present and future of pelvic floor issues in female athletes"](https://blogs.bmj.com/bmjopensem/2021/06/29/past-present-and-future-of-pelvic-floor-issues-in-female-athletes/); [Thieme review PDF](https://www.thieme-connect.com/products/ejournals/pdf/10.1055/a-1073-7977.pdf)
- **Teixeira et al. 2018 (Int Urogynecol J)**, a meta-analysis: 36% prevalence of UI in female athletes, and 177% higher risk of UI versus sedentary women **[Strong for prevalence and association]**. Source: [Teixeira et al. 2018 PDF](https://springerlink.bibliotecabuap.elogim.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s00192-018-3651-1.pdf)
- **Pires et al. 2020 (J Hum Kinet)**, a meta-analysis: 25.9% UI and 20.7% SUI in female athletes; volleyball (75.6%) the highest among high-impact sports **[Moderate]**. Source: [J Hum Kinet](https://johk.pl/?p=5625)
- Conflicting: a 2022 review reports about 40% UI in young nulliparous athletes and higher prevalence in high-impact sports. It also cites a meta-analysis finding that prevalence did **not** change with the degree of sport impact **[Moderate; the evidence conflicts]**. Source: [Curr Urol Rep 2022](https://springerlink.bibliotecabuap.elogim.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s11934-022-01133-6.pdf)
- **Damage or unmasking?** Bø's view is that a "stiff" and strong pelvic floor positioned optimally counteracts the abdominal-pressure spikes of high-impact activity. Athletes may need **better** pelvic floor function than non-exercisers. No source established that high-impact sport causes lasting pelvic floor damage in nulliparous women **[Opinion/Weak; mechanism-based expert view]**. Sources: [BMJ Open SEM blog](https://blogs.bmj.com/bmjopensem/2021/06/29/past-present-and-future-of-pelvic-floor-issues-in-female-athletes/); [ClinicalTrials.gov NCT05341024](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT05341024)
- **What to do**: pelvic floor muscle training (PFMT) "has no serious adverse effects" and is first-line treatment. A randomised pilot in elite volleyball players found a significant effect. A 2023 systematic review and meta-analysis of PFMT in female athletes exists, though overall athlete-specific evidence is thin **[Moderate for safety and first-line status; Weak–Moderate for effect in athletes]**. Sources: [Kari Bø publication list (SciSpace)](https://typeset.io/authors/kari-bo-2t3mpp8m98); [PFMT in female athletes: SR/MA (PubMed 37688407)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37688407/); [Pilot in nulliparous sport students](https://ouci.dntb.gov.ua/works/4Ka0Xmo9)

**Knee / ACL**
- **Petushek et al. 2019 (AJSM)**, a meta-analysis of 18 studies with 27,231 young female athletes: neuromuscular training (NMT) cut ACL injury odds (OR 0.51), from 1 in 54 to 1 in 111. The effect was larger in middle and high school athletes. Landing stabilisation and lower-body strength exercises in each session improved the benefit **[Strong]**. Sources: [PubMed 30001501](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30001501/); [QxMD](https://read.qxmd.com/read/30001501/evidence-based-best-practice-guidelines-for-preventing-anterior-cruciate-ligament-injuries-in-young-female-athletes-a-systematic-review-and-meta-analysis)
- Earlier review: plyometric training combined with biomechanical analysis and technique training was common to all effective ACL-prevention programmes. Approaches without plyometrics had limited success **[Moderate]**. Source: [PMC2779043](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2779043)
- 2025 Sports Med meta-analysis (13 studies, 648 youth females): training increased peak knee flexion (g = 0.58) and reduced knee valgus (g = −0.86) on landing. Study quality was mixed **[Moderate]**. Source: [Cardiff Met record](https://pure.cardiffmet.ac.uk/en/publications/influence-of-neuromuscular-training-interventions-on-jump-landing/)

**Tendons / shins / overuse: plyometric dosing**
- Beginner plyometric volume: widely taught NSCA-derived tables give 80–100 foot contacts per session (intermediate 100–120, advanced 120–140). Some coaches start at 60–80, and one physiotherapy protocol starts at 60, then progresses to 90 and 120 **[Weak–Moderate; practice standard rather than trial-derived]**. Sources: [NSCA-CPT study guide](https://open-exam-prep.com/study-guides/nsca-cpt/mobility-bodyweight-performance/plyometrics); [Mass General Brigham jump/plyometric progression](https://www.massgeneralbrigham.org/content/dam/unified-xwalk/pdf/patient-education/english/mass-general-jump-and-plyometric-training.pdf); [Steward Health progression](https://content.steward.org/node/659066)
- Progression advice: start with 1 session a week and add a second only if the first goes well; space sessions 48–72 h apart; do bilateral before single-leg; progress on landing quality (quiet, stable) rather than by the calendar **[Weak; practitioner guidance]**. Sources: [Outside Online](https://www.outsideonline.com/running/training/workouts/ready-for-power-building-plyometrics-take-this-test-and-start-with-these-exercises/?scope=anon); [Pliability](https://pliability.com/stories/plyometric-exercises-for-beginners)
- Achilles tendinopathy is more likely when the musculoskeletal system is not conditioned for high-velocity loading. Pain during jumping should stop the session and prompt review **[Weak]**. Sources: [Pliability](https://pliability.com/stories/plyometric-exercises-for-athletes); [Steward Health](https://content.steward.org/node/659066)
- The bone stimulus needs far less volume than athletic plyometrics: 10–100 jumps a day in premenopausal trials, 50 jumps a day in Bassey 1998, about 50 moderate impacts a day per ROS, and 5–10 minutes a day per archived NIH guidance **[Moderate]**. Sources: [premenopausal design review](https://oalib-perpustakaan.upi.edu/Record/peertech__10_17352_asmp_000015/Description); [Bassey 1998](https://data.zhenzhi.com/details/1/876293885); [NIAMS archive](https://webarchive.library.unt.edu/eot2008/20090508021841/http:/www.niams.nih.gov/Health_Info/Bone/Bone_Health/Exercise/bone_exercise_packet.asp)

**When jumping is inadvisable or needs modifying**
- **Low energy availability / REDs / missed periods**: LEA suppresses estrogen, lowers BMD and raises bone stress injury risk. Athletes with a history of BSI need BMD assessment, and bone recovery takes years **[Strong]**. Sources: [ISAKOS](https://isakos.com/GlobalLink/Newsletter/2025-Volume-3/Bone-Health-and-Females); [BJSM 2025](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/59/1/24)
- **Pregnancy**: ACOG Committee Opinion 804 (2020) is summarised as advising avoidance of contact sports and activities with high fall risk. Secondary sources advise limiting jerky or bouncy movement because of ligament laxity and pelvic floor load. Low-impact aerobics, walking and swimming are listed as safe **[Moderate for ACOG's fall-risk and contact-sport advice; Weak for the jumping-specific advice]**. Sources: [DONA summary of ACOG CO 804](https://dona.org/new-acog-opinion-on-exercise-in-the-childbearing-year/); [News-Medical](https://www.news-medical.net/health/Is-it-Safe-to-Exercise-During-Pregnancy.aspx)
- **Osteoarthritis or frailty**: ESSA states impact may need modifying (not relevant to a healthy 22-year-old, but a valid generator rule) **[Strong]**. Source: [ECU record of ESSA 2017](https://ro.ecu.edu.au/ecuworkspost2013/2797)
- Practitioner stop rules: poor landings, acute injury, or pain during jumping **[Weak]**. Sources: [NSCA-CPT study guide](https://open-exam-prep.com/study-guides/nsca-cpt/mobility-bodyweight-performance/plyometrics); [Steward Health](https://content.steward.org/node/659066)

### Inferences
- **[Opinion]** For bone, the useful dose (tens of jumps) is well below the volumes at which plyometric overuse problems typically arise (hundreds of contacts). A bone-focused jump block can therefore stay at the low end of plyometric volume, which keeps tendon and shin risk low.
- **[Opinion]** Leakage during jumping in a nulliparous 22-year-old is common and is best treated as a signal to train the pelvic floor (and see a pelvic health physiotherapist if it persists), not as a reason to ban impact. The evidence does not show that impact itself damages the nulliparous pelvic floor, but it also does not rule out harm.
- **[Opinion]** ACL-protective landing technique and bone-building jumps fit together naturally. The landing-technique and plyometric components of NMT can double as the bone stimulus.

### Gaps
- The female-to-male ACL injury rate ratio in jumping and cutting sports was not retrieved (background knowledge suggests 2–8×; unverified here).
- No trial-based guidance was found on the safest drop-box height for a bone-focused beginner (e.g., 20–30 cm). Any specific height is practitioner opinion.
- Bø's primary reviews (e.g., Sports Med 2004) and Nygaard 1994 were not read in full. The 0–80% range is reported via secondary sources.
- There is no direct evidence on postpartum return to impact (outside scope for a nulliparous 22-year-old).
- ACOG CO 804's exact text on jumping was not retrieved.

## Q5. Other bone-health basics for young women that matter as much as or more than jumping

### Takeaway
Energy availability and regular periods are preconditions: jumping cannot offset the bone loss caused by estrogen suppression from under-fuelling. Calcium (Grade A in youth) and vitamin D (good evidence) underpin bone gains, and ESSA states that exercise programmes must be paired with adequate calcium and vitamin D. Heavy progressive resistance training is recommended alongside impact in every adult guideline reviewed.

### Cited Findings
- REDs/Triad: LEA leads to suppressed ovulation and estrogen, which reduces bone formation, increases resorption, lowers BMD and raises BSI risk **[Strong]**. Sources: [ISAKOS](https://isakos.com/GlobalLink/Newsletter/2025-Volume-3/Bone-Health-and-Females); [AMSSM / IOC REDs consensus listing](https://learn.amssm.org/AssetListing/Best-Practice-Case-Studies-1088/Case-113-Asset-3-IOC-Consensus-Statement-Relative-Energy-Deficiency-in-Sport-4884)
- Weaver 2016: Grade A for calcium and physical activity (in youth). Good evidence for vitamin D and dairy. Good evidence of harm from DMPA (the contraceptive injection). Smoking possibly graded C (snippet garbled) **[Strong for calcium and activity; Moderate for vitamin D and dairy; smoking unverified]**. Sources: [PubMed 26856587](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26856587/); [News-Medical](https://www.news-medical.net/news/20160210/NOF-provides-evidence-based-guidance-to-help-children-and-adolescents-attain-optimal-bone-health.aspx); [Moving Medicine](https://birmingham.movingmedicine.ac.uk/evidence/paed-obesity-improved-bone-health/)
- ESSA 2017: programmes should include adequate calcium and vitamin D **[Strong]**. Source: [ECU/ESSA record](https://ro.ecu.edu.au/ecuworkspost2013/2797)
- Resistance training: ACSM (adults: resistance exercise 2–3 times a week), ESSA (high-intensity PRT), UK CMO (strength on 2 or more days) and LIFTMOR (HiRIT at >85% 1RM safe and effective in older women with low bone mass) all pair heavy resistance work with impact **[Strong]**. Sources: [ACSM PDF](https://docs.univr.it/documenti/OccorrenzaIns/matdid/matdid600016.pdf); [UK CMO PDF](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5d839543ed915d52428dc134/uk-chief-medical-officers-physical-activity-guidelines.pdf); [LIFTMOR](https://academic.oup.com/jbmr/article/33/2/211/7605709)

### Inferences
- **[Opinion]** For a 22-year-old, the ranking of what matters most for bone is roughly: (1) adequate energy intake and regular cycles, (2) heavy resistance training and impact loading, (3) calcium and vitamin D sufficiency and adequate protein, (4) not smoking and moderate alcohol. Jumping is a useful, cheap add-on, but it ranks below energy availability.

### Gaps
- No primary or official source was retrieved in this session for alcohol, smoking or protein effects specific to young adult women, nor for specific calcium or vitamin D targets (e.g., RDA 1,000 mg/day calcium for women aged 19–50; this is background knowledge, not verified here). The NIH ORBD young-women page that would cover these was blocked.
- The effect of hormonal contraceptives other than DMPA on peak bone mass was not reviewed.

## Q6. Verdict: is jumping "for her stage", and what safety rules should a workout generator apply?

### Takeaway
**Yes, jumping is for her stage.** It is not a menopause-specific recommendation. Mainstream adult bone guidance (ACSM 2004; NIH; ESSA 2017; ROS public advice) includes impact or jumping for adults generally. The premenopausal trial evidence is at least as good as the postmenopausal evidence, and in the classic head-to-head study it was better (Bassey 1998). What is menopause-specific is the **packaging** (Sims, LIFTMOR) and the extra care needed for fragile bone. At 22 the honest framing is: modest bone gain or consolidation at the hip, plus a higher starting point for later life, at low injury risk if dosed small and progressed on landing quality.

### Cited Findings
- Adult jumping recommendation: [ACSM 2004 PDF](https://docs.univr.it/documenti/OccorrenzaIns/matdid/matdid600016.pdf) **[Strong]**; [NIAMS archive](https://webarchive.library.unt.edu/eot2008/20090508021841/http:/www.niams.nih.gov/Health_Info/Bone/Bone_Health/Exercise/bone_exercise_packet.asp) **[Moderate]**
- Premenopausal benefit, and larger than postmenopausal in direct comparison: [Bassey 1998](https://data.zhenzhi.com/details/1/876293885) **[Moderate]**; [Zhao 2014 meta](https://www.springermedicine.com/efficiency-of-jumping-exercise-in-improving-bone-mineral-density/22254016) **[Moderate]**
- Small doses are effective: [premenopausal design review](https://oalib-perpustakaan.upi.edu/Record/peertech__10_17352_asmp_000015/Description) **[Moderate]**
- Landing-technique/NMT halves ACL risk: [Petushek 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30001501/) **[Strong]**
- PFMT is safe and first-line for SUI: [PFMT in female athletes SR/MA](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37688407/) **[Moderate]**

### Inferences: proposed workout-generator safety rules (all **[Opinion]**, derived from the cited findings above)
1. **Screen before including impact.** Exclude or swap to low-impact if the user reports pregnancy, a current lower-limb injury or pain (shin, foot, Achilles, knee), a history of bone stress fracture, or missed periods or low intake (possible REDs). For the last two, prompt a clinician check rather than prescribing more loading. (ESSA modification rule; REDs consensus; ACOG CO 804 summaries.)
2. **Dose for bone, not for athletic plyometrics.** Target about 20–50 impacts per session (up to about 100 once well adapted), on 3–7 days a week if short, or inside 2–3 strength sessions. More volume adds little bone benefit and more tendon risk. (Bassey 50/day; review 10–100/day; ROS about 50/day; NIH 5–10 min.)
3. **Progress by stages, gated on landing quality.** Order: (a) skips, pogo hops and low jumps in place; (b) countermovement and squat jumps landing on two feet; (c) small step-offs or low drop landings from a low box; (d) multi-directional and lateral hops; (e) single-leg hops; (f) higher drop or box work. Advance only after 1–2 weeks of quiet, stable landings with knees tracking over toes and no inward collapse. Prefer stepping **down** from boxes over jumping down from height early on.
4. **Teach landing technique every session.** Soft, quiet landings with hips and knees flexed and no knee valgus. Fold the jumps into an NMT-style warm-up (balance, landing stabilisation, lower-body strength), which is the evidence-based ACL-prevention package.
5. **Rest and spacing.** Leave at least 48 h between harder plyometric sessions. Short daily low-amplitude hops are acceptable. Start with 1 harder session a week and add a second when tolerated.
6. **Stop rules.** End the impact block if landings degrade, or if there is sharp or increasing pain in the shin, foot, Achilles or knee. Pain persisting beyond about 24 h means regress a stage and suggest professional review.
7. **Pelvic floor rule.** If leakage occurs with jumping, keep low-to-moderate impact, add pelvic floor muscle training, and suggest a pelvic health physiotherapist. Do not frame leakage as normal or as damage.
8. **Always pair with heavy resistance training** (2–3 sessions a week of progressive, compound lifts) and include a short note on adequate energy, calcium and vitamin D. Jumping is an add-on to this foundation, not a substitute.
9. **Messaging.** Present jumping as lifelong bone training appropriate at 22, not as a menopause intervention. Set realistic expectations: a few percent of hip BMD over 6–12+ months.

### Gaps
- No guideline gives explicit drop heights or impact counts for healthy 18–30-year-old women. The numbers above are extrapolated from premenopausal RCTs and general plyometric practice, not from a guideline written for her group.
- Long-term fracture outcomes of jumping started in the 20s are unknown. All benefits are inferred from BMD.
