# Longevity Training Levers Beyond Hypertrophy for a 22-Year-Old Woman

Scope: what non-hypertrophy training qualities a healthy 22-year-old woman should build now (bone, cardiorespiratory fitness, power/strength, balance/function, injury resilience, recovery), and the minimum effective doses that fit inside a 2–5 session/week hypertrophy-focused gym program. Research date: 2026-11-01. Evidence-strength tags used below: **[STRONG]** = RCT/meta-analysis or large consistent cohorts; **[MODERATE]** = smaller RCTs, consistent mechanistic + human data; **[WEAK]** = single small studies, extrapolation from other populations, animal data; **[OPINION]** = expert/practitioner framework.

Method note for the report writer: web search worked, but direct page fetches were blocked by the session's network allow-list (pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov and healthybonesaustralia.org.au failed DNS). Most figures below therefore come from abstracts/press summaries surfaced by search, not full texts. Where a number could not be cross-checked, it is flagged.

## 1. Peak bone mass: timing, why the early 20s matter, and what loading builds bone

### Takeaway
In women, hip BMD peaks around age 19 and lumbar spine around 20 to the early 30s (estimates vary by method), so at 22 the hip is at or just past its peak and the spine may still be gaining. Bone still responds to loading in this window. The best-supported recipe is heavy axial resistance training (about 80–85%+ 1RM) plus short, multidirectional, high-impact jumping: roughly 10–50 jumps per day, 3+ days/week, with rest between jumps.

### Cited Findings
**Timing of peak bone mass**
- NHANES 2005–2014 (Bone, 2020): estimated age at peak BMD in women was 18.7 y (femoral neck), 19.0 y (total hip), and 20.1 y (lumbar spine). Men peaked later (20.5, 21.2, 23.6 y). [STRONG for cross-sectional US estimate] — [Healio summary of the 2020 Bone paper](https://cm.healio.com/news/endocrinology/20191126/peak-bmd-attainment-varies-by-sex-skeletal-site); [PubMed 31760214](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31760214/)
- Canadian longitudinal (CaMos) data put total-hip peak bone mass in women at 16–19 y. One summary reports lumbar-spine peak at 33–40 y in women, much later than NHANES. That figure was surfaced by search and not verified against full text. — [Peak bone mass from longitudinal data (Berger et al., JBMR 2010)](https://latam-cl.demo.elsevierpure.com/fr/publications/peak-bone-mass-from-longitudinal-data-implications-for-the-preval)
- An older Mayo-cohort analysis reported peak bone mass in women in the early 20s at the proximal femur, about 28 y at the spine, and about 31 y at the forearm. — [PubMed 10871911](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10871911)
- The NOF position statement (Weaver et al., Osteoporos Int 2016;27:1281–1386) estimates that lifestyle factors (diet, physical activity, and others) determine 20–40% of adult peak bone mass. [STRONG, systematic review] — [PMC4791473 record](https://vufind.lboro.ac.uk/PrimoRecord/cdi_pubmedcentral_primary_oai_pubmedcentral_nih_gov_4791473); [PMC5101070](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5101070)

**Loading that builds bone: heavy resistance plus impact (LIFTMOR)**
- LIFTMOR (Watson et al., JBMR 2018;33:211–220): 101 postmenopausal women with low bone mass did 8 months of twice-weekly, 30-minute supervised high-intensity resistance and impact training (HiRIT; 5 sets × 5 reps at >85% 1RM) or a low-intensity home program. Lumbar spine BMD rose 2.9% with HiRIT vs a 1.2% loss in controls. Femoral neck BMD rose 0.3% vs a 1.9% loss, and femoral neck cortical thickness also improved. Compliance was 92% (HiRIT) vs 85% (control). The only adverse event was one minor low-back spasm. [MODERATE; single RCT, but a strong safety signal for heavy loading even in fragile bone] — [OUP JBMR abstract](https://academic.oup.com/jbmr/article/33/2/211/7605709); [PubMed 28975661](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28975661/); [Full-text PDF mirror](https://www.rvn.se/contentassets/50f5c4b1d85e464cb6ea892b6ac2a5aa/j-of-bone---mineral-res---2017---watson---highintensity-resistance-and-impact-training-improves-bone-mineral-density-and.pdf)
- The LIFTMOR authors note that high-intensity loading has traditionally been discouraged in osteoporosis because of a perceived fracture risk. The trial tested that premise and found HiRIT safe under supervision. — [OUP JBMR abstract](https://academic.oup.com/jbmr/article-abstract/33/2/211/7605709)

**Jump-training evidence in young and premenopausal women**
- Kato et al. 2006 (J Appl Physiol), RCT in college-aged women (mean about 20.7 y): **10 maximal vertical jumps/day, 3 days/week** significantly increased lumbar spine (L2–L4) BMD vs controls. Hip sites showed no significant interaction effect. [MODERATE; small RCT in exactly the target age group] — search summary of [PEDro record](https://search.pedro.org.au/search-results/record-detail/14578); [author record](https://scite.ai/authors/takeru-kato-lXdYZv)
- A 1998 trial (widely attributed to Bassey et al., JBMR 1998; authorship not confirmed in retrieved snippet) used **50 vertical jumps, 6 days/week**. Premenopausal women gained **2.8% femoral BMD over 5 months**, while postmenopausal women showed no significant gain on the same protocol. [MODERATE] — via search summary; see [2014 jumping meta-analysis](https://www.springermedicine.com/efficiency-of-jumping-exercise-in-improving-bone-mineral-density/22254016)
- A meta-analysis of six studies (Sports Med 2014) found that jumping significantly increased femoral neck and trochanter BMD in premenopausal women, while the lumbar spine benefited less. [MODERATE] — [Springer Medicine](https://www.springermedicine.com/efficiency-of-jumping-exercise-in-improving-bone-mineral-density/22254016); [Bohrium mirror](https://bohrium.dp.tech/paper/arxiv/24981245)
- A BYU RCT (16 weeks, premenopausal women aged 25–50) compared **10 vs 20 jumps, twice daily**. Both produced hip/trochanter BMD gains vs controls. [WEAK–MODERATE] — [BYU ScholarsArchive](https://scholarsarchive.byu.edu/etd/667)
- An ESSA 2017 position statement summary lists jumping, hopping, skipping, bounding, and drop jumps as impact options. It suggests **about 50 impacts per session, 4–7 days/week**, and recommends screening out people with established osteoporosis, fragility fracture, or high fall risk from high impact. The attribution comes from search summaries; the primary page was not retrieved. — [Wild Physio summary](https://wildphysiofitness.au/blogs/physiotherapy/exercise-for-bone-health); [Medibank summary](https://medibank.com.au/livebetter/be-magazine/exercise/want-stronger-bones-exercise-will-help)
- Practitioner guidance: short bursts of jumping, hopping, or skipping **in different directions** are "very effective", and any bone benefit takes at least 6–12 months to show. [OPINION/secondary] — [Medibank](https://medibank.com.au/livebetter/be-magazine/exercise/want-stronger-bones-exercise-will-help); [Wild Physio](https://wildphysiofitness.au/blogs/physiotherapy/exercise-for-bone-health)
- Griffith University's OPTIMA-Ex trial compares supervised impact training (boxing and jumping) with resistance training in women aged 18–30 with below-average bone mass. It is ongoing or unreported in what I found. — [News-Medical](https://www.news-medical.net/news/20160804/New-Griffith-study-aims-to-determine-optimum-mode-of-exercise-to-improve-bone-strength-of-young-women.aspx)

**Rest between loading bouts (Robling)**
- Robling, Burr & Turner (2001), in rats: after a bout of loading, bone cells desensitize. About **8 h of recovery restored full mechanosensitivity**, and bone formation in the 8-h group was more than double that of the 0–0.5 h groups. Short rests of **about 14 s between individual load cycles** increased relative bone formation rates by 66–190%. [WEAK for humans; animal model] — [PubMed 11606612](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11606612/); [Robling 2002 PDF](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/biology/2002-robling.pdf)
- Robling et al. (2002), in rats: 360 load cycles delivered as **4 bouts × 90 cycles separated by 3 h** built more bone than 360 cycles in one bout. — [Shorter, more frequent loading sessions enhance bone mass](https://data.zhenzhi.com/details/1/867444014)
- Turner & Robling review: inserting rest periods can **double** the anabolic response to mechanical loading. — [PMC1725178](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1725178)

### Inferences
- At 22, the hip is likely at or near peak, so the goal is to **maximize and consolidate peak bone mass and geometry, then maintain it**. The spine may still be gaining. A higher starting peak is the main lever against postmenopausal osteoporosis, because women lose bone quickly around menopause.
- Bone responds to **magnitude, rate, and novelty of strain**, not to repetition count. A small number of high-impact, multidirectional jumps (10–50) with brief rests between jumps is more bone-efficient than long continuous low-impact activity. Splitting a day's impacts into 2 bouts at least 4–8 h apart is mechanistically justified (Robling) but only animal-proven.
- Heavy axial lifting (squat, deadlift, overhead press at about 80–85%+ 1RM) gives an osteogenic stimulus that a hypertrophy program can provide almost for free if it includes some heavy, low-rep compound work twice a week. Hypertrophy training at 60–75% 1RM probably contributes less to bone than the LIFTMOR-style heavy sets do (inference, untested head-to-head).
- Because a hypertrophy program alone delivers little ground-reaction impact, **jumping is the main thing missing for bone**.

### Gaps
- LIFTMOR's exact exercise list was not retrieved this session. My background knowledge is that HiRIT used deadlift, back squat, and overhead press plus jumping chin-ups with drop landings, but that is unverified here. LIFTMOR-M (the men's trial) and Vlachopoulos (adolescent PRO-BONE football/plyometric) papers were not retrieved.
- No head-to-head trial in 18–30-year-old women tests hypertrophy-style training vs heavy low-rep training vs added jumping for BMD. OPTIMA-Ex may answer this but no results were found.
- Healthy Bones Australia's page could not be fetched (network block), so its exact young-adult dose wording is unverified.
- The commonly quoted model that a 10% higher peak bone mass delays osteoporosis by about 13 years (Hernandez 2003) was not retrieved; treat it as unverified.
- Heel drops: no primary human trial was retrieved this session.

## 2. Cardiorespiratory fitness (VO2max) as a longevity marker, and minimum effective dose alongside lifting

### Takeaway
Cardiorespiratory fitness (CRF) is one of the strongest observational predictors of all-cause mortality, with no upper limit to the benefit. Each 1-MET gain is associated with about 14% lower risk. For a lifter, one weekly session of 4×4-min intervals, or 2–3 short sprint-interval finishers, plus walking or other moderate activity meets WHO guidelines and raises VO2max. Concurrent aerobic work does not measurably blunt whole-muscle hypertrophy, but it does blunt explosive strength when done in the same session. Cycling interferes less than running.

### Cited Findings
**CRF and mortality**
- Mandsager et al. 2018 (JAMA Netw Open; 122,007 adults treadmill-tested at Cleveland Clinic, 1991–2014): mortality fell as fitness rose. "Elite" (top 2.5% by age and sex) had about 80% lower mortality risk than low performers, with **no upper limit to the benefit**. Low CRF carried risk comparable to or greater than smoking and diabetes. In younger age groups, **elite vs high was not statistically different**; the elite advantage was significant in those aged 70+ (HR 0.71). [STRONG association; observational, referred clinical population] — [TCTMD](https://www.tctmd.com/news/big-fitness-equals-big-survival-benefit-new-study-shows); [Cleveland Clinic newsroom](https://newsroom.clevelandclinic.org/2018/10/19/cleveland-clinic-research-shows-better-cardiorespiratory-fitness-correlates-to-a-longer-life/); [ACC summary](https://acc.org/latest-in-cardiology/articles/2019/02/20/08/11/cardiorespiratory-fitness-and-long-term-mortality); [ScienceDaily](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2018/10/181019120711.htm)
- Kokkinos et al. 2022 (JACC 80; 750,302 US veterans, median follow-up 10.2 y): CRF was inversely associated with mortality across age, sex, and race. **Each 1-MET increase had an adjusted HR of 0.86 (0.85–0.87).** The least fit (20th percentile) had **4× the mortality risk** of the 98th percentile, and extremely high CRF showed no increased risk. [STRONG association] — [Healio](https://cm.healio.com/news/cardiology/20220801/high-cardiorespiratory-fitness-reduces-veterans-death-risk-regardless-of-age-sex-race); [Medicine Today](https://cardiology.medicinetoday.com.au/ct/2022/october/journal-watch/better-cardiorespiratory-fitness-associated-lower-mortality-risk-all); [GWU record](https://hsrc.himmelfarb.gwu.edu/gwhpubs/1500)

**Guideline dose**
- WHO 2020 (Bull et al., BJSM 2020;54:1451–1462): adults should do **150–300 min/week moderate or 75–150 min/week vigorous** aerobic activity (or an equivalent mix), **plus muscle-strengthening on ≥2 days/week**. The ≥2-day figure was confirmed via a secondary summary; the abstract says "regular". [STRONG, guideline] — [BJSM full text](https://bjsm.bmj.com/node/121498.full); [UGR repository PDF](https://digibug.ugr.es/bitstream/handle/10481/66573/1451.full.pdf?sequence=1); [Consultant360](https://www.consultant360.com/exclusive/primary-care/who-new-guidelines-physical-activity-and-sedentary-behavior)

**Time-efficient protocols**
- Gillen et al. 2016 (PLOS ONE): sedentary men (n=27) did **3 × 20-s all-out cycle sprints (about 500 W) with 2 min easy cycling between, in a 10-min session (1 min of hard work), 3×/week for 12 weeks**. VO2peak rose about 19%, the same as 45-min continuous moderate cycling, with similar gains in insulin sensitivity and mitochondrial content. [MODERATE; small, men only] — [DOAJ/PLOS ONE](https://doaj.org/article/0d8f38860885479c87ee1ba61460002b); [Relias Media](https://reliasmedia.com/articles/137938-interval-training-for-cardiovascular-health-less-is-more)
- Gibala cautions that all-out SIT "is not suited for everyone". [OPINION] — [Dr Sharma summary](https://www.drsharma.ca/one-minute-of-vigorous-all-out-exercise-may-be-all-you-need-for-health)
- Helgerud et al. 2007 (MSSE 39:665–671): with work-matched protocols done 3×/week for 8 weeks, **4×4-min intervals (Norwegian 4×4) raised VO2max by 7.2%** and 15/15-s intervals by 5.5%, more than long slow distance or lactate-threshold training. Stroke volume rose about 10%, but only after interval training. [MODERATE; small RCT in young men] — [SDSU coaching summary](https://coachsci.sdsu.edu/csa/vol131/helgerud.htm); [Typeset/SciSpace](https://typeset.io/papers/aerobic-high-intensity-intervals-improve-vo2max-more-than-34pn939laf)
- Storoschuk et al. 2025 ("Much Ado About Zone 2", Sports Med 55:1611–1624), narrative review: current evidence **does not support Zone 2 as the optimal intensity** for improving mitochondrial or fat-oxidation capacity. Prioritizing **higher intensities (above Zone 2) is critical** for cardiometabolic benefit, especially when training volume is low. [MODERATE; narrative review] — [PubMed 40560504](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40560504/); [UESCA commentary](https://uesca.com/is-zone-2-really-the-endurance-panacea/)

**Interference with hypertrophy**
- Schumann et al. 2022 (Sports Med 52:601–612; 43 studies): concurrent aerobic plus strength training had **no effect on whole-muscle hypertrophy (SMD −0.01)** or maximal strength. **Explosive strength was reduced (SMD −0.28)**, more so when both were done **in the same session**. [STRONG] — [JCU record](https://researchonline.jcu.edu.au/71546/); [PDF](https://www.fisiologiadelejercicio.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/12/Compatibility-of-Concurrent-Aerobic-and-Strength-Training.pdf)
- Lundberg et al. 2022 (Sports Med 52:2391–2403; 15 studies): a small, borderline negative effect on **muscle fiber** hypertrophy (SMD −0.23). The type I fiber penalty appeared **only with running, not cycling** (SMD −0.81), based on few studies. [MODERATE/WEAK] — [SportRxiv preprint](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/download/37/71/54); [Fisiología del Ejercicio summary](https://www.fisiologiadelejercicio.com/compatibilidad-del-entrenamiento-concurrente-sobre-la-hipertrofia-y-la-fuerza-muscular/)

### Inferences
- For a young woman, being in a **"high" fitness tier** (above average for age; Mandsager showed no elite vs high difference in younger groups) is the realistic, sufficient target. CRF built at 22 is a reserve that declines with age, so starting high matters.
- Minimum effective dose: **1 hard interval session per week** (4×4 at about 90–95% HRmax with 3-min active recovery, about 25–30 min including warm-up) **or 2–3 SIT finishers** (3×20 s all-out, 10 min), plus daily walking or moderate activity to reach about 150 min/week moderate-equivalent. Vigorous intervals count about 2:1 toward the WHO target.
- Interference is small for hypertrophy. Practical rules: favor **cycling, rowing, or ski-erg** over running for high-intensity intervals; put intervals **after** lifting or in a separate session; avoid hard lower-body intervals right before a heavy leg or jump day, because explosive qualities are what suffer.
- Zone 2 is useful as easy volume, recovery, and step-count, but the Attia-style 3+ h/week of Zone 2 is not required for a time-limited lifter (see Q6).

### Gaps
- I found no trial data on minimum cardio dose specifically in young women who lift. Gillen 2016 and Helgerud 2007 studied men. Female SIT/HIIT trials exist but were not retrieved.
- The common "separate cardio and lifting by 3+ hours" rule was not retrieved as a primary citation this session. Schumann only shows that same-session work hurts explosive strength.

## 3. Muscle strength and power as longevity markers: should a 22-year-old train power?

### Takeaway
Grip and muscular strength strongly predict mortality (PURE: each 5 kg lower grip is linked to about 16% higher all-cause mortality). Relative muscle power (e.g., sit-to-stand power per kg) declines with age, accelerating after about 50, and somewhat faster in women. Power training at 22 is low-cost and pairs naturally with jump-for-bone work, but the direct longevity evidence for training power in young adults is weak.

### Cited Findings
- Leong et al. 2015 (Lancet 386:266–273; PURE, 139,691 adults aged 35–70 from 17 countries, median 4 y follow-up): **per 5 kg lower grip strength, HR 1.16 (1.13–1.20) for all-cause mortality**, 1.17 for CV mortality, 1.17 for non-CV mortality, 1.07 for MI, and 1.09 for stroke. Grip was a stronger predictor of all-cause and CV mortality than systolic blood pressure. The authors note it is unknown whether **improving** strength lowers risk. [STRONG association; causality unproven] — [Healio](https://www.healio.com/news/cardiology/20150821/grip-strength-may-predict-risk-for-mortality-cvd); [News-Medical](https://www.news-medical.net/news/20150514/Hand-grip-strength-could-be-used-as-a-predictor-of-stroke-and-heart-attack.aspx); [AKU record](https://scholars.aku.edu/en/publications/prognostic-value-of-grip-strength-findings-from-the-prospective-u/)
- Alcazar et al. 2021 (J Cachexia Sarcopenia Muscle; European cohort): **relative sit-to-stand (STS) power fell significantly after age 50** and was strongly associated with mobility limitations. [MODERATE] — [Zendy record](https://zendy.io/title/10.1002/jcsm.12737); [DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/f0054693f5bf4e24888c7e8abad09ced)
- Spanish cohort: relative STS power changed little at 30–50 y (−0.05 W/kg/y, not significant), declined faster at 50–80 y (−0.10 to −0.13 W/kg/y), and continued declining above 80. In women the negative association with age persisted **throughout old age**. The Toledo cohort (2025) found linear declines of **1.0%/y in men vs 1.5%/y in women**. [MODERATE] — [Nature Sci Rep PDF](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-023-39182-7.pdf); [DOAJ 2025](https://doaj.org/article/e41fe3b487f3490789d34f95f0e2587c)
- Muscle-strengthening activity was associated with 15% lower all-cause mortality (RR 0.85). Combined with aerobic activity it was associated with **40% lower** mortality (RR 0.60) vs neither. — [Momma et al. 2022, BJSM](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/56/13/755.abstract); [News-Medical](https://www.news-medical.net/news/20220301/30-60-minutesweek-of-muscle-strengthening-activities-may-lower-mortality-risk.aspx)

### Inferences
- A hypertrophy program already builds strength and grip if it includes heavy pulls, rows, and carries. Grip can be encoded as a byproduct: farmer carries, heavy holds, and pull-ups.
- **Power training at 22 is cheap and synergistic.** The jumps prescribed for bone are also power training. Adding 1–2 low-volume ballistic movements (kettlebell swings, medicine-ball throws, jump squats) costs about 5–8 min and little fatigue. The best-supported reason is bone plus athletic capacity and maintaining fast-twitch qualities. Direct "power at 22 leads to longer life" evidence does not exist.
- Olympic lifts are optional and technique-heavy. For an emailed, unsupervised program, simpler derivatives or substitutes (kettlebell swings, jump squats, med-ball throws, box jumps) give most of the stimulus at lower injury and technique risk (inference; no head-to-head data retrieved).

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve a primary source showing that **power declines faster than strength** with age (often cited from Skelton 1994 or Reid & Fielding 2012). The specific Alcazar 2018 paper was not retrieved.
- I found no trial showing that power training in young adulthood changes later-life power or mortality.
- No data were retrieved on grip strength norms or thresholds for young women.

## 4. Balance, single-leg stability, mobility, and loaded carries

### Takeaway
Simple functional tests (10-s one-leg stance; sit-to-floor-and-rise) predict mortality in middle-aged and older adults. At 22 these are maintenance qualities, not deficits, and most can be built as byproducts of unilateral lifting plus a few minutes of balance and floor-transition practice. The longevity evidence is observational and from older cohorts; there is no training trial in young women.

### Cited Findings
- Araujo et al. 2022 (BJSM 56:975; 1,702 adults aged 51–75, median 7 y): about 20% could not complete a **10-s one-leg stance**. Mortality was 17.5% in non-completers vs 4.6% in completers, with an adjusted HR of **1.84 (1.23–2.78)**. Adding the test to established risk factors improved mortality prediction. Recent falls and physical activity were not controlled. [MODERATE association] — [BJSM](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/56/17/975); [Healio](https://cm.healio.com/news/orthopedics/20220623/older-patients-unable-to-complete-onelegged-stance-test-may-have-high-mortality-risk); [Advisory Board](https://www.advisory.com/daily-briefing/2022/06/24/ols-test)
- Sitting-rising test (SRT), scored 0–10: Brito et al. 2012 (Eur J Prev Cardiol; over 2,000 adults aged 51–80) found **each 1-point increase associated with 21% lower mortality**. Araújo et al. 2025 (Eur J Prev Cardiol; 4,282 adults, mean age 59, 32% women, 12-y follow-up) found the lowest scorers had about 3.8× the natural-cause and about 6× the CV death risk of top scorers. 10-y survival was 97% (score 10), 88% (score 8), and 73% (score 0–4). Figures come from press coverage. [MODERATE association] — [ScienceDaily 2012](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2012/12/121213085202.htm); [News-Medical 2012](https://www.news-medical.net/news/20121214/Simple-screening-test-of-musculo-skeletal-fitness-proves-predictive-of-all-cause-mortality.aspx); [TCTMD 2025](https://www.tctmd.com/news/simple-sitting-rising-test-linked-natural-and-cv-mortality)

### Inferences
- A healthy, lifting 22-year-old will almost always pass these tests now. Their value is as **lifelong tracking metrics and habits**. The generator can include an occasional self-test (10-s one-leg stance eyes open and closed; SRT score) as a check-in.
- Minimum dose: **at least one unilateral lower-body exercise per lower-body session** (split squat, single-leg RDL, step-up, lunge variations), **1–2 min of single-leg balance** in warm-ups (progressing to eyes closed or unstable reach), and **loaded carries 1–2×/week** (2–4 × 20–40 m) as a finisher that also trains grip and trunk stiffness.
- Mobility: full-range lifting (deep squats, RDLs, overhead pressing) maintains most functional range. Add about 5 min of targeted mobility only where needed (hips, thoracic spine, ankles). Floor-transition practice (get-ups) is a cheap SRT-specific drill.

### Gaps
- I found no RCT or longevity data on **loaded carries** specifically; they are recommended on practitioner grounds (grip, trunk, work capacity). [OPINION]
- I found no data linking mobility or flexibility training in young adults to longevity outcomes.
- The balance and SRT cohorts are all middle-aged or older (Brazil, CLINIMEX); generalizing to 22-year-olds is extrapolation.

## 5. Injury prevention specific to young women: ACL, hamstring and groin, pelvic floor, shoulder

### Takeaway
Women have a higher ACL injury rate than men: about 1.5–2.2× overall and up to about 5.5× in high-impact landing or rotational sports. The often-quoted "2–8×" is not supported by recent meta-analyses. Multicomponent neuromuscular training (strength plus landing mechanics plus proximal/hip control, with feedback and good compliance) roughly halves ACL risk. Nordic hamstring curls are reasonable low-dose insurance, though the evidence is contested. High-impact work raises urinary-incontinence prevalence in female athletes, and pelvic-floor training helps. Shoulder-specific evidence was not retrieved.

### Cited Findings
**ACL risk**
- Montalvo et al. 2019 (BJSM; 58 studies): the female athletes' ACL injury rate per season was **1.7× that of males** (incidence proportion about 1.5×). With follow-up of 1 year or more, the sex difference was not significant (IRR 2.1, 95% CI 0.9–4.8). — [PubMed 29514822](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29514822/); [QxMD](https://read.qxmd.com/read/29514822/-what-s-my-risk-of-sustaining-an-acl-injury-while-playing-sports-a-systematic-review-with-meta-analysis)
- By sport type: no sex difference in collision sports. Females were at higher risk in contact sports, and the largest gap (**IRR 5.51**) was in fixed-object, high-impact rotational landing sports (e.g., gymnastics). In football (soccer) the female IR was 2.2× the male IR. **I found no 8-fold figure.** [STRONG] — [BJSM 53:1003](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/53/16/1003); [PMC6561829](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6561829)

**Neuromuscular training (NMT)**
- A 2019 systematic review and meta-analysis (18 studies, about 27,000 participants; appears to be Petushek et al., AJSM) found NMT **reduced ACL injury risk from 1 in 54 to 1 in 111 (OR 0.51, 0.37–0.69)**. Effects were larger in middle- and high-school athletes than in college or pro athletes. The authors recommend trained implementers and **lower-body strength exercises (Nordic hamstrings, lunges, heel/calf raises)**. [STRONG] — [PubMed 30001501](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30001501/)
- Sugimoto et al. 2016 meta-regression: **age, training dose, exercise variety, and verbal feedback** predicted ACL risk reduction. Including any one of these components reduced risk by about 17%. [MODERATE] — [PubMed 27251898](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27251898/)
- A 2015 analysis found **strength and proximal (hip/trunk) control** exercises gave the most benefit, with no significant reduction from balance-only or plyometric-only programs. Multi-component programs beat single-type programs. [MODERATE] — via search summary of [PEDro record](https://search.pedro.org.au/search-results/record-detail/57948)
- Compliance matters: high- vs low-compliance studies had an injury incidence rate ratio of **0.27 (0.07–0.80)**. [MODERATE] — [PMC3499896](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3499896)

**Hamstring and groin**
- van Dyk et al. 2019 (BJSM 53:1362; 15 studies, 8,459 athletes): programs including the **Nordic hamstring exercise halved hamstring injuries (RR 0.49)**. RCTs only gave RR 0.52, and excluding high risk-of-bias studies gave 0.55. [MODERATE] — [BJSM](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/53/21/1362); [QxMD](https://read.qxmd.com/read/30808663/including-the-nordic-hamstring-exercise-in-injury-prevention-programmes-halves-the-rate-of-hamstring-injuries-a-systematic-review-and-meta-analysis-of-8459-athletes); [PEDro summary](https://pedro.org.au/english/systematic-review-found-injury-prevention-programs-include-nordic-hamstring-exercises-decrease-risk-hamstring-injuries-among-soccer-players/)
- Contradicting reappraisal (J Clin Epidemiol 2021): restricting to RCTs gave **RR 0.59 (0.27–1.29)**, which is inconclusive and mostly from high risk-of-bias RCTs. [Conflict noted] — via search summary ([UTS repository](https://opus.lib.uts.edu.au/handle/10453/151354))
- Copenhagen adductor exercise: a 2025 meta-analysis (Quintana-Cepedal et al.) concluding against its effectiveness was **retracted** for misrepresenting outcomes, so it should not be cited either way. The positive Harøy et al. 2019 RCT (male footballers) was not retrieved. — via search summary ([sponet record](https://sponet.de/sponet/Record/4094912))

**Pelvic floor and high-impact work**
- Pires et al. 2020 (J Hum Kinet; 9 studies): pooled urinary incontinence (UI) prevalence in female athletes was **25.9%** (stress UI 20.7%). Volleyball was highest at 75.6%. [MODERATE] — [PMC7386138](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7386138)
- Teixeira et al. 2018 (Int Urogynecol J): pooled UI prevalence in female athletes was **36%**, with about 177% higher odds than sedentary women. — [Record](https://searchworks.jioinstitute.edu.in/articles/asx__133080637)
- A conference meta-analysis found high-impact sport UI prevalence of about 47.5%, with prevalence rising with ground impact (high heterogeneity). — [ICS 2021 abstract](https://www.ics.org/2021/abstract/98)
- A 2023 Sports Health meta-analysis notes UI ranges from about 11% (cycling) to 80% (trampolining). **Pelvic floor muscle training (PFMT) significantly increased pelvic floor strength and reduced leakage in athletes.** [MODERATE] — [PMC11346234](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11346234)
- A separate meta-analysis covers UI in female CrossFit athletes. — [PubMed 35635565](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35635565/)

### Inferences
- Even a gym-only trainee benefits from the **ACL-relevant components**: hip/posterior-chain strength, landing mechanics (soft, knees tracking over toes, no dynamic valgus), deceleration, and single-leg control. These align naturally with the jump-for-bone block, which can double as landing-mechanics practice with cueing text in the email.
- Nordic hamstring curls are low-cost (2–3 sets × 3–6 reps, 1–2×/week) and also build eccentric hamstring strength useful for hypertrophy. The injury-reduction evidence is moderate and contested, so encode them as "recommended", not "essential".
- **Pelvic floor safeguard for the impact block:** include a brief screening prompt ("any leakage during jumps, skipping, or heavy lifts?"). If yes, regress to lower-impact options (pogo hops, low box step-downs, heel drops) and suggest PFMT or a pelvic-health physiotherapist. Leakage is common, not "normal", and is trainable.
- Shoulder health (inference only): keep horizontal and vertical pulling volume at or above pressing volume and include some external-rotation or scapular work. This is standard practice, but no evidence was retrieved this session.

### Gaps
- FIFA 11+ trial data (e.g., Soligard 2008) were not retrieved.
- No evidence was retrieved on shoulder injury prevention in recreational female lifters.
- The ACL prevention literature is almost entirely in **sport-playing adolescents**. Whether NMT reduces injury in a non-sport gym trainee is untested; the benefit for this user mainly applies if she plays field or court sports or skis.
- Hormonal-contraceptive or menstrual-phase effects on ACL risk were not researched.

## 6. The Attia / Outlive "centenarian decathlon" framework: summary and critique

### Takeaway
Outlive's framing is a useful planning heuristic: train now for the physical tasks you want to do at 90, built on stability, strength, aerobic base (Zone 2), and VO2max. Its broad prescription (lift, do cardio, include high intensity) matches the evidence. It overreaches on Zone 2 as a uniquely essential intensity and on precise dose targets that are drawn from observational or athlete data.

### Cited Findings
- Clinician review: the decathlon's goal "is not simply to live to 95 but to retain function at that age". Clinicians use it to build plans around Zone 2 for mitochondrial density and metabolic flexibility and resistance training for muscle and insulin sensitivity. [OPINION] — [Clinician review](https://ivf-genesis-dnepr.ua/doctor/perelygin-vladimir-igorevich/publications/outlive-peter-attia-review/)
- Critique: Outlive's Zone 2 (near the first lactate threshold) is "pretty intense" and heavily carbohydrate-dependent for untrained people. For an unfit person, walking may already be Zone 1–2. [OPINION/blog] — [Deus ex Vita Substack](https://deusexvita.substack.com/p/review-1-of-2026-outlive-by-peter); [The Motte forum](https://www.themotte.org/post/3530/wellness-wednesday-for-january-28-2026/406948?context=8)
- Peer-reviewed counterweight: Storoschuk et al. 2025 (Sports Med) found Zone 2 is **not** supported as the optimal intensity for mitochondrial or fat-oxidation adaptations. Much of the Zone 2 emphasis comes from observational data in elite endurance athletes, and higher intensities matter more when volume is low. [MODERATE] — [PubMed 40560504](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40560504/); [UESCA](https://uesca.com/is-zone-2-really-the-endurance-panacea/); [Sigma Nutrition podcast](https://sigmanutrition.com/episode571/)
- Commentators stress the review is not "anti-Zone 2". Zone 2 is useful for base endurance and lipid regulation, but there is no evidence it is superior. — [UESCA](https://uesca.com/is-zone-2-really-the-endurance-panacea/); [Canyon Ranch](https://www.canyonranch.com/well-stated/post/zone-training-longevity-fitness)

### Inferences
- **What to keep:** the backward-planning idea (decide what you want to be able to do at 80–90 and build reserve now), the four-domain structure (stability, strength, aerobic base, VO2max), and the emphasis on muscle and CRF as top modifiable mortality predictors. All are consistent with Q2–Q4 evidence.
- **Where it overreaches:**
  1. Zone 2 volume targets (my recollection is about 3 h/week, not verified this session) are not evidence-based minima. For a lifter with limited time, high-intensity intervals give more VO2max per minute (Helgerud, Gillen, Storoschuk).
  2. Mortality associations (VO2max, grip, balance) are observational. "Moving from the bottom to the top quartile buys X" implies a causal effect not demonstrated in trials.
  3. Elite-tier VO2max goals: Mandsager found no elite vs high difference in younger adults, so "high" is enough.
  4. "Stability" as Attia uses it draws on practitioner methods such as DNS (dynamic neuromuscular stabilization), with little outcome evidence.
- **For the generator:** adopt the domains, not the specific hour targets.

### Gaps
- Outlive itself was not accessed. Its specific prescriptions (e.g., about 3 h/week Zone 2 plus 1 VO2max session/week; the "stability" components) are from my background knowledge and should be verified before quoting.
- I found no peer-reviewed critique of the decathlon framework as a whole; critiques found were blogs or forums, plus Storoschuk on Zone 2.

## 7. Guidelines and dose-response of strength training vs mortality: implications for someone training more

### Takeaway
WHO recommends at least 2 days/week of muscle strengthening plus 150–300 min moderate or 75–150 min vigorous aerobic activity. Observationally, the mortality benefit of muscle-strengthening peaks around 30–60 min/week, with a J-shaped curve beyond that. The upturn at high volumes is uncertain and likely confounded, so it is not a reason to cap training. It does mean the **marginal longevity value of extra lifting time is small**, and the time is better spent adding cardio, impact, and power.

### Cited Findings
- WHO 2020: 150–300 min moderate or 75–150 min vigorous aerobic per week, plus regular muscle strengthening (≥2 days/week) for all ages. — [BJSM full text](https://bjsm.bmj.com/node/121498.full); [Consultant360](https://www.consultant360.com/exclusive/primary-care/who-new-guidelines-physical-activity-and-sedentary-behavior)
- Momma et al. 2022 (BJSM 56:755–763; 16 prospective cohorts): muscle-strengthening activities were associated with **10–17% lower mortality** (all-cause RR 0.85). The **dose-response was J-shaped** for all-cause, CVD, and total cancer mortality, with the **maximum reduction (about 10–20%) at about 30–60 min/week**. Diabetes followed an L-shape. The authors say the effect of higher volumes is **"unclear"** and call for more diverse data. **Combined strength and aerobic activity: RR 0.60.** [MODERATE; observational, self-reported] — [BJSM abstract](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/56/13/755.abstract); [News-Medical](https://www.news-medical.net/news/20220301/30-60-minutesweek-of-muscle-strengthening-activities-may-lower-mortality-risk.aspx); [Hospital Healthcare](https://hospitalhealthcare.com/specialisms/cardiovascular/muscle-strengthening-activities-reduces-cvd-cancer-and-all-cause-mortality-risk/); [J-STAGE secondary publication](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/ree/24/2/24_2203/_article/-char/en)

### Inferences
- A 22-year-old doing 2–5 hypertrophy sessions (about 2–5 h/week) is well past the 30–60 min/week where most of the observational mortality benefit sits. The J-shape at high volumes rests on few high-volume participants, self-reported time, and no measures of intensity, drugs, or sports. It does **not** demonstrate harm from structured hypertrophy training, and RCTs show no adverse physiology at these volumes (inference).
- Practical consequence for the generator: lifting volume can stay at whatever hypertrophy requires. For longevity, though, the **largest gains come from the combination** (RR 0.60 for strength plus aerobic vs 0.85 for strength alone). Make sure conditioning, impact, and power are present every week rather than adding more sets.

### Gaps
- No RCT data on hard outcomes link strength-training dose to mortality in young women; all of this is cohort data, mostly from older adults.
- The ACSM progression position stand was not retrieved this session.

## 8. Recovery factors: sleep, energy availability (REDs), and iron in young women

### Takeaway
For a young woman training hard (especially if also dieting for leanness), **problematic low energy availability** is the main recovery threat to bone. It can cause irreversible BMD loss and directly undermines the peak-bone-mass goal. Iron deficiency is common in exercising women and clusters with low energy availability. Sleep matters but was not specifically researched here.

### Cited Findings
- IOC 2023 REDs consensus (Mountjoy et al., BJSM 2023, doi:10.1136/bjsports-2023-106994): REDs is impaired physiological and/or psychological function caused by **problematic (prolonged and/or severe) low energy availability (LEA)**, which sits on a spectrum from adaptable to problematic. **Bone health** is a key outcome, potentially including **permanent BMD loss**. Low carbohydrate availability accelerates REDs and is associated with poor bone health. The statement includes the IOC REDs CAT2 traffic-light clinical tool. [STRONG, consensus] — [Human Performance Alliance](https://humanperformancealliance.org/news/2023-international-olympic-committee-consensus-statement-on-reds/); [Mountjoy 2023 PDF](https://appliedsportpsych.org/site/assets/files/1113/mountjoy_et_al_-_2023_reds_update.pdf); [Amsterdam UMC record](https://pure.amsterdamumc.nl/en/publications/2023-international-olympic-committees-ioc-consensus-statement-on-/)
- Female athlete triad: athletes can develop **low BMD at a young age**, with recurrent bone or stress fractures. — [WTOP](https://wtop.com/news/2024/06/how-to-spot-and-prevent-the-female-athlete-triad-and-red-s)
- Iron: a 2026 review (Quality in Sport) calls iron deficiency the most prevalent nutritional deficit in female athletes, **up to 60%** depending on discipline, load, and menstrual status. That is a weak, high-range estimate. — [apcz.umk.pl](https://apcz.umk.pl/QS/article/view/74547)
- A 2021 MSSE study found low-iron markers associated with Triad risk factors, and suggested women with a history of anemia or iron supplementation be screened for low EA. [MODERATE] — [LIDA record](https://lida.sport-iat.de/twm/Record/4073927)
- A small case series found 46% of 13 highly trained female endurance athletes had sub-optimal iron. [WEAK] — [PubMed 36554486](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36554486/)

### Inferences
- The generator should include **non-diagnostic recovery prompts**: eat enough to support training; **loss of a regular menstrual cycle (not explained by hormonal contraception), recurrent bone stress injury, or unexplained fatigue are red flags** to see a clinician; consider periodic ferritin checks, especially with heavy periods, plant-based diets, or endurance work.
- Aggressive cutting phases plus high training volume, especially extra cardio added for fat loss, are the main risk scenario for a lean-muscle-focused woman. Encode a rule to avoid stacking high conditioning volume during prolonged deficits, and to keep impact and heavy loading in place during cuts, since bone needs mechanical signal plus energy.
- Sleep: standard adult guidance is 7–9 h/night. It was not researched this session, so treat it as background knowledge.

### Gaps
- Specific energy-availability thresholds (e.g., the classic <30 kcal/kg FFM/day cut-off and how the 2023 IOC statement de-emphasizes a single threshold) were not retrieved in detail.
- No primary population estimate was retrieved for iron deficiency prevalence in **non-athlete** young women in a high-income country (often cited around 20–40% depending on ferritin cut-off; unverified here).
- No sleep-and-hypertrophy or sleep-and-injury evidence was retrieved.

## 9. Parameters to encode in the workout generator (synthesis)

### Takeaway
Keep hypertrophy as the core. Every week, add a small, sequenced dose of (a) jumps/impact, (b) heavy low-rep compound sets, (c) hard intervals, (d) power, (e) unilateral/balance/carries, and (f) injury-prevention accessories. Place explosive and impact work first, conditioning last or separate, and prefer cycling or rowing for intervals. The whole package costs about 15–30 min/week beyond the hypertrophy work.

### Cited Findings
(Parameters are derived from the findings above. Primary support: [Kato 2006](https://search.pedro.org.au/search-results/record-detail/14578), [jumping meta-analysis 2014](https://www.springermedicine.com/efficiency-of-jumping-exercise-in-improving-bone-mineral-density/22254016), [BYU 10–20 jumps RCT](https://scholarsarchive.byu.edu/etd/667), [ESSA summary](https://wildphysiofitness.au/blogs/physiotherapy/exercise-for-bone-health), [LIFTMOR](https://academic.oup.com/jbmr/article/33/2/211/7605709), [Robling rest-inserted loading](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11606612/), [Helgerud 2007](https://coachsci.sdsu.edu/csa/vol131/helgerud.htm), [Gillen 2016](https://doaj.org/article/0d8f38860885479c87ee1ba61460002b), [WHO 2020](https://bjsm.bmj.com/node/121498.full), [Schumann 2022](https://researchonline.jcu.edu.au/71546/), [Lundberg 2022](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/download/37/71/54), [ACL NMT meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30001501/), [Sugimoto meta-regression](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27251898/), [van Dyk NHE](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/53/21/1362), [PFMT in athletes](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11346234), [IOC REDs 2023](https://humanperformancealliance.org/news/2023-international-olympic-committee-consensus-statement-on-reds/).)

### Inferences

**A. Weekly impact/jump dose (bone)**
- Target: **about 100–200 impacts/week spread over at least 3 days**, about 30–60 per gym session, in sets of 5–10 with **10–20 s rest between individual jumps or short clusters** (rest-inserted; Robling 14-s finding, animal data).
- Sessions/week mapping:
  - **2 sessions:** 50–60 jumps each, plus an optional "home jump snack" (10–20 jumps) on 1–3 non-gym days, at least 4–8 h from other loading if same day.
  - **3 sessions:** 40–50 each.
  - **4–5 sessions:** 25–40 on each session (or on lower-body days only, 40–50 each).
- Exercise menu, **multidirectional and progressive:** countermovement jumps and squat jumps, broad jumps, lateral bounds or skater hops, single-leg hops (forward, lateral), tuck jumps, drop landings, then drop jumps from 20 cm progressing to 30–40 cm, plus jump rope or pogo bursts as a low tier.
- Progression: start with about 20–30 low-height bilateral jumps per session for 2–3 weeks, then add volume and progress height, unilateral, and multidirectional difficulty. Change the jump mix every 2–4 weeks for strain novelty.
- Regressions for pelvic-floor symptoms or joint pain: pogo hops, heel drops, step-downs, and lower volume.
- **Heavy axial loading for bone:** at least **2 exposures/week of 3–5 sets × 3–6 reps at about 80–85%+ 1RM (RPE 8)** on a squat or deadlift pattern, plus an overhead press variant (LIFTMOR-style). These can be the first compound of the day inside the hypertrophy program.

**B. Weekly conditioning dose and format (VO2max/CRF)**
- Floor: **1 VO2max session/week**, either a Norwegian 4×4 (4 × 4 min at about 90–95% HRmax or RPE 8–9, 3 min easy between; about 25–30 min with warm-up) or 2–3 SIT finishers/week (3 × 20 s all-out, 2 min easy between, 10 min total).
- Plus **daily walking or easy cardio** so total weekly activity reaches about 150 min moderate-equivalent; vigorous minutes count double.
- By frequency:
  - **2 sessions/week:** SIT finisher after both sessions (no dedicated cardio day), plus a walking target.
  - **3 sessions:** 1× 4×4 (separate day or after upper-body session) plus 1× SIT finisher.
  - **4–5 sessions:** 1× 4×4 plus 1× SIT or 20–40 min easy Zone 2 on an off-day or after an upper session.
- Modality default: **bike, rower, ski-erg, incline walk**. Limit high-intensity running volume when hypertrophy of the legs is a priority (Lundberg: type I fiber penalty only with running; weak evidence).
- Optional test: periodic 12-min or 5-km effort, or a heart-rate-based fitness estimate, to track CRF.

**C. Power work dose**
- The jumps above already count as power work.
- Add **1 ballistic upper/total-body movement 1–2×/week**: kettlebell swings 3–5 × 6–10, med-ball chest pass or rotational throws 3 × 5/side, or jump squats with light load 3 × 5.
- Low reps, full rest (60–120 s), stop sets before speed drops.
- Olympic lift derivatives (hang power clean/snatch, jump shrug) are **optional**; only offer them if the user opts in and knows the technique.

**D. Balance, carries, and mobility dose**
- **At least 1 unilateral lower-body exercise per lower-body session** (split squat, single-leg RDL, step-up, lunge).
- **Warm-up balance block 1–2 min, 2–3×/week:** single-leg stance progressing to eyes closed, single-leg reach, or balance on an unstable surface.
- **Loaded carries 1–2×/week:** farmer, suitcase, or front-rack carries, 2–4 × 20–40 m heavy, as a finisher. Doubles as grip training.
- **Mobility:** prioritize full-ROM lifting. Add about 5 min of targeted mobility (hips, ankles, thoracic) in warm-ups as needed, and occasional floor get-ups.
- **Quarterly self-tests** (optional email prompts): 10-s one-leg stance eyes closed, sit-rise score, grip (if a dynamometer is available), conditioning benchmark.

**E. Injury-prevention inclusions**
- **NMT-style warm-up (8–12 min) at the start of every lower-body session:** glute/hip activation, lateral band walks, landing-mechanics drills (stick landings: soft knees, knees over toes, no valgus, hips back), deceleration, and single-leg control. Include **verbal feedback cues in the email text**, since feedback is a predictor of effect.
- **Nordic hamstring curls, 1–2×/week:** 2–3 sets × 3–6 reps (assisted or eccentric-only to start).
- **Copenhagen adductor plank or side-lying adduction, 1–2×/week:** 2–3 sets (short-lever to long-lever progression). Weak evidence for this population; optional.
- **Calf and Achilles work:** heavy calf raises 2×/week. This supports the impact work; heel/calf raises were among the NMT components.
- **Shoulder (practice-based):** keep weekly pulling sets at or above pushing sets, and do face pulls or external-rotation work 1–2×/week.
- **Pelvic floor:** before progressing impact, ask once per block about leakage. Offer PFMT cues and a regression path, and refer to a pelvic-health physio if symptoms persist.

**F. Sequencing rules to protect hypertrophy and power**
1. Session order: **NMT/balance warm-up, then jumps and power (fresh, before fatigue), then heavy compound (80–85%+ 1RM), then hypertrophy work, then carries/core, then conditioning finisher.**
2. Same-session conditioning goes **after** lifting. Hard intervals ideally go on a separate day, or at least several hours apart. Same-session concurrent work mainly blunts explosive strength (Schumann 2022).
3. Do not schedule a hard leg-intensive interval session within about 24 h before a heavy lower-body or jump session; put leg-heavy intervals after an upper-body day or on an off-day.
4. **Cap high-intensity conditioning at about 2–3 sessions/week**. Count jumps toward lower-body fatigue and don't add plyometric volume on top of high-volume leg days without reducing sets.
5. **Do not drop impact or heavy loading during fat-loss phases**. Do avoid adding large cardio volume during a prolonged calorie deficit (REDs risk).
6. Recovery prompts: eat enough to support training, sleep about 7–9 h, and see a clinician for a missed or irregular cycle, recurrent bone pain, or persistent fatigue (consider a ferritin check).

### Gaps
- Exact per-session jump counts for **gym-based hypertrophy trainees aged 18–30** have not been tested in an RCT. Doses above interpolate from Kato (10/day × 3/week), Bassey-type protocols (50/day × 6/week), BYU (10–20 twice daily), and ESSA (about 50 per session, 4–7 days).
- The ≥24-h and "several hours" separation rules are practitioner rules, not trial-derived thresholds.
- No research was retrieved on menstrual-cycle-phase periodization of these levers. Current evidence was not checked this session, but this is a common question to address separately.
