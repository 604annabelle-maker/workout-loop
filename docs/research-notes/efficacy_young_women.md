# Efficacy of jump (impact) training for bone in premenopausal young adult women (18–35), and its relevance at age 22

> Method note for the report writer: in this session NCBI (PubMed/PMC), Europe PMC, Springer, journals.physiology.org and other publisher hosts were blocked by the network sandbox. Every number below comes from abstract text or excerpts that came back through web search, not from full texts I read. Where a figure came only from a secondary source or my own arithmetic, I say so. Evidence grades: **strong** = consistent meta-analytic or large-cohort evidence; **moderate** = a single decent RCT or a meta-analysis with caveats; **weak** = small, short or unpublished trials, or secondary-source figures; **opinion** = expert view or modelling.
> Population flags: [YA] = young adult women about 18–30; [PRE-older] = premenopausal women about 30–50; [POST] = postmenopausal; [ADOL] = children or adolescents.

## Q1. When is peak bone mass reached at the hip and spine in women, and is bone still gained or consolidated between 20 and 30?

### Takeaway
Peak bone mass is reached at different ages at different sites. In women the hip (total hip, femoral neck) peaks around ages 16–20, so a 22-year-old's hip is at or just past its peak. The lumbar spine keeps gaining into the mid-20s to late 30s (CaMos: 33–40). The 20s are therefore a consolidation window. Some modest gain is still possible, mostly at the spine and whole body, and the hip is maintained more than built.

### Cited Findings
- **CaMos longitudinal data (Berger et al. 2010, JBMR 25:1948–57; n=615 women and 527 men aged 16–40).** In women, **lumbar spine peak BMD (1.046 ± 0.123 g/cm²) occurred at ages 33–40**, and **total hip peak BMD (0.981 ± 0.122 g/cm²) at ages 16–19**. The authors conclude that the age at peak "varies by sex and skeletal site". Grade: **moderate–strong** (large Canadian cohort, longitudinal). — [Berger 2010 abstract (UMN Experts)](https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/peak-bone-mass-from-longitudinal-data-implications-for-the-preval/); [Endocrinology Advisor summary](https://www.endocrinologyadvisor.com/news/age-at-attainment-of-peak-bmd-varies-by-sex-and-skeletal-site/)
- A separate CaMos analysis (Berger et al. 2008, CMAJ) found that **bone loss at all 3 measured sites began in women at age 40–44**, so the 20s and 30s are a plateau or consolidation period, not a period of loss. Grade: **moderate**. — [Berger 2008 CMAJ (RRM Academy listing)](https://rrmacademy.org/library/change-in-bone-mineral-density-as-a-function-of-age-in-women-and-men-and-associa-recuovpgnb4yfxood/)
- Lin et al. 2003 (Bone, "Peak spine and femoral neck bone mass in young women") found the **highest BMD at 23.0 ± 1.4 y at the spine, 18.5 ± 1.6 y at the femoral neck, 14.2 ± 2.0 y at the greater trochanter and 15.8 ± 2.1 y at Ward's triangle**. Grade: **moderate**. Sample size not retrieved. — [Lin 2003, Bone (ScienceDirect)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S8756328203000620)
- **Recker et al. 1992 (JAMA 268:2403–8)** tested whether bone mass rises in healthy women after linear growth stops. It concluded that **"gain in bone mass occurs in healthy young women during the third decade of life"** and that **physical activity and dietary calcium both had a positive effect on this gain**. Oral contraceptive use was associated with greater total-body gain (r=.31, P=.01). A secondary source gives the cohort as 156 women aged about 20–30. Grade: **moderate** (prospective cohort; my secondary source gave no per-site % figures). — [Recker 1992, JAMA abstract](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/article-abstract/400945)
- **Weaver et al. 2016, NOF position statement (Osteoporos Int):** defines peak bone mass as the bone gained "by the time a stable skeletal state is reached in young adulthood". At population level it is attained when age-related change "is no longer positive and ha[s] attained a plateau or maximum value". A secondary source says the statement places attainment in the axial skeleton and proximal femur "by the end of the second decade of life". That wording is not verified against the paper. Grade: **strong** for the definition (systematic-review-based position statement). The statement's evidence base is overwhelmingly [ADOL]. — [Weaver 2016 (PubMed)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26856587/); [Springer full record](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00198-015-3440-3)

### Inferences
- At 22, the hip has essentially reached peak (CaMos 16–19; Lin 18.5 for femoral neck), while the spine may still be rising (Lin 23; CaMos 33–40). Any exercise at 22 therefore works mainly by **adding a small increment and consolidating or maintaining** at the hip, with somewhat more room at the spine.
- The two cohorts disagree on spine peak age (23 vs 33–40). Differences in population, method (cross-sectional vs longitudinal modelling) and definition probably explain this. The report should present a range rather than a single "peak age".
- Recker 1992 supports the idea that physical activity in the 20s still affects bone. That fits "the 20s still count" but does not show a specific effect of jumping.

### Gaps
- **Lu et al. (NHANES-based age-of-peak analysis)**: no retrievable data in this session. Not verified.
- **Recker 1992 per-site magnitudes:** figures commonly quoted from memory (roughly 5% forearm, 6–7% spine and 12–13% total-body BMC gained across the third decade) could not be confirmed. They should not be cited unless the JAMA full text is checked.
- Weaver 2016's exact wording on age of attainment and its evidence grades for physical activity (grade A in children and adolescents, from memory) were not retrieved. I found no statement in it that grades exercise evidence specifically for ages 18–30.

## Q2. RCTs and meta-analyses of jump or high-impact training in premenopausal adult women: sites, effect sizes, duration and adherence

### Takeaway
Across RCTs in premenopausal women, high-impact or jump training reliably produces **small, site-specific gains, mainly at the femoral neck and hip, of about +1% to +3% over 5–18 months** relative to controls. Spine effects are inconsistent. Only one well-known RCT (Kato 2006) was done squarely in women about 21 years old. Most of the classic trials (Heinonen, Vainionpää, Winters & Snow, Tucker) enrolled premenopausal women in their late 20s to 40s, not menopausal women. Pooled meta-analyses support a femoral-neck benefit and a weaker or null lumbar-spine benefit from jumping alone.

### Cited Findings
**Meta-analyses (premenopausal women)**
- **Zhao, Zhao & Zhang 2014 (Sports Med 44:1393–1402), jumping-only RCTs in premenopausal women.** Jumping **significantly increased femoral-neck BMD: weighted mean difference (WMD) 0.017 g/cm²**, with a significant gain at the trochanter. The **lumbar spine "seemed to benefit less" (p = 0.181, not significant)**. The response was "site-specific", with the "largest benefit at the hip". Searches ran up to September 2013. Grade: **moderate** (meta-analysis of small RCTs). — [Zhao 2014 (PubMed)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24981245/); [DARE appraisal](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK291433/); [Springer record](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-014-0220-8)
- **Martyn-St James & Carroll 2010 (J Bone Miner Metab 28:251–67), controlled trials of impact exercise in premenopausal women.** **High-impact-only protocols were effective at the femoral neck (WMD 0.024 g/cm²).** **Combined protocols (odd- or high-impact plus high-magnitude resistance exercise) increased BMD at both the lumbar spine and the femoral neck.** Caveats: funnel-plot asymmetry for positive outcomes, which suggests publication bias; too few protocols measured total hip; "diverse methodological and reporting discrepancies". Grade: **moderate**. — [Martyn-St James & Carroll 2010 (PubMed)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20013013/); [DARE appraisal](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK80517/)
- **Kelley, Kelley & Kumar 2013 (meta-analysis of RCTs of exercise of any type in premenopausal women)** concluded that exercise benefits femoral-neck and lumbar-spine BMD. Pooled numbers were not retrieved. Grade: **moderate**. — [Kelley 2013 (PMC)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3563173/)

**Individual RCTs**
- **Kato et al. 2006 (J Appl Physiol 100:839–43), [YA]: the trial most relevant to a 22-year-old.** 36 female college students, **mean age 20.7 ± 0.7 y**. **10 maximal vertical jumps per day, 3 days a week, for 6 months**, vs control. **Compliance 82%** (about 2.5 sessions a week). Femoral-neck BMD rose **from 0.984 to 1.010 g/cm² (P < 0.01; about +2.6%, my calculation from the abstract values)**. Lumbar spine (L2–4) BMD rose **from 0.991 to 1.015 (P < 0.01; about +2.4%, my calculation)**. Controls had no significant change. The authors concluded that low-repetition, high-impact jumps enhanced BMD at specific sites "in young women who had nearly reached peak bone mass". Grade: **moderate** (single small RCT, but the right age group). — [Kato 2006 (PubMed)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16269526/); [J Appl Physiol](https://journals.physiology.org/doi/full/10.1152/japplphysiol.00666.2005)
- **Heinonen et al. 1996 (Lancet 348:1343–7), [PRE-older].** Sedentary premenopausal women; ages commonly cited as 35–45, not re-verified here. Progressive high-impact exercise **3 times a week for 18 months**. **Femoral-neck BMD +1.6% (95% CI 0.8–2.4) in trainees vs +0.6% (−0.2 to 1.4) in controls, p = 0.006.** The authors cautioned that "long-term studies are required" to show lasting benefit. Grade: **moderate** (RCT published in the Lancet; n about 98, not re-verified). — [Heinonen 1996 (PubMed)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8918277/); [Lancet PDF](https://thelancet.com/pdfs/journals/lancet/PIIS0140-6736(96)04214-6.pdf)
- **Heinonen trial hip structural analysis with 3.5-year follow-up, [PRE-older].** After the 18-month intervention, trainees had significantly larger femoral-neck **section modulus (Z) +3.2% (p = 0.047)** and **cross-sectional area +2.8% (p = 0.043)** than controls. Grade: **moderate** (secondary analysis of an RCT). — [Physiotherapy Canada, HSA follow-up](https://utppublishing.com/doi/10.3138/ptc.2010-37)
- **Vainionpää et al. 2005 (Osteoporos Int), 12-month RCT in premenopausal women, [PRE-older].** Ages roughly 35–40 from memory, not verified. A search summary attributes a **12-month femoral-neck change of +1.1% vs −0.4% in controls (p = 0.003)** to this trial. Adherence and other site results were not retrieved. Grade: **moderate**, with the figure flagged for verification. — [Vainionpää 2005 (Springer)](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00198-004-1659-5)
- **Vainionpää et al. 2006 (Osteoporos Int), "Intensity of exercise is associated with bone density change in premenopausal women",** a secondary analysis that used accelerometers to measure dose. Per the title, the **intensity** of impacts (rather than the volume of activity) tracked BMD change. Numeric thresholds were not retrieved. Grade: **moderate**. — [Vainionpää 2006 (Springer)](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00198-005-0005-x)
- **Bassey et al. 1998 (JBMR 13:1805), premenopausal vs postmenopausal women on the same jumping programme** (about 50 jumps a day per secondary sources). **Premenopausal women gained about 2–3% at the femoral neck and trochanter after about 5 months; one review gives femoral-neck +2.8%.** **Postmenopausal women, with or without HRT, showed no BMD change.** The accompanying editorial noted that it is unknown why the older women failed to respond. Grade: **moderate** (figures from secondary sources). — [Marcus 1998 JBMR editorial "Exercise: Moving in the Right Direction"](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1359/jbmr.1998.13.12.1793)
- **Tucker et al. 2015 (Am J Health Promot 29:158–64), [PRE, ages 25–50].** 60 completers. **10 or 20 jumps twice daily, with 30 s rest between jumps, for 16 weeks.** The **20-jump group had significantly greater hip-BMD gains than controls**, and the 10-jump group showed **"marginally greater"** gains. Grade: **weak–moderate** (small, short). — [Tucker 2015 (SAGE)](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.4278/ajhp.130430-QUAN-200)
- **Winters & Snow 2000 (JBMR 15:2495–503), [PRE-older, ages 30–45].** 12 months of **100 jumps plus 100 lower-body resistance reps, 3 times a week**, progressing with weighted vests to 10% (jumps) and 13% (resistance) of body weight. 29 exercisers vs 22 controls. Training improved hip BMD, strength and power, and these reversed with detraining (see Q4). Grade: **moderate**. — [Winters & Snow 2000 (Scilit record)](https://scilit.com/publications/73c58583432c5a6a7afcb3844dde13fb)
- **Kato's group, postmenopausal low-repetition jumping study, [POST]:** concluded that it "may be effective especially [at the] proximal femur region for postmenopausal women". This is flagged as a different population. Grade: **weak**. — [KAKEN grant report](https://kaken.nii.ac.jp/en/grant/KAKENHI-PROJECT-18500470)

### Inferences
- The jump RCT evidence base in women is mostly in **premenopausal** women, and the one dedicated trial in about-21-year-olds (Kato 2006) was positive. The idea that jumping is "mainly a menopause intervention" is not supported by where the trials were done. Bassey 1998 suggests the **premenopausal skeleton responds better** to the same jumping than the postmenopausal one.
- The effect size is modest: about 0.017–0.024 g/cm² pooled at the femoral neck, roughly 1.5–2.5% of a typical young-adult femoral-neck BMD of about 1.0 g/cm² (my arithmetic). Per-trial gains are about 1–3% relative to controls.
- The doses that worked were small: 10 maximal jumps 3 times a week (Kato), or 10–20 jumps twice daily (Tucker). The time cost is minutes per week.
- The hip is the most consistently responsive site to jumping alone. The spine responds more reliably when impact is combined with heavy resistance (Martyn-St James & Carroll).

### Gaps
- **Niu et al. 2010** (office-based brief high-impact exercise in premenopausal women, Sendai) could not be retrieved. No data.
- **Babatunde et al. 2012** (meta-analysis of brief high-impact exercise in premenopausal women) could not be retrieved or verified.
- **Friedlander 1995** numeric site results were not retrieved (see Q3).
- Pooled k and n, and 95% CIs, for the Zhao 2014 and Martyn-St James 2010 meta-analyses were not retrieved. Kelley 2013 pooled numbers were not retrieved.
- Heinonen 1996 lumbar-spine and other-site results, and its exact n and age range, were not re-verified. Bassey 1998 premenopausal age range and exact protocol were not verified (only secondary sources).
- I found no RCT in already-resistance-trained young women testing whether adding jumps helps (see Q3).

## Q3. Does resistance training alone build or maintain bone in young women, and does adding impact give additional benefit?

### Takeaway
Resistance training alone has modest RCT evidence of increasing **lumbar-spine** BMD in women about 19–22. Its effect at the hip is mixed: Mosti found a small total-hip gain, Snow-Harter found no femoral change. Head-to-head trials of impact vs resistance in young women are few and small and point in different directions. A 12-month trial favoured step-aerobic impact at the heel, with strength training showing no significant gain at any measured site. The OPTIMA-Ex trial (ages 18–30) found both work at the spine, with resistance training better at the proximal femur and impact better at distal limb sites. The meta-analytic signal is that combining impact with heavy resistance covers both the spine and the hip best.

### Cited Findings
- **Snow-Harter et al. 1992 (JBMR 7:761), [YA, mean age about 19.9].** 8-month RCT: control vs progressive jogging vs weight lifting; **31 completers**. **Both running and resistance training "modestly increased lumbar spine" bone mineral**. **Femoral measures did not change significantly in any group.** Grade: **weak–moderate** (small RCT). — [Snow-Harter 1992 (PubMed)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1642145/); [JBMR abstract](https://academic.oup.com/jbmr/article-abstract/7/7/761/7500402)
- **Mosti et al. 2014 (J Strength Cond Res 28(10)), [YA, 22 ± 2 y].** 30 healthy women randomised. **12 weeks of squat maximal strength training at 85–90% 1RM**, with high concentric acceleration. In the training group, **lumbar-spine BMD +2.2% and total-hip BMD +1.0%**, serum P1NP +26.2%, and 1RM +97.7%. The authors suggest squat MST "may serve as a simple, time-efficient strategy to optimize peak bone mass in early adulthood". Grade: **weak–moderate** (small, short). — [Mosti 2014 (PubMed)](https://www.pubmed.gov/24736773/); [NTNU Open](https://ntnuopen.ntnu.no/ntnu-xmlui/handle/11250/263187)
- **"Effect of high-impact aerobics and strength training on BMD in young women aged 20–35 years" (PubMed 21165807), [YA–PRE], a head-to-head trial.** Over 12 months, **step-aerobic impact exercise increased heel (calcaneal) aBMD by 4.4%**, while **strength training showed no significant aBMD increase at any measured site**. Lumbar-spine and femoral-neck values were not reported in the retrieved summary. Grade: **weak–moderate**. — [PubMed 21165807](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21165807/)
- **OPTIMA-Ex trial (Lambert, Beck, Weeks et al., Griffith University), [YA, ages 18–30, T-score ≤ 0], a head-to-head trial.** **10 months, twice weekly**: supervised high-intensity impact training (IT), supervised high-intensity resistance training (RT), or a home-based low-intensity control. Completers: **IT n=10, RT n=12**. Preliminary results: **"both RT and IT improve spine bone mass"**. **IT increased distal radius and tibia pQCT BMD and bone strength index more than RT.** **RT had stronger effects at the proximal femur and along long-bone shafts**, and the authors suggest RT may have the broader overall effect. The authors stress "minimal statistical power". Grade: **weak** (very small, preliminary). — [OPTIMA-Ex preliminary findings](https://research-repository.griffith.edu.au/items/05f7ef03-a1e8-4476-8248-6c4996d36fb1); [OPTIMA-Ex comparison paper record](https://research-repository.griffith.edu.au/items/ead64eb4-370a-4459-a8b7-e30cbf43dbfa); [Protocol, BMJ Open 2017](https://bmjopen.bmj.com/content/7/9/e016983); [ANZCTR registration](https://anzctr.org.au/ACTRN12616001444471.aspx)
- OPTIMA-Ex also measured **landing impact intensities of jump exercises in trained vs untrained women**, which is relevant to dosing jumps for someone who already lifts. Results not retrieved. — [Landing impact intensities, OPTIMA-Ex (ResearchGate)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/335086267_Landing_Impact_Intensities_for_Jumping_Exercises_From_the_OPTIMA-Ex_Trial_in_Trained_and_Untrained_Women)
- **Friedlander et al. 1995, [YA–PRE, ages 20–35].** A **2-year combined aerobics plus weight-training programme had "beneficial effects on BMD"**. Site-level numbers were not retrieved. Grade: **moderate** (2-year RCT). — [Friedlander 1995 (PubMed)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7610928/)
- **Lohman et al. 1995, "Effects of resistance training on regional and total BMD in premenopausal women: a randomized prospective study".** Exists, but its results were not retrieved. — [PubMed 7484276](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7484276/)
- **Meta-analytic comparison (Martyn-St James & Carroll 2010):** combined impact plus high-magnitude resistance protocols improved **both the lumbar spine and the femoral neck**, whereas impact-only protocols improved the femoral neck. Grade: **moderate**. — [Martyn-St James & Carroll 2010 (PubMed)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20013013/)

### Inferences
- For a woman who already lifts 2–4 times a week, heavy lower-body lifting probably already covers much of the spine stimulus. The trials suggest jumps add a **different, site-specific stimulus**: high-rate, high-magnitude loading at the hip (Kato, Heinonen, Zhao) and the distal limbs (OPTIMA-Ex, the heel trial).
- Whether jumps add measurable benefit **on top of** an established heavy-lifting routine has **not been directly tested** in trained young women. The best available inference is the combined-protocol meta-analytic result, which is indirect and of moderate certainty.
- The head-to-head trials conflict (heel trial: impact better; OPTIMA-Ex: resistance better at the proximal femur), are tiny, and measured different sites. Neither modality has been shown to be clearly superior for the hip in young women.

### Gaps
- No adequately powered RCT compares "resistance alone" with "resistance plus jumps" in already-trained women aged 18–30.
- OPTIMA-Ex final DXA % changes (lumbar spine, femoral neck) could not be retrieved, nor could the exact 2020 journal citation of its main results.
- I found no well-specified trial data on what the person's current type of lifting (exercise selection, load, whether it includes deadlifts or squats) adds at the hip.

## Q4. Is the benefit lasting (detraining, the "bone bank" idea), and does peak bone mass gained in the 20s matter for later osteoporosis risk?

### Takeaway
Exercise-induced BMD gains in premenopausal women appear to **regress when training stops**: Winters & Snow 2000 found a return toward baseline within 6 months. This argues for jumping as a habit to maintain rather than a one-off "deposit". Some structural benefit was still detectable after the Heinonen 18-month programme, but the long-term follow-up results were not retrieved. A higher peak bone mass is modelled as the single biggest lever on when osteoporosis begins (Hernandez 2003: +10% peak BMD gives about 13 years' delay). That figure is a model prediction. Exercise in the 20s produces about 1–3%, not 10%.

### Cited Findings
- **Winters & Snow 2000 (JBMR 15:2495–503), [PRE-older, ages 30–45].** After 12 months of jump plus resistance training (n=29 exercisers vs 22 controls), **6 months of detraining caused BMD, muscle strength and power to fall back toward baseline, while controls were unchanged**. The authors concluded that the benefits **reverse when training stops**. Grade: **moderate**. — [Winters & Snow 2000 (Scilit)](https://scilit.com/publications/73c58583432c5a6a7afcb3844dde13fb); [Bohrium record](https://bohrium.dp.tech/paper/arxiv/11127215)
- **Heinonen trial HSA follow-up:** femoral-neck section modulus +3.2% and CSA +2.8% vs controls after the 18-month intervention, with participants followed to 3.5 years. Whether these advantages persisted at 3.5 years was not retrieved. Grade: **moderate**, as an RCT secondary analysis. — [Physiotherapy Canada HSA follow-up](https://utppublishing.com/doi/10.3138/ptc.2010-37)
- **Hernandez, Beaupré & Carter 2003 (Osteoporos Int 14:843–7):** a bone-remodelling computer model predicts that **a 10% increase in peak BMD delays osteoporosis onset by about 13 years**, whereas a 10% change in menopause age or in non-menopausal bone loss shifts onset by only about 2 years. The authors conclude that peak BMD "may be the most important single factor" in the development of osteoporosis. Grade: **opinion/modelling**, not observed outcome data. — [Hernandez 2003 (Springer DOI record)](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s00198-003-1454-8); [DeepDyve abstract](https://www.deepdyve.com/lp/doc/1F0HP2gmYZ)
- **Berger 2010 (CaMos):** using longitudinally derived peak bone mass changed estimated osteoporosis prevalence in women aged 50 and over to **12.0% at L1–L4 and 9.1% at the total hip**. This shows that the reference peak matters for diagnosis. Grade: **moderate**. — [Berger 2010](https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/peak-bone-mass-from-longitudinal-data-implications-for-the-preval/)

### Inferences
- The "bone bank" framing is only partly supported for adult women. The best detraining RCT (Winters & Snow) found gains were **not banked** after 6 months off. The case for jumping at 22 is therefore stronger as a **sustained habit** (low time cost) than as a one-time deposit.
- Scaling Hernandez's model linearly is speculative, but it suggests that a 1–3% increase in peak BMD would buy about 1–4 years' delay in osteoporosis onset. That is a non-trivial but modest effect, and only if it is retained. Treat this as an illustrative inference, not a finding.
- Childhood and adolescent loading has the best evidence for lasting effects (the Weaver 2016 evidence base is mainly [ADOL]). Evidence that adult-onset exercise gains persist for decades is lacking.

### Gaps
- I could not retrieve the 3.5-year follow-up results of the Heinonen trial. From memory, a Kontulainen et al. 2004 follow-up reported residual femoral-neck benefits in former trainees. If confirmed, that would partly contradict Winters & Snow. Unverified; the writer should flag the conflict as unresolved.
- I found no long-term (more than 5 years) follow-up of jump trials in women aged 18–30, and no fracture-outcome data from any premenopausal exercise trial.
- I found no observational data directly linking exercise in the 20s to fracture risk at 60 and above.

## Q5. Is there evidence that jump training is not useful, or not worth it, at this age?

### Takeaway
I found no evidence that jumping is harmful or futile for bone in healthy 22-year-olds. The "against" evidence is about **limits**:
- effects are small, site-specific and mostly at the hip, with jumping alone weak or null at the spine;
- short programmes (8 weeks) show nothing;
- possible publication bias inflates pooled estimates;
- gains fade with detraining;
- one small head-to-head trial suggests resistance training may matter more for the proximal femur.

Jumping is "for her stage" in the sense that most of the positive premenopausal jump trials were in her age range or slightly older. Its marginal value on top of existing heavy lifting is unproven.

### Cited Findings
- **8-week maximal jumping in college-aged women, [YA]** (thesis, University of Oklahoma repository). **10 maximal jumps, 5 days a week, for 8 weeks** produced **no significant DXA changes**. The authors suggested a longer intervention is needed for bone formation to show. Grade: **weak** (unpublished, short). — [ShareOK thesis record](https://shareok.org/items/0f84c622-2378-4325-9472-e90e53e16f02)
- **Zhao 2014:** jumping did **not significantly improve lumbar-spine BMD (p = 0.181)** in premenopausal women. Grade: **moderate**. — [Zhao 2014 (PubMed)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24981245/)
- **Martyn-St James & Carroll 2010:** funnel-plot **asymmetry for positive outcomes** (possible publication bias), **insufficient protocols assessing total hip**, and "diverse methodological and reporting discrepancies". Grade: **moderate**. — [Martyn-St James & Carroll 2010 (PubMed)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20013013/)
- **Tucker 2015:** the 10-jumps-twice-daily dose gave only **"marginally greater"** hip gains than control. Only 20 jumps twice daily was significant. A small dose may be near the threshold of effect. Grade: **weak–moderate**. — [Tucker 2015 (SAGE)](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.4278/ajhp.130430-QUAN-200)
- **OPTIMA-Ex, [YA]:** resistance training had **stronger effects at the proximal femur**, and impact training's advantages were at distal limb sites. Grade: **weak** (n=10 vs 12). — [OPTIMA-Ex preliminary findings](https://research-repository.griffith.edu.au/items/05f7ef03-a1e8-4476-8248-6c4996d36fb1)
- **Winters & Snow 2000:** benefits reverse within 6 months of stopping. Grade: **moderate**. — [Winters & Snow 2000 (Scilit)](https://scilit.com/publications/73c58583432c5a6a7afcb3844dde13fb)
- **Counterpoint on age relevance:** Bassey 1998 found that the same jumping programme raised hip BMD about 2–3% in premenopausal women but **had no effect in postmenopausal women**. This undercuts the idea that jumping is mainly a postmenopausal tool. Grade: **moderate** (secondary-source figures). — [Marcus 1998 JBMR editorial](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1359/jbmr.1998.13.12.1793)
- OPTIMA-Ex investigators also published on **enjoyment and acceptability** of impact vs resistance exercise in young women (Lambert et al. 2020, Health Promot J Austr). This is relevant to adherence, but results were not retrieved. — [Lambert 2020 (Wiley)](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/hpja.321)

### Inferences
- Most of the evidence that jumping helps bone in women comes from premenopausal women aged about 20–45. Jumping is at least as much a premenopausal intervention as a menopausal one. Advice aimed at menopause, such as Dr Stacy Sims's, is an extension of this evidence to an older group, not the origin of it.
- For a 22-year-old who already lifts, the realistic framing is this: jumping is **low-cost, evidence-supported for the hip, and likely a small additional benefit**, but **not essential**. Its marginal effect on top of heavy lifting is untested. Benefits need ongoing practice and need programmes of at least about 4–6 months to appear on DXA.
- None of the retrieved trials reported injury or adverse-event rates, so "not worth it" cannot be argued from harms on this evidence.

### Gaps
- No retrieved trial reported adverse events (for example, lower-limb overuse injury or pelvic-floor or stress-urinary-incontinence symptoms with high-impact training in young nulliparous women). This was not searched in depth and is out of scope for this efficacy brief.
- The Lu et al. 2016 NHANES analysis, Niu 2010 and Babatunde 2012 were not retrieved, so I cannot say whether they contain contrary findings.
- I found no 2024–2026 RCT of jumping in women aged 18–30 in this session's searches. Because full-text databases were blocked, more recent literature may have been missed. A JBMR 2023 lifespan meta-analysis of moderate- to high-impact training on bone structure exists, but I did not retrieve its age-specific results. — [JBMR 2023 lifespan meta-analysis](https://academic.oup.com/jbmr/article/38/11/1612/7610456)
