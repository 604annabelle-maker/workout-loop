# Jumping Fits 22, but Weekly Falls Short

Jump training is for her stage. If anything it suits her better than it suits menopause. Almost every controlled jumping trial in women enrolled premenopausal women aged roughly 20–45. The only trial built around college women (mean age 20.7) found that **10 maximal jumps a day, three days a week, raised femoral-neck and lumbar-spine density by about 2.5% in six months**. The classic head-to-head study gave the same jumping programme to both groups and found it **helped premenopausal women and did nothing for postmenopausal ones**. Dr Stacy Sims and the LIFTMOR trial tied jumping to menopause, but they did not originate the idea. The American College of Sports Medicine's main adult bone guideline put "activities that involve jumping" into its prescription for all adults in 2004. Her "maybe once a week" is where the honest answer changes. No human trial has tested once-weekly jumping. The closest frequency trial found that **50 hops twice a week left hip density unchanged while daily hopping raised it**. Bone also stops responding after a few dozen impacts in a session, so one longer weekly session cannot make up for low frequency. Even done properly, the payoff is modest: **about 1–3% more femoral-neck density over 6–18 months** compared with not jumping. The gain is concentrated at the hip, fades within months of stopping, and has never been measured on top of heavy lifting like hers, which builds the spine more reliably than jumping does. The minimum effective dose is small: roughly **10 maximal jumps on at least three days a week, kept up for at least six months and then indefinitely**. She can reach it by spending two minutes jumping at the start of each lifting session. At these doses the risks are manageable. Leakage during impact affects roughly a quarter to a third of female athletes and responds to training. Landing-technique programmes halve ACL injury risk. Bone-building volumes sit far below the contact counts that strain tendons. The generator should offer her weekly session honestly labelled as power and landing practice. It should default to a three-exposure minimum dose built from short jump primers, progress through five stages gated on landing quality, and screen for pregnancy, lower-limb pain, missed periods and past stress fractures before prescribing any impact.

## Jumping started as premenopausal research that menopause advice borrowed

### Sims and LIFTMOR made jumping famous among midlife women

Stacy Sims sells jumping to women in midlife. Her OSTEO-GAINS "Jump Jump Rest Repeat!" programmes take under five minutes a day at home. She writes that bone density "in peri-menopausal women can be improved with only three to five minutes of bone-stimulating jump exercises per day," and notes that she took part in the trials ([Sims](https://www.drstacysims.com/newsletters/articles/posts/bone-health)) **[Opinion; trial results not retrieved]**. Her power material pairs plyometrics with heavy lifting and sprints, under titles such as "How to Power Your Way Through Menopause" ([Sims](https://www.drstacysims.com/newsletters/articles/posts/How_to_Power_Your_Way_Through_Menopause)). One interview puts her plyometric dose at about three 10-minute blocks a week ([Unfiltered](https://unfilteredonline.com/dr-stacy-sims-why-all-women-should-lift-heavy-weights/)). Nothing found in her material restricts jumping to menopause; her audience simply happens to be peri- and postmenopausal women. Even her menopause dose is daily or several times a week, not weekly.

The LIFTMOR trial gave that audience its evidence. It enrolled 101 postmenopausal women aged 65 ± 5 with low bone mass. Eight months of twice-weekly, 30-minute supervised sessions of heavy lifting (5 × 5 above 85% of one-rep max) plus impact work raised **lumbar-spine density 2.9% while controls lost 1.2%**. The only adverse event was one minor back spasm ([LIFTMOR](https://academic.oup.com/jbmr/article/33/2/211/7605709)) **[Strong for older women with low bone mass]**. The trial overturned the old rule that fragile bone must be protected from heavy and impact loading. A large, under-served group of women losing bone after oestrogen falls took the message from there.

The jumping research itself is older and was done in younger women. Bassey and colleagues gave premenopausal and postmenopausal women the same programme: **50 small vertical jumps (mean height 8.5 cm) six days a week**. Premenopausal women gained **2.8% femoral density in five months** relative to controls (n = 31, p < 0.001). Postmenopausal women showed no difference from controls at 12 or 18 months, whether or not they took HRT ([Bassey 1998 record](https://acnpsearch.tweb-dev.unibo.it/singlejournalindex/60643465); [Marcus 1998 editorial](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1359/jbmr.1998.13.12.1793)) **[Moderate]**. In the young women those jumps landed at only about three times body weight ([Times Higher Education](https://www.timeshighereducation.com/node/6216)).

The one trial built around college-aged women, Kato 2006, enrolled 36 students averaging **20.7 years**. They did **10 maximal vertical jumps a day, three days a week, for six months**. Femoral-neck density rose from 0.984 to 1.010 g/cm² (about +2.6%) and lumbar-spine density from 0.991 to 1.015 g/cm² (about +2.4%). Controls did not change ([Kato 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16269526/); [PEDro](https://search.pedro.org.au/search-results/record-detail/14578)) **[Moderate; one small RCT in the right age group]**. The authors pitched the protocol as a way to raise density "in young women who had nearly reached peak bone mass."

The young skeleton responds to modest impacts that the postmenopausal skeleton ignores. That explains why menopause programmes push for harder, stiffer landings, and why a 22-year-old does not need to chase them.

### Her hip has just peaked and her spine is still consolidating

Where she sits on the bone-mass curve sets what jumping can do for her. US NHANES data put women's peak density at **18.7 years at the femoral neck, 19.0 at the total hip and 20.1 at the lumbar spine** ([Healio](https://cm.healio.com/news/endocrinology/20191126/peak-bmd-attainment-varies-by-sex-skeletal-site); [PubMed 31760214](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31760214/)). The Canadian CaMos cohort, followed over time, found the total-hip peak at 16–19 but the lumbar-spine peak at 33–40 ([Berger 2010](https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/peak-bone-mass-from-longitudinal-data-implications-for-the-preval/)). A third study put the spine peak at 23.0 and the femoral-neck peak at 18.5 years ([Lin 2003](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S8756328203000620)) **[Moderate; estimates vary by cohort and method]**. The Royal Osteoporosis Society's public line that density peaks around 30–35 ([Fit&Well](https://www.fitandwell.com/exercise/strength-workouts/the-way-you-move-today-helps-protect-your-bones-for-tomorrow-an-osteoporosis-specialist-on-four-forms-of-exercise-we-should-all-be-doing-to-offset-aging)) fits the spine, not the hip.

Bone is still added in the third decade. A JAMA cohort concluded that "gain in bone mass occurs in healthy young women during the third decade of life," and that physical activity and dietary calcium both contributed ([Recker 1992](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/article-abstract/400945)) **[Moderate]**. CaMos women did not start losing bone at any measured site until 40–44 ([Berger 2008](https://rrmacademy.org/library/change-in-bone-mineral-density-as-a-function-of-age-in-women-and-men-and-associa-recuovpgnb4yfxood/)). Even so, some women lose hip density in early adulthood despite healthy habits ([Healio 2021](https://cm.healio.com/news/endocrinology/20211019/bmd-loss-seen-in-early-adulthood-for-women-despite-healthy-behaviors)) **[Weak–moderate; observational]**.

At 22 her hip is at or just past its peak, and her spine still has room to gain. Lifestyle accounts for an estimated **20–40% of adult peak bone mass** ([BHOF press release](https://www.bonehealthandosteoporosis.org/wp-content/uploads/2016/02/Peak-Bone-Mass-Press-Release.pdf)). The strongest (Grade A) evidence for physical activity, though, covers late childhood and puberty, not her decade ([Weaver 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26856587/)) **[Strong]**. Jumping at 22 is therefore a consolidation tool. It adds a little at the hip, heads off small early losses, and raises the starting point for the bone loss that begins at menopause.

How much that matters in the long run is modelled, not measured. A bone-remodelling model predicts that **a 10% higher peak density delays osteoporosis by about 13 years**. The same model gives only about two years for a 10% change in menopause age ([Hernandez 2003](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s00198-003-1454-8)) **[Opinion; computer model]**. Scaled linearly to the 1–3% that jumping produces, that would be roughly one to four years, and only if she keeps the gain. Treat that as an illustration, not a finding.

### Pooled effects

Pooled analyses agree on size and location. A meta-analysis of jumping-only trials found a **femoral-neck gain of 0.017 g/cm²** and a significant trochanter gain. The lumbar spine "seemed to benefit less" (p = 0.181, not significant) ([Zhao 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24981245/)) **[Moderate; six small trials]**. A second meta-analysis found that **high-impact-only protocols raised femoral-neck density by 0.024 g/cm²** ([Martyn-St James & Carroll 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20013013/)) **[Moderate]**. Against Kato's baseline femoral neck of 0.984 g/cm², those pooled figures equal roughly **1.7–2.4%**. A review of programme design found that protocols of 10–100 jumps a day, on 3–7 days a week for 4–18 months, produced femoral-neck gains of **0.6–3.4%** ([design review](https://oalib-perpustakaan.upi.edu/Record/peertech__10_17352_asmp_000015/Description)) **[Moderate]**.

### Bone shape, not just density

Density understates the effect on strength, because jumping also changes bone shape. After Heinonen's 18-month programme, trainees' femoral necks had a **3.2% larger section modulus and 2.8% larger cross-sectional area** than controls' ([Heinonen HSA](https://utppublishing.com/doi/10.3138/ptc.2010-37)). Cross-sectionally, women athletes in high-impact and "odd-impact" sports had femoral-neck cortex about 20% thicker than non-athletes, and finite-element fracture loads 11–26% higher ([Nikander thesis](https://jyx.jyu.fi/jyx/Record/jyx_123456789_20275)) **[Moderate–weak; self-selection possible]**.

### What the numbers do and do not show

Several caveats keep these numbers in proportion. Part of the measured benefit is **not losing bone**. In Tucker's trial neither jump group gained significantly from baseline; the hip difference arose because controls lost density ([PEDro](https://search.pedro.org.au/search-results/record-detail/45237)). Controls also drifted down in Vainionpää's and Bailey's trials. In adult women, the realistic win is partly a gain and partly a defence.

Gains take time to appear. Eight weeks of jumping showed nothing on DXA ([thesis](https://shareok.org/items/0f84c622-2378-4325-9472-e90e53e16f02)). Belinda Beck, a lead author of the Australian guideline, says benefits need at least 6–12 months of training ([Griffith news](https://news.griffith.edu.au/?p=43912)) **[Opinion; expert]**.

Gains also do not bank. After 12 months of jumping plus resistance training, six months off returned density, strength and power toward baseline while controls stayed level ([Winters & Snow 2000](https://scilit.com/publications/73c58583432c5a6a7afcb3844dde13fb)) **[Moderate]**. Heinonen's bone advantages shrank to non-significance by 3.5 years, while the performance gains held ([UKK Institute](https://ukkinstituutti.fi/?p=8266)). A residual structural benefit has been claimed for that cohort, but the claim was not verified.

The evidence base itself has gaps. One meta-analysis detected funnel-plot asymmetry, a sign of publication bias that inflates pooled estimates ([Martyn-St James & Carroll 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20013013/)). No trial in this age group has reported fracture outcomes, so every long-term benefit is inferred from density.

### What this means for a woman who already lifts

One more inference applies directly to her. Most trial participants were sedentary or untrained, and a skeleton already loaded by 2–4 lifting sessions a week has less room to respond. A reasonable expectation is the lower half of the 1–3% range at the hip, little extra at the spine from jumps alone, and gains that last only while she keeps jumping. **[Opinion]**

## Frequency, not volume, sets the minimum dose

### Once a week has no supporting trial, and the closest data point against it

The only human trial that varied jump frequency in premenopausal women is Bailey & Brooke-Wavell. Women did 50 multidirectional hops on one leg, with the other leg as a within-person control, at four assigned frequencies. Femoral-neck changes in the hopping leg were about **−0.3% at 0 days a week, 0.0% at 2 days, +0.9% at 4 days and +1.8% (95% CI 0.8–2.8) at 7 days**. The group effect was significant (p = 0.015), and the authors concluded that "brief, daily hopping exercises increased femoral neck BMD in premenopausal women but less frequent exercise was not effective" ([Bailey & Brooke-Wavell 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20004758/)) **[Moderate; per-group figures read from an abstract snippet and worth checking against the full text]**.

The only once-weekly data come from growing rats doing 10 jumps per session for eight weeks. Tibial mass gains relative to sedentary rats were:

| Sessions per week | Tibial mass gain |
|---|---|
| 1 | +7.5% (not significant) |
| 3 | +12.6% (significant) |
| 5 | +12.0% (significant) |
| 7 | +19.8% (significant) |

([Umemura 2008](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00774-007-0848-7)) **[Weak; animal]**

In older women, a 12-year cohort concluded that "an overall exercise frequency of at least 2 sessions/week may be crucial, even if exercise is applied with high intensity/impact" ([Kemmler EFOPS](https://cris.fau.de/publications/117839524)). A related review put the minimum effective frequency at about 2.1–2.5 sessions a week ([Kemmler 2017](https://cris.fau.de/publications/205197211)) **[Moderate; postmenopausal women]**. One 2020 meta-analysis in postmenopausal women found the opposite pattern at the spine, which its authors attributed to interacting exercise variables ([Springer Medicine](https://www.springermedicine.com/exercise-frequency-and-fracture-risk-in-older-adults-how-often-i/21943906)).

The mechanism explains why a bigger weekly session cannot substitute for frequency. Bone's mechanosensors become less responsive after a couple of dozen loading cycles in one bout, and they take hours to days to recover ([Turner & Robling 2003](https://core.ac.uk/download/215746207.pdf); [Umemura 2016](https://doaj.org/article/6bd05782895243588b231b363d385967)). In rats, about **8 hours of recovery fully restored sensitivity**, and inserting about 14 seconds between individual load cycles raised bone formation by 66–190% ([Robling 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11606612/)). Four bouts of 90 cycles separated by three hours built more bone than 360 cycles in one bout ([Robling 2002](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/biology/2002-robling.pdf)) **[Weak; animal]**. A weekly session of 100 jumps is therefore not equivalent to 20 jumps on five days.

So, as a bone prescription, **once a week is below every frequency that has worked in humans**, and the indirect evidence says it is not enough. That does not make her idea worthless.

First, she is not choosing between jumping once a week and doing nothing. Her 2–4 lifting sessions already load her skeleton, and a weekly jump session adds a different kind of strain on top. The size of that addition is unknown, not zero.

Second, a weekly session trains power and landing skill, and those gains outlast the bone gains. Heinonen's trainees kept their vertical-jump improvement at 3.5 years after the bone advantage had faded ([UKK Institute](https://ukkinstituutti.fi/?p=8266)). Plyometric training with technique feedback was common to every effective ACL-prevention programme in one review ([PMC2779043](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2779043)).

Third, practitioner progressions for plyometric beginners start with **one session a week** and add a second only once the first is tolerated ([Outside Online](https://www.outsideonline.com/running/training/workouts/ready-for-power-building-plyometrics-take-this-test-and-start-with-these-exercises/?scope=anon)) **[Weak; practitioner guidance]**. Her instinct is the right first rung. It is not the right destination for bone.

### Ten maximal jumps on three days a week is the smallest proven dose

The smallest protocol shown to work in women her age is Kato's: **10 maximal countermovement jumps, three days a week, for six months**. Compliance was 82%, so participants actually averaged about 2.5 sessions a week ([Kato 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16269526/)) **[Moderate]**. That shortfall is a practical argument for planning four exposures and expecting to hit three.

Tucker's trial marks the per-day floor for submaximal jumps. Twenty jumps twice a day beat controls at the hip; ten twice a day was only "marginally" better ([Tucker 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24460005/)) **[Weak]**. Accelerometer data from Vainionpää's trial put the threshold for improving hip density at **fewer than 100 impacts a day above 3.9 g**. A related report places it at about 60 a day, roughly the same number of jumps ([Vainionpää 2006](https://www.springermedicine.com/intensity-of-exercise-is-associated-with-bone-density-change-in-/20631404); [DOAJ 2009](https://doaj.org/article/0ef030b4a8244c998b9fe43e39bb9e85)) **[Moderate]**.

Guidelines describe a fuller dose. The UK society advises building to about 50 moderate impacts on most days ([SfE quick guide](https://www.endocrinology.org/media/3590/final-quick-guide_strong-steady-straight_dec18.pdf)). The Australian statement is summarised as about 50 impacts per session on 4–7 days a week ([Wild Physio](https://wildphysiofitness.au/blogs/physiotherapy/exercise-for-bone-health)) **[Opinion; secondary]**.

The evidence therefore separates two tiers. The **minimum effective dose** is 10–20 maximal jumps on at least three days a week. The **better-supported dose** is 20–50 impacts on four to seven days a week, which matches Bassey's 6-day and Bailey's daily protocols. Both tiers have to be sustained for at least six months before any change is measurable, and kept up after that.

### Height, speed, direction and rest decide whether a jump counts

Four loading features matter, and none of them requires heavy volume.

The first is **magnitude**. The accelerometer threshold above sits near 4 g, and effective premenopausal protocols loaded at 2–6 times body weight ([design review](https://oalib-perpustakaan.upi.edu/Record/peertech__10_17352_asmp_000015/Description)).

The second is **rate**. In Vainionpää's cohort, activity with steep acceleration slopes, meaning fast, crisp ground contacts, was associated with hip gains, so the speed of loading matters as well as its peak ([Heikkinen 2007](https://ersearch2.cvtisr.sk/vufind/SummonRecord/FETCH-LOGICAL-c601t-8ab9a504f86f363d8400d50ce19cb4dd522b347da13a2dfcde4d5a95097c6b093?print=1)) **[Moderate–weak]**. The same design review put effective loading rates above 43 body weights per second ([design review](https://oalib-perpustakaan.upi.edu/Record/peertech__10_17352_asmp_000015/Description)).

The third is **direction**. Athletes in odd-impact sports such as squash, soccer and step aerobics had femoral necks as robust as those of high-impact athletes, while weightlifting did not clearly raise the femoral neck's section modulus. The authors concluded that "loadings which arise from high impacts or impacts from atypical loading directions seem particularly effective" ([Nikander](https://jyx.jyu.fi/jyx/Record/jyx_123456789_20275)) **[Moderate–weak]**.

The fourth is **rest**. In animals, a 10-second unloaded pause between cycles turned a non-osteogenic stimulus into an osteogenic one ([Srinivasan 2002](https://data.zhenzhi.com/details/1/898692233)) **[Weak]**.

Two practical rules follow. A jump onto a box spares the landing, which is the part bone needs. Stepping down removes the impact entirely, while a deliberate drop landing from the box supplies it **[Opinion]**. And because Bassey's 8.5-cm jumps worked in young women, she gets her stimulus from honest, maximal-effort jumps with controlled landings. Stiff-legged landings, which carry more knee risk, are not necessary **[Opinion]**.

## Heavy lifting builds her spine, and jumping adds what the barbell misses

Resistance training alone builds bone in young women, mainly at the spine. In 30 women aged **22 ± 2**, twelve weeks of squats at **85–90% of one-rep max** raised lumbar-spine density **2.2%** and total-hip density **1.0%**. The bone-formation marker P1NP rose 26% and squat strength nearly doubled ([Mosti 2014](https://www.pubmed.gov/24736773/)) **[Weak–moderate; small and short]**. In women averaging about 20, eight months of either weightlifting or jogging "modestly increased lumbar spine" mineral, and neither changed the femur ([Snow-Harter 1992](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1642145/)) **[Weak–moderate]**.

Head-to-head trials of the two modes are few, tiny and split. In women aged 20–35, twelve months of step-aerobic impact raised heel density **4.4%**, while strength training produced no significant gain at any measured site ([PubMed 21165807](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21165807/)) **[Weak–moderate]**. OPTIMA-Ex compared supervised impact and resistance training twice a week for ten months in women aged 18–30 with below-average bone mass. Both improved the spine. Impact training did more at the distal radius and tibia; resistance training did more at the proximal femur and along long-bone shafts. The authors stress "minimal statistical power" with ten and twelve completers ([OPTIMA-Ex](https://research-repository.griffith.edu.au/items/05f7ef03-a1e8-4476-8248-6c4996d36fb1)) **[Weak; preliminary]**.

Pooled data point toward combining the two. Impact-only protocols improved the femoral neck. **Combined impact plus high-magnitude resistance protocols improved both the lumbar spine and the femoral neck** ([Martyn-St James & Carroll 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20013013/)) **[Moderate]**. A two-year programme of aerobics plus weights in women aged 20–35 had "beneficial effects on BMD" ([Friedlander 1995](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7610928/)). Every adult guideline in the table above pairs impact with resistance training rather than ranking one above the other.

The honest answer on adding jumps to her lifting is that **no adequately powered trial has tested resistance training versus resistance training plus jumps in already-trained women aged 18–30**. The case for adding them rests on the fact that the two load bone differently. Lifting applies high, slow, predictable strain, mostly through the spine and along the long bones. Jumping applies fast, high-rate strain at the hip, often from unusual directions, which lifting does not provide. On top of her lifting, jumps are a complementary stimulus, not a duplicate one.

How much they add also depends on how she lifts. Mosti's spine and hip gains came from loads of 85–90% of one-rep max. If most of her training is 8–15-rep hypertrophy work at moderate loads, she gives her bones less than a heavy-squat programme does, and jumps carry more of the bone load. That inference has not been tested head-to-head **[Opinion]**.

Neither jumps nor heavy lifting can offset under-fuelling. Low energy availability suppresses ovulation and oestrogen and shifts bone turnover toward resorption. The results are lower density and higher stress-fracture risk, and recovery takes years ([ISAKOS](https://isakos.com/GlobalLink/Newsletter/2025-Volume-3/Bone-Health-and-Females); [IOC REDs 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37752011/)) **[Strong]**. The Australian statement also makes calcium and vitamin D sufficiency part of any bone programme ([ESSA](https://ro.ecu.edu.au/ecuworkspost2013/2797)). A reasonable ranking for her is: adequate energy and regular cycles first, heavy lifting plus impact second, and nutrient sufficiency third **[Opinion]**.

## Bone-sized doses keep pelvic floor, knee and tendon risk low

The safety picture starts with dose. Commonly taught plyometric tables give beginners **80–100 foot contacts per session** ([NSCA-CPT guide](https://open-exam-prep.com/study-guides/nsca-cpt/mobility-bodyweight-performance/plyometrics)), and one hospital protocol starts rehabilitating athletes at 60 ([Mass General Brigham](https://www.massgeneralbrigham.org/content/dam/unified-xwalk/pdf/patient-education/english/mass-general-jump-and-plyometric-training.pdf)) **[Weak–moderate; practice standard]**. The bone dose of 10–50 contacts sits well below that range.

Trials in fragile bone add some reassurance. LIFTMOR recorded one minor adverse event in 101 older women with low bone mass ([LIFTMOR](https://academic.oup.com/jbmr/article/33/2/211/7605709)). The UK consensus found "little evidence that physical activity is associated with significant harm" ([Nursing in Practice](https://www.nursinginpractice.com/clinical/musculoskeletal/encourage-osteoporosis-patients-to-exercise-regularly-say-experts/)). However, none of the premenopausal jump trials reviewed here reported injury rates in the available abstracts, so her injury risk is inferred rather than measured.

### Leakage is common in jumping women, trainable, and not proof of damage

Urinary incontinence is the female-specific cost of impact. Pooled prevalence in female athletes is **36%, with a 177% higher risk than in sedentary women** ([Teixeira 2018](https://springerlink.bibliotecabuap.elogim.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s00192-018-3651-1.pdf)) **[Strong for prevalence]**. Another meta-analysis found **25.9% for any incontinence and 20.7% for stress incontinence**, with volleyball highest at 75.6% ([Pires 2020](https://johk.pl/?p=5625)) **[Moderate]**. Among young nulliparous elite athletes, reported rates run from 0% in golf to 80% in trampolining ([BMJ Open SEM blog](https://blogs.bmj.com/bmjopensem/2021/06/29/past-present-and-future-of-pelvic-floor-issues-in-female-athletes/)). A 2022 review reports about 40% in young nulliparous athletes but cites a meta-analysis in which prevalence did not rise with the degree of impact ([Curr Urol Rep 2022](https://springerlink.bibliotecabuap.elogim.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s11934-022-01133-6.pdf)), so the dose–response is contested.

Kari Bø's view is that leakage reflects load exceeding the pelvic floor's capacity, and that athletes need a stronger, better-timed pelvic floor than non-exercisers ([BMJ Open SEM blog](https://blogs.bmj.com/bmjopensem/2021/06/29/past-present-and-future-of-pelvic-floor-issues-in-female-athletes/)) **[Opinion; mechanism-based]**. No source shows that impact causes lasting pelvic floor damage in women who have not given birth, though none rules it out either. Pelvic floor muscle training is first-line, has no serious adverse effects, and has a 2023 meta-analysis behind it in female athletes ([PFMT in athletes](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37688407/)) **[Moderate]**.

Leakage during jumps is therefore a signal to cap impact, train the pelvic floor and see a pelvic health physiotherapist if it persists. It is not a reason to ban jumping, and it is not something to accept as normal.

### Landing practice turns jumping into ACL protection

Women tear the ACL about **1.7 times as often as men overall**, and about **5.5 times** as often in high-impact landing sports such as gymnastics ([Montalvo 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6561829)) **[Strong]**. Across 18 studies and 27,231 young female athletes, neuromuscular training **cut ACL injury odds roughly in half (OR 0.51)**, from about 1 in 54 to 1 in 111. Programmes that included landing stabilisation and lower-body strength did better ([Petushek 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30001501/)) **[Strong; mostly adolescents in sport]**. In youth females, such training increased knee flexion on landing (g = 0.58) and reduced knee valgus (g = −0.86) ([Cardiff Met 2025](https://pure.cardiffmet.ac.uk/en/publications/influence-of-neuromuscular-training-interventions-on-jump-landing/)) **[Moderate]**.

The jump block can serve as her landing-technique practice. Every set is a chance to rehearse knees tracking over toes, hips back, no inward collapse and a quiet but not mushy landing. That benefit applies even at once a week.

### Tendons tolerate tens of contacts far better than hundreds

Achilles and patellar tendon problems arise when high-velocity loading outpaces conditioning. Pain during jumping should stop the session ([Steward Health](https://content.steward.org/node/659066)) **[Weak]**. Practitioner rules add bilateral-before-single-leg progression, 48–72 hours between harder plyometric sessions, and progression by landing quality rather than by the calendar ([Outside Online](https://www.outsideonline.com/running/training/workouts/ready-for-power-building-plyometrics-take-this-test-and-start-with-these-exercises/?scope=anon)) **[Weak]**. Short primers of 10–20 contacts sit at a fraction of beginner plyometric volume and do not need that spacing. Drop jumps and single-leg bounding do.

### Four findings should stop impact before it starts

Four screening findings should hold back impact before it is prescribed.

**Missed periods or signs of under-eating.** These point to low energy availability. Among more than 200 elite athletes assessed with the IOC's REDs tool, those with REDs had more amenorrhoea, more bone stress injuries and lower density ([BJSM 2025](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/59/1/24)) **[Moderate]**. The answer is a clinician and more food, not more loading. On the combined pill, withdrawal bleeds are drug-induced, so a regular "period" does not confirm adequate energy **[Opinion; inference from how the pill works]**.

**A past bone stress injury.** Athletes with a history of bone stress injury need a bone density assessment ([ISAKOS](https://isakos.com/GlobalLink/Newsletter/2025-Volume-3/Bone-Health-and-Females)).

**Pregnancy.** ACOG's Committee Opinion 804 is summarised as advising against contact sports and activities with high fall risk, with low-impact options preferred ([DONA](https://dona.org/new-acog-opinion-on-exercise-in-the-childbearing-year/)) **[Moderate for fall-risk advice; weak for jumping specifically]**.

**Current lower-limb pain or injury.** This needs resolving before impact starts.

Two further conditions call for modified rather than excluded impact. The Australian statement says impact needs modifying for osteoarthritis or frailty ([ESSA](https://ro.ecu.edu.au/ecuworkspost2013/2797)). Beck advises supervision for people with osteoporosis ([Griffith news](https://news.griffith.edu.au/?p=43912)). Neither applies to a healthy 22-year-old, but both belong in a generator that might serve other users.

## Conclusion

The real lesson of her question is that bone and muscle answer to different schedules. Muscle growth tracks weekly hard sets, and once each muscle is trained about twice a week, how those sets are spread matters little. That is why "once a week" sounds reasonable to someone who lifts. Bone works the other way. It saturates within a few dozen impacts and needs hours to days to become responsive again. A large weekly dose wastes most of its reps, while a tiny dose repeated often does the work. That is why the most efficient design is not a jump workout at all but a two-minute jump primer attached to every lifting session. Frequency then comes for free, and her once-weekly session can carry the skill progression. The same mismatch explains the menopause framing. Older skeletons need harder, more frequent impact to respond at all, so the programmes written for them look more demanding. That is a sign that 22 is the cheaper time to start, not a sign that she is too early.

The open question is the one that matters most to her and has never been tested: how much jumping adds on top of heavy lifting in a woman who already trains. Neither her logs nor her mirror will answer it, because the effect is a few percent of density measurable only by DXA, and only after six months or more. The generator should therefore judge the module by adherence, landing quality and symptoms rather than by visible results. It should present the bone benefit as a cheap, evidence-backed bet rather than a promise, and store each rule's source and grade so the dose can be revised when OPTIMA-Ex-style trials in trained young women finally report.
