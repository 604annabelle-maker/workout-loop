# Pair Two Muscles Daily, Train Everything Twice

Her best setup is a **themed upper/lower split**. Each session keeps two headline muscle groups, and the pairs rotate so that every major muscle is trained twice a week. **Monday is glutes and hamstrings (heavy deadlift), Tuesday back and chest (heavy bench), Wednesday a class, Thursday shoulders and back (heavy overhead press and pull-up), Friday quads and glutes (heavy squat), Saturday a class and Sunday rest.** Each roughly 55-minute session opens with a two-minute jump primer. Then comes one heavy main lift of 4 sets of 3–6 reps, stopped 1–3 reps short of failure, followed by two supersets of opposing muscles and a short finisher. That adds up to **15–20 working sets per session and about 10–19 weighted sets per muscle per week**, the range supported by the 2026 American College of Sports Medicine (ACSM) position stand and the largest dose-response analysis to date. Her own once-a-week body-part pairing builds about as much muscle when weekly sets are equal. But it practises the bench, overhead press and pull-up only once a week and leaves hamstrings, chest and triceps under 10 sets, so it is a fallback, not the default. A hybrid week with one full-body "athletic" day is the alternative if power and jumping matter more to her than arm size. Main lifts stay fixed for each 4–6-week block, usually for two blocks. One or two accessory slots per session change each block, and every exercise comes with a default plus three ranked swaps. A dislike retires an exercise for good, so her chest-supported dumbbell row becomes a seated cable row and the medicine-ball chest pass is dropped. Logging shrinks to a one-word reply. She sends `done` when a session went as written, short exceptions such as `A easy` or `B 50 8 8 6` when it did not, and **no reply holds every load instead of progressing it**. The email puts a one-line-per-exercise list and the reply instruction on the first screen. Below it, simple two-frame line drawings appear only for new or flagged exercises. The strong evidence covers weekly volume, twice-weekly frequency, effort and the finding that women train by the same rules as men. The templates, rotation timings, swap rankings, reply format and email layout are reasoned design, to be tuned with her.

*Evidence tags used throughout: **[Strong]** = consistent meta-analyses or position-stand guidance; **[Moderate]** = a meta-analysis with caveats, a systematic review of small trials, or one or two decent randomised trials; **[Weak]** = single small studies, preprints, conference abstracts or secondary reporting; **[Opinion]** = coaching convention or my own design inference, with no direct trial. Most primary full texts could not be opened during this research, so figures come from abstracts and summaries.*

## Rotating the pairs gives every muscle two days a week

### With weekly sets matched, frequency matters for strength, not size

Her preference for "two muscle groups a day" raises two questions: can it coexist with training each muscle twice a week, and does that matter? An early meta-analysis of 10 studies favoured training a muscle at least twice weekly over once (effect sizes **0.49 vs 0.30**) ([Schoenfeld 2016](https://springerlink.bibliotecabuap.elogim.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s40279-016-0543-8.pdf)). But in many of its studies the higher-frequency groups also did more sets ([Henselmans review](https://mennohenselmans.com/training-frequency-2018-meta-analysis-review/)). Re-run with 25 studies and weekly volume held equal, **frequency had no significant effect on muscle growth** ([Schoenfeld 2019](https://www.pubmed.gov/30558493/)) **[Moderate]**.

The 2025 Pelland dose-response meta-regressions cover 67 studies and about 2,058 people. They found that more weekly sets build more muscle with diminishing returns, roughly **+0.24% growth per extra weekly set** at average volumes. Counting a synergist's set as half a set predicted results best ([Pelland 2025](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/398333408_The_Resistance_Training_Dose_Response_Meta-Regressions_Exploring_the_Effects_of_Weekly_Volume_and_Frequency_on_Muscle_Hypertrophy_and_Strength_Gains_The_Resistance_Training_Dose_ResponseJ_C_Pelland_et_al)) **[Strong]**. The frequency effect on growth was compatible with zero, while **the probability of a positive frequency effect on strength was 100%** ([train.fitness summary](https://train.fitness/personal-trainer-blogs/not-all-sets-are-created-equal)) **[Moderate]**. Grgic's strength meta-analysis found a small overall frequency benefit (d = 0.265) that vanished when volume was equated, plus a significant frequency effect in a women-only subgroup (p = 0.030) ([Grgic 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29470825)) **[Moderate; subgroup Weak]**. The 2026 ACSM position stand, built on 137 systematic reviews, sets the practical floor at **every major muscle on at least 2 days a week** and about **10 or more sets per muscle per week** for growth ([ACSM infographic](https://acsm.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Resistance-Training-Position-Stand-infographic.pdf); [NFPT](https://nfpt.com/beyond-the-guidelines-what-the-2026-acsm-position-stand-gets-right-and-what-it-changes-for-personal-trainers/)) **[Strong as guidance]**.

The once-a-week split hits its limit at the level of a single session. A companion preprint puts the point beyond which extra sets in one session stop showing a detectable benefit at about **11 weighted sets per muscle for hypertrophy and about 2 direct sets for strength** ([Remmert preprint](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/537)) **[Weak; not peer reviewed, sparse data at high volumes]**. A muscle trained once a week has to fit its 10 or more weekly sets into that one session. That sits right at the ceiling, and in 60 minutes it crowds out everything else. A once-weekly body-part split is therefore **a small but real disadvantage for "lifting heavier" and for upper-body volume, not a dealbreaker for lean muscle** **[Moderate inference]**. Two findings on women point the same way. Women gain the same *relative* muscle as men and larger relative upper-body strength ([Roberts 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32218059/)) **[Moderate]**, so her bench, press and pull-up should climb quickly and reward twice-weekly practice. Men's *absolute* growth advantage is concentrated in the upper body ([Refalo 2025](https://peerj.com/articles/19042)), which argues for protecting her back and shoulder work rather than letting glute volume crowd it out **[Moderate data; Opinion recommendation]**.

Set counts below follow Pelland's weighting: a set counts **1.0 for a prime mover and 0.5 for a synergist**. Examples are triceps on the bench press, biceps on rows, and hamstrings and quads on deadlifts. Jumps and conditioning count zero.

Template A is the only design examined that meets all four of her constraints at once. Each day reads as two muscles. Every major muscle gets two sessions and roughly 10–19 sets. Each pressing and pulling pattern gets one heavy day and one moderate day for strength. And both classes fall at least 48 hours before a heavy lower day **[Opinion, built on the Strong and Moderate findings above]**. It also matches the lower–upper–lower–upper rhythm that glute-focused coach Bret Contreras describes for four-day clients ([Contreras via podcast summary](https://yage-health.pages.dev/articles/exercise/build-your-ideal-physique-dr-bret-contreras)) **[Opinion]**.

It has four honest costs. First, a third muscle slips in as a superset partner or finisher: the leg curl on quad day, and arms and side delts on upper days. That is the price of twice-weekly frequency. Second, quads get only one direct day. Third, Monday's glutes reach about 12 weighted sets, just past the ~11-set per-session point, which is why hip abduction is the first set to cut. Fourth, the upper days run six exercises because the last pair are small isolation moves. If six feels like too many, dropping that pair leaves arms at roughly 6–7 weekly sets **[Opinion]**.

**Template B** keeps her pairs. Monday matches A. Tuesday is back and biceps: heavy pull-up, row with curl, pulldown with hammer curl, and a rear-delt finisher. Thursday is chest, shoulders and triceps: bench with face pull, dumbbell press with lateral raise, incline press with overhead extension. It is the longest session, at 55–60 minutes, and the only one with three muscle groups. Friday is quads and calves: squat, leg press with calf raise, leg extension with seated calf raise. Glutes still train twice, and hypertrophy is probably close to A's wherever weekly sets match. Upper-body lifts get one practice a week, though, and three muscles sit under 10 sets. Three five-minute "patches" fix most of that, each adding a small third muscle to a day: 2–3 sets of leg curl on Friday, 2 sets of push-ups in Tuesday's finisher and 2 sets of pull-ups on Thursday **[Opinion]**.

**Template C** keeps A's Monday, Tuesday and Friday. Thursday becomes a full-body athletic day. It opens with a power block: box or broad jumps 4 × 3, paired with medicine-ball slams 3 × 5 (slams replace the chest pass she disliked). Then push press or overhead press 4 × 3–6 with chin-ups 4 × 4–8. Then a reverse lunge or single-leg Romanian deadlift 3 × 6–8 each leg, kept at 3 reps in reserve because Friday is squat day, paired with lateral raises. It finishes with push-ups and farmer carries 3 × 30–40 m. Every muscle still gets two sessions, but arms fall under 10 sets and back sits near 11. **If either class is HIIT, much of C's athletic content is already covered and A is the better fit** **[Opinion]**.

### Leg-heavy classes sit 48 hours before heavy squats and deadlifts

Concurrent training is safe for her goals over the long run. A 43-study meta-analysis found that adding aerobic training **did not compromise hypertrophy or maximal strength**. It did blunt explosive strength, especially when both were done in the same session ([Schumann 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34757594/)) **[Moderate]**. The preprint version found that blunting only when sessions were less than about 3 hours apart ([Schumann preprint](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/37)) **[Weak]**. The real problem is acute. In a small study of men, cycling intervals impaired strength 4 and 8 hours later, **only in the muscles the cycling used**, and the authors advised about 24 hours between hard aerobic and strength sessions ([Sporer & Wenger via Kravitz](https://www.unm.edu/~lkravitz/Article%20folder/concurrent.html)) **[Weak]**. Cycling produced a larger acute hit to strength endurance than treadmill work ([PMC4425969](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4425969)) **[Weak]**.

That gives four placement rules **[Opinion built on the above]**. Leg-heavy classes go at least 48 hours before a heavy squat or deadlift day, and never on the evening before one. Their best slot is an upper-body day, at least 3 hours after lifting, or the day after a lower day. If a class shares a day with lifting, she lifts first. And a two-rest-day week is possible by moving Class 1 to Tuesday evening, which frees Wednesday.

| Class | Main stress | Interference with lifting | Placement | Adjust the lifting week |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pilates | Core, low load | Very low | Anywhere | None; can replace extra core work. Add one hard interval finisher a week for fitness |
| Spin | Quads and glutes, intervals | Moderate for legs, none for upper body | Wednesday, or the evening of an upper day | Drop conditioning finishers on lower days |
| HIIT | Whole body, jumps | Highest | Mid-week, at least 48 h from heavy lower days | Drop conditioning finishers; keep jump primers at about 10 jumps and skip separate power blocks |
| Barre | High-rep glute and quad work, isometric holds | Moderate for legs | Not the day before a lower day | Count as 2–4 light glute/quad sets; trim hip abduction |
| Strength class | Whole-body moderate volume | Moderate, whole body | An upper day, or between lower days | Count as about +1 set per major muscle; trim one accessory superset |

No study has examined Pilates or barre interference specifically, so those rows are reasoning from intensity and local fatigue.

The classes are also her "fitter" lever. The WHO recommends **75–150 minutes of vigorous or 150–300 minutes of moderate activity a week** on top of strength work ([WHO 2020](https://bjsm.bmj.com/node/121498.full)) **[Strong as guidance]**. Two spin or HIIT classes probably cover most of the vigorous target. Two Pilates or barre classes do not **[Opinion]**. In that case she needs one short, hard effort a week. Three 20-second all-out sprints raised VO2peak as much as 45 minutes of steady cycling ([Gillen 2016](https://doaj.org/article/0d8f38860885479c87ee1ba61460002b)), and four 4-minute intervals raised VO2max 7.2% in eight weeks ([Helgerud 2007](https://coachsci.sdsu.edu/csa/vol131/helgerud.htm)) **[Moderate; men]**. Either fits as a bike finisher on Tuesday or Thursday.

## Each session is one heavy lift, two supersets and a short finish

### Heavy compound first, moderate-rep pairs after

For strength, the ACSM's prescription is **at least 80% of one-rep max, 2–3 sets per exercise, at least twice a week**. For hypertrophy, loads anywhere from **30% to 100% of one-rep max** work if sets end near failure, and stopping 1–2 reps short matches failure when volume is equal ([Rep Fitness summary of ACSM 2026](https://repfitness.com/blogs/training/acsm-updated-resistance-training-guidelines)) **[Strong for load and effort; guidance-level for the strength prescription]**. One session can serve both goals. A heavy main lift of 4 × 3–6 at 1–3 reps in reserve (RIR) delivers the strength stimulus. Accessories at 6–20 reps and 0–2 RIR deliver the remaining hypertrophy volume.

Formal daily undulating periodisation is unnecessary. Periodised training beat non-periodised training for one-rep max only in already-trained people, and undulating models had a trivial-to-small edge over linear ones. A 2026 meta-analysis reportedly did not confirm even that ([Examine on Moesgaard 2022](https://examine.com/faq/are-periodized-training-programs-superior-for-strength-gains)) **[Weak–Moderate, conflicting]**. For hypertrophy, linear and undulating plans were identical (effect size −0.02) ([Grgic 2017](https://peerj.com/articles/3695)) **[Moderate]**.

Template A already undulates week to week, because each pattern gets one heavy and one moderate exposure. Bench is heavy on Tuesday and push-ups come on Thursday. The overhead press is heavy on Thursday and the incline press comes on Tuesday. Pull-ups are heavy on Thursday and pulldowns come on Tuesday. Deadlift and squat each get one heavy day, and the other lower day hits the same muscles through Romanian deadlifts or split squats **[Opinion]**. If lower-body strength stalls, she can add 2 top sets of the other main lift to each lower day, for example 2 × 3 paused squats at RIR 3–4 after Monday's deadlifts. The "about 2 sets per session" strength figure suggests this captures most of the extra benefit **[Weak]**. Heavy work also pays off in bone. In 30 women aged 22 ± 2, twelve weeks of squats at 85–90% of one-rep max raised lumbar-spine density **2.2%** ([Mosti 2014](https://www.pubmed.gov/24736773/)) **[Weak–Moderate]**.

The warm-up primer carries the athletic dose. In college women, **10 maximal jumps three days a week raised hip and spine density by about 2.5% in six months** ([Kato 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16269526/)) **[Moderate]**. Hopping twice a week did nothing, while daily hopping raised hip density **1.8%** ([Bailey & Brooke-Wavell 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20004758/)) **[Moderate]**. Short primers on all four lifting days therefore beat one long weekly jump session. They also give her landing practice: neuromuscular training that includes landing work roughly **halved ACL injury odds (OR 0.51)** in young female athletes ([Petushek 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30001501/)) **[Strong in adolescent athletes; extrapolated to her]**. Jumps are done fresh with about 10–20 seconds between reps. They are never taken to failure and never counted as hypertrophy sets. She should progress from two-footed to multidirectional to single-leg jumps only once landings are quiet and stable. Leakage or pain stops the progression and sends her back a stage; pelvic-floor training is the first-line fix for leakage ([PFMT in athletes](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37688407/)) **[Moderate]**. The stage order itself is **[Opinion]**.

### Fifteen to twenty working sets fit in about 50 minutes

| Block | Content | Time |
|---|---|---|
| Warm-up | 5 min bike or row, a few dynamic drills, jump primer | ~6–8 min |
| Main block | 2–4 ramp-up sets, then 4 working sets with 2.5–3 min rest (paired with a row or pull-up on upper days) | ~15–20 min |
| Superset 1 | 3 rounds of two exercises, 60–90 s between exercises | ~10–12 min |
| Superset 2 | 3 rounds | ~9–10 min |
| Finisher | Short conditioning or a light arm/delt pair | ~5 min |
| **Total** | **15–20 working sets** | **~45–55 min, with a 5–10-min buffer** |

The timings are arithmetic, not measurements **[Opinion]**, but the structure rests on good evidence. A 2025 meta-analysis of 19 studies found **supersets matched traditional sets for strength (SMD 0.10) and hypertrophy (SMD −0.05)**. Opposing-muscle supersets allowed more reps, while supersets of two exercises for the same muscle cost volume load ([Zhang 2025](https://digibug.ugr.es/handle/10481/102755)) **[Moderate]**. Rest longer than 60 seconds gives a small hypertrophy benefit, with no clear gain beyond about 90 seconds ([Singer 2024](https://frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2024.1429789/abstract)) **[Weak]**. In a superset round, each muscle actually rests 2–3 minutes even though the clock shows 60–90 seconds between exercises, which satisfies both findings.

Three pairing rules follow **[Opinion grounded in Zhang 2025]**. Pair opposing muscles (press with row, quad with hamstring) or regions that do not compete (lateral raise with curl). Never pair two exercises with the same prime mover, such as hip thrust with Romanian deadlift or bench with incline press. And never superset a heavy squat or deadlift with anything demanding. Cap any one muscle at **about 10–11 weighted sets per session**, and move further volume to another day instead of stacking it **[Weak basis]**.

### Stop one to three reps short on heavy lifts, closer on small ones

| Exercise type | Examples in her week | Target reps in reserve | Last set | Rest |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Heavy barbell lift | Deadlift, squat, bench, overhead press, hip thrust | 2–3 early in a block, 1–2 late | Never to failure | 2.5–3 min |
| Dumbbell or single-leg compound | Incline DB press, split squat, DB row, DB Romanian deadlift | 1–2 | 1 | 90–120 s |
| Machine or cable compound | Cable row, pulldown, assisted pull-up | 1–2 | 0–1 late in a block | 75–120 s |
| Isolation | Leg curl, leg extension, lateral raise, curl, triceps extension, calf raise, abduction | 1 | 0 allowed | 60–90 s |
| Jumps, swings, sleds | Primer, finisher | Movement quality limits it | Never to failure | As needed |

Growth improves as sets end closer to failure, while strength barely depends on it ([Robinson 2024](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-024-02069-2)) **[Moderate for growth; Strong for strength]**. In a within-person trial that included trained women, failure and stopping 1–2 reps short grew the quadriceps identically (**+0.181 vs +0.182 cm**), and failure cost more fatigue ([Refalo 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38393985/)) **[Moderate]**. Failure on big lifts delayed recovery by **24–48 hours** in trained men ([Morán-Navarro 2017](https://www.springermedizin.de/time-course-of-recovery-following-resistance-training-leading-or/15095268)) **[Moderate; men]**. Her more likely error runs the other way: trained women chose loads of about **42% of one-rep max when training alone versus 51% with a trainer** ([Ratamess 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18296962/)) **[Moderate]**. That is why her sessions are built around a genuinely heavy main lift with explicit reps-in-reserve targets.

Progression is **double progression**: add reps within the range, then add the smallest load step and drop back to the bottom of the range. Adding reps and adding load produced similar results in trained lifters ([Plotkin 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36199287/)) **[Moderate]**. Wide ranges such as 8–12 also absorb the large relative jump between dumbbells, since going from 10 to 12 kg is a 20% increase. Within each 4–6-week block, reps in reserve ramp from about 3 in week 1 toward 1 by the last week. A lighter week follows every 5–8 weeks, mirroring the **6.4 ± 1.7 days every 5.6 ± 2.3 weeks** that surveyed strength and physique athletes report ([Rogerson 2024](https://www.pubmed.gov/38499934/)) **[Opinion; survey data]**. Alternating blocks can tilt the emphasis: a strength block with mains at 4 × 3–5 and 3 accessory sets, then a hypertrophy block with mains at 3 × 6–8 and one extra accessory set **[Opinion]**.

## Anchors stay put, accessories rotate, and every slot has three swaps

### Varying exercises neither adds nor costs muscle, but random shuffling is a risk

Her "some exercises the same, some different" instinct matches the evidence. The only trial in young women found **virtually identical strength and muscle gains** whether exercise selection was varied or held constant over 10 weeks ([Kassiano 2024](https://ro.ecu.edu.au/ecuworks2022-2026/4912)) **[Moderate]**. A systematic review of 8 trials in men concluded that moderate, *planned* variation helps regional growth and dynamic strength, while excessive or *random* variation can compromise gains ([Kassiano 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35438660/)) **[Moderate]**. When an app randomised exercises every session for trained men, strength gains matched the fixed group. Only the fixed group grew one quadriceps head (the vastus intermedius), but the varied group's intrinsic motivation rose ([Baz-Valle 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6934277)) **[Weak–Moderate]**.

Two small trials found that systematically varied exercises grew every measured quadriceps or arm region, while fixed exercises missed some sites ([Fonseca 2014](https://paulogentil.com/pdf/Changes%20in%20exercises%20are%20more%20effective%20than%20in%20loading%20schemes%20to%20improve%20muscle%20strength.pdf); [Costa 2021](https://ouci.dntb.gov.ua/works/4Eg3qax9)) **[Weak]**. A study of squat variations in recreationally trained women found no such regional advantage ([Enes et al.](https://www.citedrive.com/en/discovery/the-effects-of-squat-variations-on-strength-and-quadriceps-hypertrophy-adaptations-in-recreationally-trained-females)) **[Weak, conflicting]**.

Strength is the part that argues for consistency. One-rep max is highly specific to the lift practised. Merely testing an arm's one-rep max raised it as much as training did, without any growth ([PubMed 27875635](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27875635/)) **[Moderate]**. In a squat versus hip thrust trial, each group got stronger mainly in its own lift ([Plotkin 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2023.1279170/full)). Experienced lifters also need about **2–3 familiarisation sessions** before one-rep max values stabilise on a new exercise ([SciELO RBME](https://scielo.br/j/rbme/a/w3KnY8pk9fQxy7qV5L39jfP/?lang=en)) **[Moderate]**. Swapping a main lift every few weeks throws that practice away.

### Exercises that load a stretched muscle are the best-tested defaults

Where direct trials exist, they favour loading muscles at long lengths. Seated leg curls grew the hamstrings **+14% versus +9%** for lying curls ([Maeo 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33009197/)) **[Moderate]**. Overhead cable triceps extensions grew the whole triceps **+19.9% versus +13.9%** for the same movement with arms at the sides ([Maeo, Eur J Sport Sci](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/full/10.1080/17461391.2022.2100279)) **[Moderate]**. In 45 untrained women, leg extensions over the deep-bend part of the range grew the lower quadriceps more than full range ([Pedrosa 2022 summary](https://getfitcraft.com/science/lengthened-partials-research)) **[Weak–Moderate; secondary source]**. In young women, calf raises in the stretched range grew the gastrocnemius more than short-range partials ([Kassiano 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37015016/)) **[Moderate]**.

For glutes, hip thrusts and back squats produced similar growth on MRI, and squats added quadriceps and adductor growth ([Plotkin 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2023.1279170/full)) **[Moderate]**. That is why her week contains both, plus a hinge. A widely cited trial claiming squats beat hip thrusts in trained women (Barbalho 2020) has been retracted, so no trustworthy trial compares the two in trained women. For lats, chest, delts and biceps no direct trials were found. Those defaults follow ease of loading and a good stretch **[Opinion]**: lat pulldown, dumbbell or machine press, cable lateral raise, incline curl.

### Two-thirds of slots stay constant and one or two per session rotate each block

| Day | Anchors (kept 8–12 weeks while progressing) | Rotating slots (new variant each 4–6-week block) |
|---|---|---|
| Mon | Deadlift · barbell hip thrust · seated leg curl | DB Romanian deadlift → 45° back extension → cable pull-through · hip abduction (cable ↔ machine) |
| Tue | Bench press · seated cable row · lat pulldown · cable lateral raise | Incline DB press → incline Smith or machine press · incline DB curl → behind-body cable curl → preacher curl |
| Thu | Overhead press · pull-up or chin-up · cable lateral raise · push-up | One-arm DB row → machine row · overhead cable extension → overhead DB extension → pushdown |
| Fri | Squat · leg extension · standing calf raise | Bulgarian split squat → reverse lunge → step-up · lying leg curl → Nordic curl (assisted) |

These rules are **[Opinion]**, built on the evidence above, and the generator can encode them directly. A main lift stays fixed for the whole block. It carries into the next block if her estimated one-rep max or reps at a given load improved. After two blocks, or a plateau of two or more weeks, it rotates to a *close* variant, such as back squat to hack squat or barbell to dumbbell Romanian deadlift, so most of the learned skill carries over. It never rotates mid-block except for pain. About 60–70% of slots stay unchanged from one block to the next, and one or two slots per session rotate. A rotating slot moves to the same muscle at a different angle or muscle length. Where a trial favours the stretched version (overhead extension, seated curl), that version runs two blocks for every one of its partner.

New exercises are limited to **one per session and two per block**. They are introduced as machines, cables or dumbbells rather than new barbell lifts, and their first two exposures run at an effort of about 7 out of 10 with no progression judgement. Nothing is reshuffled at random between sessions.

Choice works best when bounded. Before each block she picks one of three pre-vetted options for each rotating slot, and that pick stays for the block. More autonomous motivation is consistently linked to exercise participation, and intrinsic motivation best predicts long-term adherence ([Teixeira 2012](https://ijbnpa.biomedcentral.com/track/pdf/10.1186/1479-5868-9-78)) **[Moderate; mostly observational]**. A preference-based approach was non-inferior to standard prescription for performance, with similar adherence of **69.5% vs 71.2%** ([APART abstract](https://world.physio/congress-proceeding/comparing-autonomy-and-perceptions-approach-resistance-training-apart-standard)) **[Weak]**. In one small trial, though, letting people choose their own loads raised adherence but produced less strength than a no-choice prescription ([SCAPPS](https://www.scapps.org/jems/index.php/1/article/view/915)) **[Weak]**. So she chooses *exercises*, while *loads* stay rule-driven.

### A dislike retires an exercise; a busy machine swaps it for one day

No trial tests swap systems. But because varied and constant selection give similar gains in women ([Kassiano 2024](https://ro.ecu.edu.au/ecuworks2022-2026/4912)), a like-for-like swap costs little. Swaps should stay within the same movement pattern and primary muscle, at a similar loading potential and muscle length. Muscle-activation (EMG) rankings are a poor guide, because the hip thrust's higher glute activation did not produce more growth ([Plotkin 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2023.1279170/full)) **[Moderate for that point]**.

The reason for a swap changes what happens next **[Opinion]**. **Busy** means a one-day swap, and the default returns. **Don't like** retires the exercise, and the swap becomes the default for the rest of the block and beyond. **Pain** gives the lowest-stress option, a flag and no progression in that slot until it clears.

Her two dislikes are already resolved. The chest-supported dumbbell row is replaced by a seated cable row. It is worth asking whether the chest pad was the problem; if so, chest-padded machine rows should also be ruled out. The medicine-ball chest pass is a power drill, not a strength or size exercise, so it is simply dropped. The jump primer covers the athletic slot, with slams, broad jumps or kettlebell swings as alternatives.

## A one-word reply logs a normal day, and silence holds the load

### Progressing loads needs two facts per exercise, not a full log

The app currently asks for replies like `A 57.5 x 10 10 9`. When she does not reply, it progresses loads from what it *prescribed*: progression code "reads the stored plans back per movement" ([local design spec](file:///home/user/workout-loop/docs/superpowers/specs/2026-08-26-workout-loop-design.md)). So silence is treated as success. That is exactly the "assuming" she objected to.

Effort-based progression studies show that far less data is enough. Trained men who set loads by effort rating gained as much strength and muscle as men on percentage plans, with small effects favouring effort ratings (squat effect size about 0.50) ([Helms 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29628895/)) **[Moderate]**. Squatters who set loads by reps in reserve gained more than a fixed-percentage group (**back squat +10.8% vs +7.1%**) ([Graham & Cleather 2021](https://research.stmarys.ac.uk/3067/)) **[Moderate]**. Every one of 30 autoregulated training groups in a 14-study review improved ([Larsen 2021](https://www.peerj.com/articles/10663)) **[Moderate]**. Per exercise, the next load needs only two facts: whether she did what was written, and roughly how hard it felt. Every one of these trials studied trained men, not a young woman using a coarse three-level scale.

Self-monitoring is worth keeping. Across 122 behaviour-change evaluations, self-monitoring explained the most variation in effect, and it worked better when combined with feedback or goal review (**d = 0.42 vs 0.26**) ([Michie 2009](https://ore.exeter.ac.uk/repository/handle/10871/13754)) **[Strong that it matters; no specific format tested]**. No study shows how much detail a log needs. One self-monitoring review contained a single exercise study and rated its own evidence weak ([Burke 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21185970/)). A meta-analysis of phone-prompt studies found "no compelling relationship" between questionnaire burden and compliance, though heterogeneity was huge ([Williams 2021](https://jmir.org/2021/3/e17023/PDF)) **[Moderate]**. So keeping logging short is a judgement about her stated preference, not a proven compliance effect.

### The reply grammar: `done`, plus only what differed

| She types | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `done` (also `d`, `ok`, `yes`) | Every exercise as written, effort about right |
| `done A easy` or `done A+` | All as written; A felt easy (3+ reps left) |
| `done C hard` or `done C-` | All as written; C was a grind (0–1 reps left) |
| `B 50 8 8 6` | B differed: 50 kg, reps 8, 8, 6 (today's format, now optional) |
| `A 60` | A done at 60 kg, all reps as written |
| `skip D` | D not done |
| `didn't train` or `no` | Session not done; repeat it next time |
| `pic B` | Show B's picture next time |

Matching ignores case, items can come in any order, and commas, spaces or new lines all work as separators. A full exception reply looks like `done A+ C- skip D`. The email asks for it in one line directly under the exercise list: "**Reply `done`** if it went as written. Otherwise just the differences, e.g. `A easy`, `C hard`, `B 50 8 8 6`, `skip D`." The format uses plain `+` and `-` because a phone keyboard has no ✓ or true minus sign.

| Signal | Next time |
|---|---|
| As written, "right" | +1 rep per set within the range; at the top of the range, add the smallest load step and drop to the bottom |
| `easy` / `+` | Add the load step now (2.5 kg on barbell and lower-body lifts; next dumbbell or ~1–2 kg on upper body) |
| `hard` / `-`, or reps short | Repeat the same load and reps |
| `hard` twice running on one exercise | Reduce load 5–10% |
| `skip` | Repeat the prescription; no progression credit |
| Numbers given | Numbers become the new baseline |

**With no reply**, the session is stored as "not logged", not as done. The next email carries the same loads, with one line at the top: "No log for Tuesday, so loads are unchanged. Reply `done` to that email if you did it." A late `done` applies retroactively before the next email is built. Two silent sessions in a row still only hold loads, with no automatic deload and no extra nagging. Holding is the safe failure: a missed progression step costs one session, while a wrong increase pushes her past what she can do and erodes the trust she has already said matters.

Every reply gets a one-line acknowledgement, such as "Logged Mon: all as written · A easy → next Mon 72.5 kg · C hard → hold 35 kg". That supplies the feedback Michie found strengthens self-monitoring, and it makes the system's interpretation visible and easy to correct. The progression rules and load steps are practitioner convention, not values tested in the cited trials **[Opinion]**.

### A once-a-block check keeps "done" from drifting light

The scheme's weak point is calibration. Lifters underpredict their remaining reps by about **0.95 reps** on average ([Halperin 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34542869/)) **[Moderate]**, and women training alone pick lighter loads than they do with a trainer ([Ratamess 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18296962/)) **[Moderate]**. Trained women judge reps in reserve as accurately as men, to within about **0.65 reps** on the bench ([Refalo 2024, JSCR](https://www.researchgate.net/profile/Martin-Refalo-2/publication/375687905_Accuracy_of_Intraset_Repetitions-in-Reserve_Predictions_During_the_Bench_Press_Exercise_in_Resistance-Trained_Male_and_Female_Subjects/links/65d9591fadc608480ae7db13/Accuracy-of-Intraset-Repetitions-in-Reserve-Predictions-During-the-Bench-Press-Exercise-in-Resistance-Trained-Male-and-Female-Subjects.pdf)) **[Moderate]**, so the problem is practice, not sex. One optional addition keeps the effort-based logging honest at almost no cost: once per block, the last set of one machine or isolation exercise per session is marked "as many reps as you can". She replies with that single number, for example `C 14`. If she clears the top of the range by 3 or more, the load jumps a step **[Opinion]**.

## Line drawings under a one-screen list make the email usable mid-set

### Simple drawings beat photos, and fewer pictures beat more

No trial compares line drawings with photographs for exercise technique, so the case comes from health communication and multimedia learning. A review of health-communication research found that pictures closely tied to text raised attention, comprehension, recall and adherence. It also reported that **"line drawings seem to facilitate comprehension more than do shaded drawings or photographs"** ([Houts 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16122896/); [record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/7637576_The_Role_of_Pictures_in_Improving_Health_Communication_A_Review_of_Research_on_Attention_Comprehension_Recall_and_Adherence)) **[Moderate; not exercise-specific]**. Illustrated instructions raised home-exercise compliance from **38.1% to 77.4%** in back-pain patients ([Schneiders 1998](https://search.pedro.org.au/search-results/record-detail/11882)), but added nothing in a stroke trial ([ScienceDirect 2015](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1569186115000388)) **[Moderate, conflicting]**.

Laboratory multimedia research is stronger and consistent. Removing extraneous material improved learning in 23 of 23 tests (median **d = 0.86**). Cueing what matters worked in 24 of 28 (d = 0.41). Placing words beside the picture they describe worked in 9 of 9 (d = 1.22) ([Mayer, Cambridge Handbook](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/cambridge-handbook-of-multimedia-learning/principles-for-reducing-extraneous-processing-in-multimedia-learning-coherence-signaling-redundancy-spatial-contiguity-and-temporal-contiguity-principles/CD5B7AE1279A9AB81F8EEBB53DBEC86E)) **[Strong in the lab; applying it to an email is inference]**. Animation beat still pictures overall (d = 0.37), and by far the most for **procedural-motor content (d = 1.06)** ([Höffler & Leutner 2007](https://www.leibniz-ipn.de/en/research/publications/instructional-animation-versus-static-pictures-a-meta-analysis)). A later meta-analysis found a smaller overall edge, g = 0.226 ([Berney & Bétrancourt 2016](https://tecfa.unige.ch/perso/sandra/pdf/Earli2016_berney_betrancourt_FINAL.pdf)) **[Moderate–Strong]**.

The demonstrator matters less than his looks suggest. Among non-athletic female observers, a model's *ability* was a stronger similarity cue than the model's sex ([J Sport Exerc Psychol 1992](https://journals.humankinetics.com/view/journals/jsep/14/3/article-p237.xml)) **[Weak]**. Women who watched "perfect-looking" exercise models reported lower confidence about how they look while exercising, though not lower confidence in doing the exercise ([Fleming & Martin Ginis 2004](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/abs/10.1080/10413200490260080)) **[Weak–Moderate]**. Most YouTube home-workout instructors are young, thin or athletic women ([Chung 2025](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/13591053241242534)).

Her current pictures are two stills of a male demonstrator, with gym backgrounds and shading, shrunk to 400 px. The recommendation **[Moderate inference; details Opinion]**: a simple line drawing of a female or neutral, non-idealised figure on white. Show two frames, start → end, side by side directly under the exercise name, each about 45% of the column width and exported at double resolution. Pictures appear only for exercises that are new this block, the first two weeks of a block, or exercises she flags with `pic B`. A two-frame looping GIF is a cheap step toward the motion advantage if her mail client animates it and it stays under about 50 kB. That needs a test on her phone.

### Licensable line art exists, but coverage needs checking

The current source, free-exercise-db, is labelled Unlicense (public domain) ([GitHub](https://github.com/yuhonas/free-exercise-db)). But it was restructured from a dataset whose README says nothing about where the photos came from or who owns them ([wrkout/exercises.json](https://github.com/wrkout/exercises.json)). The legal risk is low for a single private user, but the provenance is unresolved **[Weak evidence of a problem]**.

wger is the cleanest verifiable alternative. Its exercise data carries per-entry Creative Commons licences ([wger README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/wger-project/wger/master/README.md)). Each image stores its own licence and author, a `style` field where `1` means line art, and an `is_ai_generated` flag ([wger image model](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/wger-project/wger/master/wger/exercises/models/image.py)). Everkinetic's open data is CC BY-SA 4.0 ([GitHub](https://github.com/everkinetic/data)), and WorkoutLabs sells illustration licences ([WorkoutLabs](https://workoutlabs.com/exercise-illustrations-licensing/)).

The sourcing order is therefore wger line art, then Everkinetic, then free-exercise-db as a fallback. Where none fits, the email shows no picture and gives a one-line cue instead. The footer carries a one-line credit such as "Illustrations: wger.de contributors, CC BY-SA 4.0". How many of her exercises have wger line art is unknown and probably patchy. Querying `https://wger.de/api/v2/exerciseimage/?style=1` against her exercise list is the first build step **[Opinion]**.

### The email puts what, how heavy and how many on the first screen

Mid-session she needs to find "what's next, how heavy, how many" in under a second. Mobile-reading research is mixed. A 2011 Nielsen Norman Group study found comprehension on a phone-sized screen at 48% of desktop, and a 2016 follow-up found no practical difference, yet both recommend cutting content for mobile ([UX Magazine on NN/g](https://uxmag.com/articles/why-reading-on-mobile-is-uniquely-challenging)) **[Moderate, contested]**. Email guides converge on **body text of at least 16 px** ([Email Love](https://help.emaillove.com/303eabcb09b781be866af7d3b0f4e14a?pvs=25)) and **a single column about 600 px wide** ([Mailchimp](https://templates.mailchimp.com/design/mobile-friendliness/)) **[Opinion; industry guidance]**. One technical fact shapes the order of the email. **Gmail clips HTML over about 102 KB, and the Gmail Android app shows no "view entire message" link** ([Email on Acid](https://www.emailonacid.com/?p=26940); [MxToolbox](https://mxtoolbox.com/dmarc/email/email-clipping)) **[Moderate]**. So the reply instruction belongs near the top, not at the bottom.

## Conclusion

The tension between her preferences and the evidence turns out to be smaller than it looks. "Two muscles a day, few exercises" sounds like a once-a-week body-part split, which would make twice-weekly frequency and 10+ weekly sets hard to reach. But once the pairs rotate along an upper/lower axis, the only cost is a small third muscle appearing as a superset partner. Where the evidence is thinnest is not the split. It is the parts that decide whether the split works for *her*. A woman training alone tends to choose loads that are too light and to underestimate how many reps she had left. A logging system that progresses on silence compounds errors in the other direction. Holding loads on silence, an explicit `easy` marker and a once-a-block rep check make her the source of truth for progression. That probably matters more for "lifting heavier" than any choice between the three templates.

What should change next comes from her, not from more literature. Her class type decides the finishers, the glute accessory volume and whether Template C is worth it. Two to three weeks of replies will show whether six-exercise upper days feel cluttered and which muscles lag. A two-version email test at the rack will settle the layout faster than any usability study. Checking wger's line-art coverage against her exercise list is the first concrete build task. No trial has tested any whole four-day program in trained young women. Most variation and effort-based progression trials used men, and drawings versus photos has never been tested for exercise technique. So the Opinion-graded defaults above are starting points that her own replies should overrule.
