# Evidence Review: Progression, Effort, Volume, Deloads, Warm-ups, Exercise Selection, Sex-Specific Issues, Rest

**Context:** single user, healthy woman about 22, 3-4 sessions/week of about 60 min, goals lean muscle and strength, arms a priority. Training age unknown.
**Date:** 2026-11-13.

**Confidence scale:** **High** means several meta-analyses agree. **Moderate** means one meta-analysis or several consistent RCTs. **Low** means a few small RCTs, mechanistic data or expert consensus. **Very low / missing** means essentially no direct trials.

**How sources were checked:** each source marked [v] was confirmed in this session through a primary or index record (PubMed, PMC, publisher site, SportRxiv). Each source marked [m] is cited from prior knowledge of the literature and was not re-fetched, so check its exact figures before quoting them.

**A general caveat:** most RCTs last 6-12 weeks and use small samples (n of about 10-40). Many use young men, and untrained subjects are over-represented. Hypertrophy is measured with ultrasound or MRI at one or two sites. The evidence on *differences between reasonable programs* is therefore much weaker than the evidence that *any reasonable program works*. The new ACSM position stand says the same thing: getting started and training consistently matter most.

---

## 0. The overarching anchor: ACSM 2026 Position Stand

- ACSM (2026). *Resistance Training Prescription for Muscle Function, Hypertrophy, and Physical Performance in Healthy Adults: An Overview of Reviews.* Med Sci Sports Exerc. doi:10.1249/mss.0000000000003897. It is an overview of 137 systematic reviews covering more than 30,000 participants. [v] (https://acsm.org/resistance-training-guidelines-update-2026/ ; infographic https://acsm.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Resistance-Training-Position-Stand-infographic.pdf)
- **Key points:**
  - Train all major muscle groups at least 2 times per week.
  - For strength, heavier loads (80% of 1RM or more), full range of motion, 2-3 sets, lifts placed early in the session, and at least 2 sessions per week work well.
  - Some variables did *not* consistently change outcomes for the average healthy adult: training to failure, machines versus free weights, exercise complexity, set structure, time under tension and periodization model.
- **Confidence: High** that the basics matter far more than the details.

## 1. Progressive overload methods

### Evidence

| Method | Key evidence | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| **Load progression vs rep progression** | Plotkin et al. 2022, PeerJ 10:e14142, https://peerj.com/articles/14142 [v]. In 43 trained people over 8 weeks, adding load (fixed reps) and adding reps (fixed load) gave similar hypertrophy and strength. Strength slightly favoured load (about +2 kg) and rectus femoris growth slightly favoured reps; neither difference was meaningful. | Both work. **Moderate** |
| **Double progression** (work through a rep range, then add load) | No head-to-head RCT exists. It mixes the two arms Plotkin tested, which suggests it is at least as good. It is the standard recommendation of Helms (*Muscle & Strength Pyramid*), Nuckols/SBS and Israetel. | **Low** (by inference), but very practical |
| **RPE/RIR-based autoregulation vs percentage-based** | Helms et al. 2018, Front Physiol 9:247, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5877330 [v]: in trained men both were effective, with a small, non-significant 1RM edge for RPE. Hickmott et al. 2022, Sports Med Open, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8762534/ [v]: in a meta-analysis of 15 studies, load autoregulation versus percentage gave no significant 1RM difference (MD about 2 kg, p=0.09). A later network meta-analysis (Zhang et al., reported as 2025; not verified, seen only in secondary sources) ranks autoregulation above percentage. | Roughly equal, maybe a small edge for autoregulation in trained lifters. **Low-Moderate** |
| **Percentage-based** | It needs a known or estimated 1RM, which a lifter of unknown experience who logs little will not have. Percentages also map onto different RIR across lifts and people. | Effective but poor fit for this app. **Moderate** |
| **Linear load progression** (add weight each session) | It is the classic novice approach (Rippetoe, GZCL, NSCA). Untrained people gain strength quickly, mostly through neural adaptation, so it works for weeks to months. It stalls quickly on small lifts, where a fixed increment is a large percentage. No RCT shows it beats double progression. | Fine for beginners on big lifts. **Low** (consensus) |
| **Volume progression** (adding sets) | Pelland et al. 2024/2026 (see section 3) show volume has a positive dose-response with diminishing returns. No RCT shows that *progressively adding sets* over a block beats fixed moderate volume when effort is matched. Some RCTs ramp volume (e.g. Aube et al. 2022 [m]) and find no clear benefit over constant moderate volume in trained men. | Use it as a lever when load and reps stall, not as the default engine. **Low** |

**Accuracy of RIR self-rating.** Halperin et al. 2022, Sports Med (scoping review and meta-analysis), https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/67 [v]:
- People underpredict reps to failure by about 1 rep on average, with high heterogeneity.
- Accuracy is better close to failure and with lower rep counts.
- Training status did not clearly matter.
- **Implication:** coarse ratings work. A done/easy/hard tap is a usable proxy, roughly easy ≈ ≥3 RIR, done ≈ 1-2 RIR, hard ≈ 0 RIR or missed reps. Expect noise of ±1 rep. **Moderate**

### What works best and is safest

- For hypertrophy, the method matters less than (a) reaching enough effort (section 2), (b) doing enough sets (section 3), and (c) logging consistently so the lifter keeps progressing. **Moderate-High**
- For strength, keeping some heavy work (rep ranges of about 3-8 on main lifts) helps through specificity. Schoenfeld et al. 2021 (*Loading recommendations for muscle strength, hypertrophy and local endurance: a re-examination of the repetition continuum*, Sports 9:32 [m]) found hypertrophy similar across about 6-30 reps when sets go near failure, while strength favours heavier loads. ACSM 2026 says the same. **High**
- Injuries in recreational lifting are rare per hour of training. No evidence ranks the progression methods on injury. Small increments and stopping 1-3 RIR on heavy compound lifts are reasonable precautions, not proven ones. **Low**
- **Simplest to automate from minimal logging: double progression with an RIR check.** Per exercise, the app needs only reps achieved plus one tap (easy/done/hard). Percentage-based training needs a 1RM. Pure RPE needs reliable ratings on every set. Linear progression fails on small lifts. **High (practical), Low (efficacy evidence)**

### Concrete parameters (expert consensus with plausible rationale; not trial-derived)

**Rep ranges by exercise class** (all hypertrophy-effective when sets go near failure):

| Exercise class | Rep range |
|---|---|
| Heavy compound (squat, deadlift/RDL, bench, row, overhead press) | 5-8 (strength bias) or 6-10 |
| Secondary compound and machines (leg press, lat pulldown, hip thrust, DB press) | 8-12 |
| Isolation (curls, triceps extensions, lateral raises, leg curl/extension, calves) | 10-15 (up to 20 for lateral raises/calves) |

Lighter isolation work with higher reps avoids load jumps that are too big and is easier on joints. **Moderate** that ranges are interchangeable for growth; **Low** for the exact boundaries.

**Load increments** (smallest available; in a commercial gym, often 2.5 kg plates or 1-2.5 kg DB steps):

| Lift type | Increment |
|---|---|
| Lower-body barbell (squat, RDL, hip thrust) | +2.5-5 kg (beginner), +2.5 kg (intermediate) |
| Upper-body barbell (bench, row, OHP) | +1-2.5 kg (use fractional plates if available) |
| Dumbbells | the next DB, usually +1-2 kg per hand, which can be a 10-20% jump on light DBs |
| Machines/cables | the next pin, usually 2.5-5 kg (or a 1.25 kg add-on) |
| Rule of thumb | an increment of about 2-5% of the working load; if the smallest step is above about 7-10%, progress reps first and widen the rep range |

Small upper-body and isolation lifts in women often hit this problem: going from 6 to 8 kg DB curls is +33%. Rely more on rep progression there, and consider a wider range such as 8-20. **Low** (consensus; Plotkin 2022 supports rep progression as valid)

**When to increase, hold or reduce:**
- **Increase load** when every working set reaches the top of the rep range with the target RIR or better ("done" or "easy"). If the tap is "easy" at the top of the range, increase even sooner, or by two increments for a beginner.
- **Add reps** next session (same load) when the lifter is inside the range and the tap is "done".
- **Hold** when the tap is "hard" but the reps were hit, or the reps fell 1-2 short.
- **Reduce load by about 5-10%** when the lifter misses the bottom of the range on two consecutive exposures, or the tap is "hard" with failed reps twice in a row.
- **Stall handling** (no progress in reps or load across about 3 exposures):
  1. Reduce load by about 10% and rebuild (a reset).
  2. If it stalls again, swap the exercise for a variant (e.g. barbell to DB bench).
  3. Check overall fatigue and volume.
- These rules descend from double progression and "reset" schemes (e.g. Starting Strength/GZCL/Helms). **Low** (no trials compare stall protocols)

**Beginner vs intermediate:**
- **Beginner** (under about 6-12 months of consistent training): progress quickly. The lifter can often add load every session on big lifts. Fewer sets are enough (about 6-10 per muscle per week). Technique practice argues for higher frequency on main lifts. Strength gains early are large and largely neural. **Moderate**
- **Intermediate:** progress weekly or per rep range, use more volume (about 10-20 sets), and RIR becomes the main regulator. **Low-Moderate**

## 2. Proximity to failure

**Evidence:**
- Refalo et al. 2023, Sports Med 53:649-665, doi:10.1007/s40279-022-01784-y [v]. Training to failure vs not did not reliably produce more hypertrophy when volume was matched. The relationship may be non-linear, with similar growth at 0 RIR and a few RIR. https://dro.deakin.edu.au/articles/journal_contribution/Influence_of_Resistance_Training_Proximity-to-Failure_on_Skeletal_Muscle_Hypertrophy_A_Systematic_Review_with_Meta-analysis/22030796
- Robinson et al. 2024, Sports Med (meta-regressions; RIR estimated, not measured) [v]. Hypertrophy increases as sets get closer to failure (a negative slope for RIR). Strength is essentially flat across the RIR range tested. The authors call the analysis exploratory. https://rke.abertay.ac.uk/en/publications/exploring-the-dose-response-relationship-between-estimated-resist/
- Grgic et al. 2022, J Sport Health Sci (failure vs non-failure meta) [m]. No significant difference for strength or hypertrophy overall. In trained subjects there was a possible small hypertrophy benefit from failure.
- Vieira et al. 2021, JSCR [m]. Similar conclusions on strength.
- ACSM 2026 [v]: training to failure has no consistent effect for the average adult.
- **Costs of failure** [m]:
  - Morán-Navarro et al. 2017 and Pareja-Blanco et al. 2017 (velocity loss studies) found failure increases recovery time (24-48 h+ for neuromuscular and hormonal markers).
  - High velocity loss (40%+) in squats reduced strength/power gains relative to 20% loss while giving similar or slightly more hypertrophy.

**Conclusions:**
- Hypertrophy needs sets that are "hard enough", roughly 0-3 RIR. Closer is probably slightly better, with diminishing returns and higher fatigue cost. **Moderate**
- Strength does not need failure. 1-4 RIR on heavy work is fine. **Moderate**
- Failure does not "hurt" outcomes in the meta-analyses, but it adds fatigue. On heavy free-weight compound lifts it adds technical risk (no strong injury data). **Low** on injury, **Moderate** on fatigue.

**Prescription by exercise type** (consensus from Helms, Refalo, Israetel and others, consistent with the data above):

| Exercise type | Target RIR | Failure? |
|---|---|---|
| Heavy free-weight compound (squat, deadlift/RDL, barbell bench, OHP) | 1-3 RIR | Avoid |
| Machine/cable compound (leg press, pulldown, chest press, hip thrust) | 0-2 RIR | Last set occasionally to failure |
| Isolation (curls, extensions, raises, leg curl/extension) | 0-1 RIR | Last set to failure is fine |
| Beginners | 2-3 RIR on most sets, with occasional AMRAP sets to calibrate RIR | |

Beginners' RIR estimates improve when they occasionally test failure on safe exercises (Halperin 2022). **Low-Moderate**

## 3. Weekly volume and frequency

**Evidence:**
- **Schoenfeld, Ogborn & Krieger 2017**, J Sports Sci 35:1073 [m/v via secondary]. There is a graded dose-response: more weekly sets give more growth, and 10+ sets per week beat fewer.
- **Baz-Valle et al. 2022**, J Hum Kinet 81:199, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8884877 [v]. In trained men, 12-20 sets per week was suggested as optimal. More than 20 sets gave no additional benefit for quads or biceps. Only 6 studies were included.
- **Pelland et al. 2024 preprint, published 2026**, Sports Med (*The resistance training dose-response: meta-regressions exploring the effects of weekly volume and frequency on muscle hypertrophy and strength gain*), https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/460 [v]:
  - Covers 67 studies and more than 2,000 participants.
  - Volume is positively associated with both hypertrophy and strength. Diminishing returns are much sharper for strength.
  - Hypertrophy keeps rising (with decreasing marginal gain) into high volumes. Strength largely plateaus after a few sets per week.
  - Frequency had clear effects only on strength, not on hypertrophy, once volume was accounted for.
  - **Fractional counting:** sets for indirectly trained muscles were scored 1 (total), 0.5 (fractional) or 0 (direct). The fractional method best predicted hypertrophy; the direct method best predicted strength. This is the best empirical support for counting, for example, a row as 0.5 of a biceps set.
  - Model fit was modest.
- **Schoenfeld, Grgic & Krieger 2019**, J Sports Sci 37:1286 [v]. When volume is equated, frequency does not change hypertrophy. Higher frequency matters mainly because it lets the lifter spread more volume.
- **Androulakis-Korakakis et al. 2020**, Sports Med (*The minimum effective training dose required to increase 1RM strength in resistance-trained men*) [m]. Even 1 set of 6-12 reps at 70-85% 1RM, 2-3 times per week, increases strength for 8-12 weeks. Minimal doses work.
- **ACSM 2026** [v]: at least 2 times per week per muscle; 2-3 sets per exercise for strength.

**Conclusions:**
- **Beginner:** about 6-10 direct (or fractional) sets per muscle per week give most of the available gains. Do not start at 20. **Moderate**
- **Intermediate:** about 10-20 fractional sets per muscle per week. More than 20 has uncertain extra value and is limited by time (60 min × 3-4 sessions is about 60-80 total working sets per week across all muscles, so 10-14 per major muscle is realistic). **Moderate**
- **Priority muscles (arms):** sit at the upper end, about 12-18 fractional sets per week. Pelland suggests hypertrophy keeps climbing with volume, and arms recover fast and are cheap to train. **Low-Moderate**
- **Frequency:** train each muscle 2 or more times per week. This is mainly practical for spreading volume (and for practising technique on lifts). Also cap productive per-session volume at roughly 6-10 hard sets per muscle per session; there is weak evidence of a per-session ceiling. **Moderate** for 2x; **Low** for the per-session cap.
- **Fractional counting rule:** direct (primary mover) = 1.0; meaningful synergist = 0.5; stabilizer = 0. **Moderate** (Pelland), though the assignment of synergists is itself a judgement call.

**Typical 0.5 mappings:**
- Rows and pulldowns/pull-ups → biceps, rear delts
- Bench, OHP and dips → triceps, front delts
- Squat and leg press → glutes (squats load glutes substantially at depth; some count 1.0)
- RDL/hip hinge → glutes (often counted as 1.0 for both hamstrings and glutes), lower back
- Lunges/split squats → glutes, quads (counted 1.0 for both is defensible)

## 4. Deloads

**Evidence (thin):**
- **Coleman et al. 2024**, PeerJ 12:e16777, https://peerj.com/articles/16777 [v]. In 39 trained adults over 9 weeks, a 1-week complete break at mid-point gave *less* lower-body strength gain and no difference in hypertrophy, power or endurance. Note the deload was full cessation, not reduced training.
- **Bell et al. 2023**, Sports Med Open (Delphi consensus on deloading) [m]. Coaches define a deload as a planned reduction in volume (mainly) and/or intensity, typically every 4-8 weeks for about 1 week. This is expert opinion only.
- No RCT shows that planned deloads improve long-term hypertrophy in recreational lifters. Breaks of 1-3 weeks cause little to no muscle loss in trained people (e.g. Ogasawara et al. 2013 periodic vs continuous: similar gains [m]).

**Conclusions:**
- Mandatory scheduled deloads are not evidence-based for a 3-4 day per week recreational lifter. **Low** (missing evidence either way)
- They are reasonable as a fatigue-management or adherence tool, or when performance drops across several exercises. **Low**
- Missing a week costs little muscle. Do not catastrophize interruptions. **Moderate**

**Reasonable app rule:**
- Deload reactively when two or more exercises regress or "hard" taps dominate for about 2 sessions. Otherwise, optionally deload every 6-8 weeks.
- Method: keep the exercises and loads, halve the sets, and stop at about 3-4 RIR for 1 week. Avoid a complete stop, which Coleman 2024 suggests may cost a little strength.

## 5. Warm-ups

**Evidence:**
- **Fradkin et al. 2010**, JSCR 24:140, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19996770/ [v]. Warm-ups (excluding stretch-only) improved performance in 79% of criteria. There is little evidence of harm. Few good RCTs.
- **Ribeiro et al. 2020**, IJERPH 17:6882 (squat/bench, specific warm-up) [v]. Specific warm-ups with a set near working load (e.g. a set at 80% 1RM) optimized squat output. Too few, light reps were insufficient. The bench results differed. Ribeiro et al. 2021 (Motricidade, systematic review) [v] found mixed results; specific warm-up is the most consistently useful component.
- **Behm et al. 2016**, Appl Physiol Nutr Metab 41:1 [v]. Static stretching held for 60 s or more per muscle reduces performance by about 4.6%. Holds under 60 s cause only about 1.1% loss, which is mostly negated by a subsequent dynamic warm-up. There is no clear effect on injury.
- **RAMP framework** — Jeffreys 2007, *Professional Strength & Conditioning* (Raise, Activate, Mobilise, Potentiate) [m]. This is a widely used coaching framework, not an empirically validated protocol.
- **General warm-up** (5-10 min light cardio): it raises muscle temperature, which improves contractile performance (Bishop 2003 review [m]). Its additional value on top of specific warm-up sets for lifting is small and unclear. **Low**
- **Injury prevention from warm-up in lifting:** essentially no direct evidence. **Very low**

**Conclusions:**
- Specific ramp-up sets before the first heavy lift of each movement pattern are well supported for performance. **Moderate**
- A brief general warm-up is optional but harmless and good for comfort. **Low**
- Short static stretches (under 60 s per muscle) are fine if desired; avoid long holds before heavy lifting. **Moderate**
- The warm-up should come from the session's actual exercises, because specificity is the active ingredient. **Moderate** (inferred from specific-warm-up data)

**App rule-set (derives the warm-up from the plan):**
1. **Raise (3-5 min, optional, default on):** easy bike/row/incline walk.
2. **Mobilise/Activate (2-4 min):** pick 2-3 dynamic drills that match the session's first two compound exercises. Use dynamic drills, no long static holds; if the user wants static, cap each hold under 30 s.

   | Session's first lifts | Drills |
   |---|---|
   | Squat / leg press / lunge | bodyweight squats ×10, walking lunges ×6/leg, leg swings ×10/leg |
   | Hinge (RDL, hip thrust) | hip hinge with dowel ×10, glute bridges ×10, leg swings |
   | Horizontal press | band pull-aparts ×15, push-ups ×5-10, arm circles |
   | Vertical press | band pull-aparts, wall slides ×10, arm circles |
   | Pull (rows/pulldown) | band pull-aparts, scapular pull-downs ×10, cat-cow |
   | Arms-only/isolation-led | light set of the first exercise (see step 3) and band curls/pushdowns ×15 |
3. **Potentiate (ramp-up sets):** for each exercise that is the first movement of its pattern in the session (or that uses a much heavier load than anything before it), use the working weight W and target reps R:

   | Working load | Ramp |
   |---|---|
   | W ≥ about 40 kg barbell, or heavy compound | empty bar (or about 40%) × 8-10 → 60% × 5 → 80% × 2-3 → work |
   | W 20-40 kg, or moderate compound | 50% × 8 → 75% × 3-4 → work |
   | Isolation, or a muscle already warmed by an earlier exercise | 0-1 sets: 50% × 10-12, or none |

   Round loads to available plates/DBs. Never take warm-up sets near failure. Warm-up reps should stay roughly 50% or less of the target reps at the higher loads.
4. Do **not** count ramp-up sets toward weekly volume.
5. Keep the total warm-up at about 8-12 minutes, so the 60-minute session still holds about 45 minutes of working sets.

## 6. Exercise selection: making sure the intended muscle is trained

**Evidence and limits:**
- **EMG:** surface EMG amplitude does *not* reliably predict hypertrophy and should not be the basis for ranking exercises (Vigotsky et al. 2018/2022, *Interpreting signal amplitudes in surface EMG studies in sport and rehabilitation sciences* [m]). Plotkin et al. 2023 (*Hip thrust and back squat training elicit similar gluteus muscle hypertrophy and transfer similarly to the deadlift*, Front Physiol [m]) compared the hip thrust (higher glute EMG) with the squat; similar glute growth undermines EMG-based selection. **Moderate**
- **Better evidence comes from:** (a) anatomy and joint actions (which joints the muscle crosses and whether it is loaded through range); (b) longitudinal hypertrophy RCTs; (c) training at long muscle lengths.
- **Long muscle length evidence:**
  - Maeo et al. 2021, MSSE 53:825, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33009197/ [v]: seated leg curl (hip flexed, hamstrings lengthened) gave about 14% vs 9% hamstring growth compared with prone. **Moderate** (n=20, within-subject)
  - Maeo et al. 2023, Eur J Sport Sci 23:1240, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35819335/ [v]: overhead cable triceps extension gave about 1.5x more long-head growth and more whole-triceps growth than pushdowns. **Moderate** (single study, n of about 20)
  - Pedrosa et al. 2023, Sports 11:39 [v]: biceps curl partials in the initial (lengthened) range beat the final range for size and strength. **Low-Moderate**
  - Kassiano et al. 2023, JSCR [v]: in 42 young women, lengthened-partial calf raises matched full ROM. **Low**
  - Wolf et al. 2023 (partial vs full ROM meta-analysis, SportRxiv/J Trainology) [v via secondary]: full ROM is about equal to or slightly better than partials. Lengthened partials tend to beat short partials and are possibly comparable to or better than full ROM, but the evidence is unsettled. **Low-Moderate**
  - ACSM 2026 [v]: full ROM is favoured for strength.
- **Practical takeaway:** choose exercises that load the target muscle hard in its stretched position, use full ROM (emphasizing the stretch), and favour stable machine/cable/DB options for isolation work. ACSM 2026 found machines and free weights equivalent. **Moderate**

**Primary/secondary mapping and a short list of well-supported exercises** (typical gym equipment):

| Muscle | Top picks (rationale) | Counts 0.5 toward |
|---|---|---|
| **Quads** | Back/front/hack squat, leg press, Bulgarian split squat; leg extension (rectus femoris crosses the hip; leg extension grows it better than squats — Zabaleta-Korta 2021 [m]) | glutes (squat/split squat), adductors |
| **Hamstrings** | **Seated leg curl** (Maeo 2021), Romanian deadlift (hip-extension function, long-length), lying curl as an alternative | glutes, erectors (RDL) |
| **Glutes** | Hip thrust, RDL, deep squat, Bulgarian split squat, cable/machine hip abduction (upper glute); hip thrust ≈ squat for glute growth (Plotkin 2023 [m]) | hamstrings (hip thrust ~0.5), quads |
| **Chest** | Barbell/DB bench (flat and incline), machine chest press, cable/pec-deck fly (stretch at the bottom) | triceps, front delts |
| **Back (lats/upper back)** | Lat pulldown/pull-up (assisted), chest-supported row, cable row, single-arm DB row | biceps, rear delts |
| **Shoulders (side delts)** | DB/cable lateral raise (cable from behind/low gives more load at a long length); overhead press mainly hits front delts | front delts and triceps (OHP) |
| **Rear delts** | Reverse pec-deck, face pull, rear-delt cable fly | upper back |
| **Biceps** | **Incline DB curl** and **preacher/Bayesian cable curl** (lengthened emphasis; Pedrosa 2023), standard DB/EZ curl, hammer curl (brachialis/brachioradialis) | — (rows/pulldowns give biceps 0.5) |
| **Triceps** | **Overhead cable or DB extension** (Maeo 2023), skull crusher/EZ extension, pushdown (still effective, lateral/medial heads), close-grip bench/dips | — (presses give triceps 0.5) |
| **Calves** | Standing calf raise / leg-press calf raise with a deep stretch and pause (Kassiano 2023), seated calf raise (soleus) | — |
| **Abs** | Cable crunch, hanging knee/leg raise, ab wheel | — |

**Arms priority implementation:**
- 2 or more direct arm exercises per week for each of biceps and triceps, split across at least 2 sessions.
- Include at least one lengthened-position movement each: incline or Bayesian curl for biceps, overhead extension for triceps.
- Plus about 0.5-set credit from all rows/pulls (biceps) and presses (triceps).
- Target 12-18 fractional sets per week each. **Low-Moderate**

## 7. Women-specific considerations

**Evidence:**
- **Roberts, Nuckols & Krieger 2020**, JSCR 34:1448, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32218059/ [v]. Relative hypertrophy and lower-body strength gains are similar between sexes. Women showed *greater* relative upper-body strength gains (mostly untrained subjects). Refalo, Nuckols et al. 2025 (Bayesian meta) [v via secondary] also found relative growth similar between sexes. **Moderate-High**
- **Hunter 2014**, Acta Physiol 210:768, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24433272/ [v]. Women are generally *less fatigable* in isometric and lower-intensity tasks. The difference is task-dependent, and less clear for dynamic and heavy contractions. Women can often do more reps at a given %1RM and may recover faster between sets. Direct evidence that women tolerate more weekly volume for hypertrophy is lacking. **Low** (the mechanism is plausible; outcome trials are missing)
- **Menstrual cycle:**
  - **McNulty et al. 2020**, Sports Med 50:1813, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32661839/ [v]: phase effects on performance are trivial (ES −0.06, slightly lower early follicular), and the evidence is of low quality.
  - **Colenso-Semple et al. 2023**, Front Sports Act Living 5:1054542, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10076834/ [v]: an umbrella review titled "Current evidence shows no influence of women's menstrual cycle phase on acute strength performance or adaptations to resistance exercise training". It found no compelling evidence for phase-based programming.
  - **Thompson et al. 2020** (Sports Med) and **Kissow et al. 2022** (Sports Med) [v]: a few small studies favour follicular-phase-weighted training; the findings conflict and the methods are weak.
  - Hormonal contraception (Elliott-Sale et al. 2020 [v]): no basis for general guidance.
  - **Verdict:** do *not* periodize around the cycle by default. **Moderate**. Allow symptom-based flexibility instead (cramps, fatigue, poor sleep): let the user mark a day "low energy" and the app reduces sets about 30-50% or keeps loads without pushing for progression. **Low** (pragmatic, consistent with McNulty's recommendation to individualize)
- **Other relevant factors** (brief, [m]):
  - Low energy availability / RED-S impairs gains and health. Lean-muscle goals with aggressive dieting are the common risk.
  - Iron deficiency is common in menstruating women.
  - These belong in a gentle note rather than programming. **Moderate** on importance; outside the app's core.
- **Equal programming principles apply.** The same volume, effort and progression rules suit women. Differences: smaller absolute load increments are proportionally larger (see section 1), and many women start with relatively weaker upper bodies but progress quickly there. **Moderate**

## 8. Rest intervals

- **Singer et al. 2024**, Front Sports Act Living 6:1429789, https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2024.1429789/abstract [v]. In a Bayesian meta-analysis of 9 studies, resting longer than 60 s gave a small hypertrophy benefit. No appreciable difference was found beyond 90 s. Heterogeneity was substantial. **Low-Moderate**
- **Schoenfeld et al. 2016**, JSCR (3 min vs 1 min in trained men) [m]: 3 min gave more strength and some more hypertrophy, mostly by preserving volume load. **Low-Moderate**
- **Grgic et al. 2017/2018 reviews** [m]: for strength, rest of 2 min or more on multi-joint lifts preserves performance.
- **Rest-pause, supersets and antagonist pairing:** paired or superset training saves time with similar outcomes in limited studies (e.g. Robbins 2010 [m]; Iversen 2021 time-efficient training review [m]). **Low-Moderate**. This is useful in a 60-minute window, especially for arms (curl + triceps extension supersets).

**Prescription:**

| Exercise | Rest |
|---|---|
| Heavy compound | 2-3 min |
| Machine/secondary compound | 1.5-2 min |
| Isolation | 60-90 s |
| Antagonist supersets (biceps/triceps, chest/back) | allowed; about 60 s between alternations |

Practical rule: rest until breathing settles and you feel able to hit the target reps. **Moderate**

## Rules an app can implement

### Data model (minimal logging)
- Per exercise per session: `weight`, `reps per set` (or "hit target" boolean), and one tap `easy | done | hard`.
- Map the tap to approximate RIR: `easy` ≈ 3+ RIR, `done` ≈ 1-2 RIR, `hard` ≈ 0 RIR or reps missed. The ±1 rep error (Halperin 2022) is acceptable.
- Session-level optional flag: `low_energy` (sleep, illness, menstrual symptoms).

### Experience classification
- **Beginner** if the user reports under 6-12 months of consistent lifting, or by default when unknown. Promote to intermediate after about 12 weeks of logged training, or when per-session load jumps on main lifts stall (two or more resets on two or more main lifts).

### Exercise catalogue fields
- `primary_muscles` (1.0), `secondary_muscles` (0.5), `class` (heavy_compound / compound_machine / isolation), `rep_range`, `rir_target`, `increment_kg`, `rest_s`, `pattern` (squat / hinge / h_push / v_push / h_pull / v_pull / arms / calves / core), `lengthened_bias` (bool).

**Default per class:**

| Class | Reps | RIR target | Rest | Increment |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| heavy_compound | 5-8 (strength days) or 6-10 | 1-3 (never prescribe failure) | 150-180 s | lower body 2.5 kg (5 kg allowed for beginners while every set is "easy"); upper body 2.5 kg (1-1.25 kg if microplates exist) |
| compound_machine | 8-12 | 1-2 | 90-120 s | next pin (about 2.5-5 kg) |
| isolation | 10-15 (lateral raise/calf 12-20) | 0-1; last set may go to failure | 60-90 s | smallest available DB/pin |

### Progression (double progression with tap check), evaluated per exercise after each session
1. **All sets ≥ top of range:**
   - `easy` → increase load by 1 increment. A beginner on a compound with an easy tap may take 2 increments.
   - `done` → increase load by 1 increment.
   - `hard` → hold the load and aim to repeat at the top of the range.
2. **Inside the range** (all sets ≥ bottom, not all at top):
   - `easy`/`done` → same load, target +1 rep on the set(s) below the top.
   - `hard` → same load, same rep target.
3. **Any set below the bottom of the range:**
   - First occurrence → hold the load.
   - Second consecutive occurrence → reduce load 5-10% (round down to the nearest increment) and restart at the bottom of the range.
4. **Increment too large:** if the next load step is more than about 10% of the current load (common with light DBs and cable stacks), do not jump. Extend the rep range top by 3-5 reps (e.g. 10-15 → 10-20), progress reps, and jump load only when the top of the extended range is reached. After the jump, the target becomes the bottom of the normal range.
5. **Stall:** no increase in reps × load over 3 consecutive exposures →
   - Reset: −10% load and rebuild.
   - After a second stall on the same exercise within 8 weeks → swap to a catalogue variant with the same `primary_muscles` and `pattern`.
6. **Beginner linear option** (heavy_compound only): while every set is `easy` or `done` at a fixed 3×8 (or 3×5), add 1 increment each session. On the first `hard` or missed rep, switch that exercise to double progression.
7. **Calibration:** once every 4-6 weeks, on one isolation or machine exercise per muscle, prompt a last-set AMRAP ("as many as you can with good form"). If the reps exceed the target by 3 or more, raise the load immediately; this also teaches the user what RIR feels like.

### Volume and frequency
- Count weekly sets per muscle fractionally: primary = 1.0, secondary = 0.5, warm-up sets = 0.
- **Targets:**
  - Beginner: 8-10 per muscle (arms 8-12).
  - Intermediate: 10-16 per muscle (arms 12-18; glutes/quads/back up to about 16).
  - Floor: 6. Hard cap: 20.
- Every major muscle should be trained in at least 2 sessions per week. Cap about 8 hard fractional sets per muscle per session (spread the excess across sessions).
- Session budget for 60 min: about 14-20 working sets including about 8-12 min of warm-up. When over budget, use antagonist supersets for arms first, then trim lowest-priority isolation work.
- **Volume progression:** add 1 set/week to a muscle only if *all* of its exercises progressed in the last 2 weeks without `hard` taps and the muscle is below the cap. Otherwise keep volume fixed. Reset to the starting volume after a deload.
- **Suggested splits:**
  - 3 days/week: Full body A / B / C.
  - 4 days/week: Upper / Lower / Upper / Lower, with arms in both uppers plus 1-2 arm sets at the end of lower days if budget allows.

### Effort rules
- Never prescribe failure on heavy free-weight compounds.
- If a heavy compound gets `hard` with missed reps two sessions running, also reduce its next-session sets by 1.

### Deload (reactive, with an optional schedule)
- **Trigger:** two or more exercises regress (fewer reps at the same load) in the same week, or `hard` taps make up at least 60% of exercises across 2 consecutive sessions, or the user flags persistent fatigue/soreness.
- **Optional:** schedule one every 6-8 weeks if no trigger has fired.
- **Deload week:** same exercises and loads, sets × 0.5 (rounded, at least 1 per exercise), target 3-4 RIR. Do not progress, and do not prescribe complete rest by default.
- **After the deload:** resume the pre-deload loads. Volume returns to the baseline for the training-age band.

### `low_energy` day (menstrual symptoms, poor sleep, illness-adjacent)
- Sets −30-50% and RIR target +1. Keep loads; no progression is evaluated from that session.
- Do not otherwise program by cycle phase.

### Warm-up generator (built from the session plan)
1. **Raise:** 3-5 min easy cardio (default on, user can toggle off).
2. **Mobilise/Activate:** look up the `pattern` of the first two compound exercises and pick 2-3 dynamic drills from the pattern table in section 5, about 2-4 min, no static holds of 30 s or more.
3. **Ramp-up sets:** for each exercise that is the first of its `pattern` in the session, or whose load is at least 1.5× any load already used for those muscles:

   | Condition | Ramp |
   |---|---|
   | heavy_compound and W ≥ 40 kg | bar/40% × 8 → 60% × 5 → 80% × 2-3 |
   | heavy_compound with W < 40 kg, or compound_machine | 50% × 8 → 75% × 3 |
   | isolation | 50% × 10 only if it is the first exercise for that muscle; otherwise none |

   Round to the available increments, skip any ramp load that is ≤ the previous one, and never count these sets toward volume.
4. Total warm-up target is 8-12 min. Show it as the email's first block, listing the exact loads.

### Exercise selection rules
- Each muscle gets at least one exercise with `lengthened_bias = true` per week where the catalogue offers one: hamstrings seated leg curl or RDL; triceps overhead extension; biceps incline or Bayesian/preacher curl; calves deep-stretch raises; chest DB press or fly with a full stretch; lateral raise from a low cable.
- Order each session from heavy compounds (when fresh) to machines to isolation, but put the arms priority first on at least one session per week if strength there lags.
- Do not use EMG figures to rank exercises; use the anatomy-based mapping in the catalogue.
- Rotate exercises only on stall or every 8-12 weeks for variety. Keep main lifts stable long enough to show progression.

### Rest rules
- heavy_compound 150-180 s; compound_machine 90-120 s; isolation 60-90 s. Antagonist supersets are allowed for arms and for chest/back with about 60 s between alternations.

### Confidence summary for the rules
| Rule area | Confidence |
|---|---|
| Any progressive scheme with hard sets works; double progression is the best fit for minimal logging | Moderate (efficacy) / High (practicality) |
| Exact increments, rep-range boundaries and stall protocol | Low (expert consensus) |
| RIR 0-3, failure optional, avoid failure on heavy compounds | Moderate |
| 2x/week frequency, about 10-20 fractional sets (fewer for beginners) | Moderate |
| Fractional 0.5 counting | Moderate |
| Deloads: reactive, reduced-volume, not complete rest | Low (evidence thin) |
| Specific ramp-up warm-up; short static stretching only | Moderate |
| Lengthened-position exercise picks (seated curl, overhead extension) | Moderate (single RCTs each) |
| Women: same principles; no cycle-phase programming | Moderate |
| Rest 60-90 s isolation, 2-3 min compounds | Low-Moderate |
