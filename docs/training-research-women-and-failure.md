# Women Grow Alike, Start Smaller, Tire Less

A 22-year-old woman building lean muscle should run the same core system as a man: roughly 10–20 hard sets per muscle per week, every muscle trained on at least two days, sets of 5–30 reps, progressive overload, and 1.6–2.2 g/kg/day of protein at maintenance calories or a small surplus. The earlier report's line that women "build muscle on the same rules as men" was right about mechanisms but misleading about what she should expect. Women grow by the same *percentage* as men but gain modestly less *absolute* muscle (SMD 0.19). They start with a roughly 40% smaller upper-body muscle mass than men and have much smaller fast-twitch fibres. They also keep their reps far better across repeated sets (about 58 vs 30 bench-press reps in one multi-set protocol), while recovering between sessions at the same rate as men. So the template stays the same, but upper-body emphasis, rest periods, how sets are prescribed and what progress to expect all shift. The earlier message that she shouldn't train to failure was also too blunt. Muscle growth improves as sets end closer to failure, and true failure adds at most a small bonus at a clear fatigue cost. The better rule is to end almost every working set 0–3 reps short of failure (usually 1–2), to use actual failure on the last set of isolation and machine exercises and on all light high-rep work, and never to plan failure on heavy barbell lifts. For a young woman training alone, the bigger documented risk is stopping too far from failure: trained women training alone chose loads of about 42% of their one-rep max, and lifters underestimate how many reps they have left by about one. Neither her menstrual cycle nor a typical contraceptive pill should change the program. What is truly female-specific is a set of health guardrails: eating enough, iron status and pelvic-floor symptoms. "Nikkiey Stott" is almost certainly the registered nurse and natural pro bodybuilder who co-founded WarriorBabe. Her lifting principles are sound, but her business is built around fat loss for women over 40. A 22-year-old should take the training basics and skip the menopause macros and reverse-dieting framing. Finally, the retracted Barbalho 2020 squat-versus-hip-thrust trial no longer supports any claim that squats beat hip thrusts in trained women. The evidence that remains says both grow the glutes about equally, and squats also build the quads and adductors.

*Evidence grades used throughout: **[Strong]** means several meta-analyses, position stands or consistent randomised trials agree. **[Moderate]** means one good meta-analysis or a few consistent controlled studies, with limits. **[Weak]** means single small, acute, observational or animal studies. **[Opinion]** means expert or coaching judgement, or this report's own inference. Full texts of most papers were blocked during the research, so the numbers come from abstracts, repository records and published summaries. Figures marked "as summarised" should be checked against the paper before they are hard-coded.*

## Women grow by the same percentage from a smaller base

Whether women build muscle differently depends on how the change is measured. On a percentage scale they do not. On an absolute scale they do. Treating those two answers as one is what made the earlier one-liner misleading.

### Relative gains match men's; absolute gains trail by a small margin

The first meta-analysis to pool sex comparisons, by Roberts, Nuckols and Krieger in 2020, found **no sex difference in hypertrophy (ES 0.07, P = 0.31; 12 outcomes from 10 studies)** ([Roberts 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32218059/)). However, it pooled standardised change scores without separating absolute from relative growth ([Edinburgh Napier](https://www.napier.ac.uk/research-and-innovation/research-search/outputs/sex-differences-in-absolute-and-relative-changes-in-muscle-size-following-resistance)). Refalo, Nuckols and colleagues addressed that in 2025 with a Bayesian meta-analysis of 29 studies. **Men gained more absolute muscle (SMD 0.19, 95% HDI 0.11–0.28), while relative gains differed by well under 1%**, and training experience did not change the result ([Refalo 2025](https://peerj.com/articles/19042/)). Their conclusion is the best current summary: "females have a similar potential to induce muscle hypertrophy as males, particularly when considering relative increases" **[Strong]**. Large single trials point the same way. In Hubal's 12-week elbow-flexor study, men gained more absolute size but **women gained more relative strength**, and individual changes in muscle size ranged from **−2% to +59%** ([Hubal 2005](https://stars.library.ucf.edu/scopus2000/3942/)) **[Moderate]**. In a mixed young-and-older MRI study, men added about **204 cm³ of quadriceps versus 101 cm³ in women** ([Ivey 2000](https://resources.keiser.com/longevity-research-on-strength-training/strength-and-power-gains/effects-of-age-gender-and-myostatin-genotype-on-the-hypertrophic)) **[Weak–moderate; half the sample was aged 65–75]**.

An equal percentage of a smaller muscle is fewer grams. A 22-year-old woman on the same program as a male friend should therefore expect visibly slower absolute change, even though her muscle is responding just as well. Two points keep this from being discouraging. The absolute gap is small in effect-size terms. And the spread between individuals within each sex (Hubal's −2% to +59%) is far larger than the average difference between the sexes, so her own response cannot be predicted from her sex **[Strong]**. Meta-analyses showing a bigger male advantage in absolute gains studied adults over 50 ([Jones 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33332016/)) and say nothing specific about her.

### The upper body is where the gap is largest

Whole-body MRI of 468 adults found that men carried **33.0 kg of skeletal muscle versus 21.0 kg in women (38.4% vs 30.6% of body mass)**. The sex difference was **larger in the upper body (about 40%)** than in the lower body ([Janssen 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10904038/)) **[Strong]**. Training gains follow the same pattern. In Refalo 2025's regional analysis, as summarised from its tables, the male absolute advantage was **SMD 0.30 (0.14–0.44) in the upper body versus 0.17 (0.056–0.29) in the lower body**. Relative differences in both regions were about 1%, with intervals spanning zero ([PMC11869894](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11869894/)) **[Moderate]**. The abstract describes the absolute difference as upper-body "but not lower-body". The lower-body gap is therefore best described as small and uncertain, not zero, and the earlier report's phrase "only in the upper body" made the split sound cleaner than the data show.

Strength runs the other way. Untrained women made **larger relative upper-body strength gains than men (ES −0.60, P = 0.002)**, with no significant lower-body difference ([Roberts 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32218059/)) **[Moderate]**. That gap is plausibly driven by neural learning from a low starting point rather than by faster growth **[Opinion]**. So her upper-body muscle responds to training the same way a man's does, but the result differs. Her upper body starts furthest behind and will change most slowly in absolute terms, and it is exactly the region that women's programs tend to under-train. RP's analysis of more than a million workouts logged in its app found that women choose programs weighted toward the lower body ([BoxRox on RP data](https://www.boxrox.com/?p=250604)). That reflects preference, not physiological need.

### Testosterone sets the starting line, not the growth rate

Women's far lower testosterone does not limit how much they grow relative to their starting size. When West and colleagues compared 8 young men with 8 young women, an **approximately 45-fold difference in exercise-induced testosterone** produced similar myofibrillar protein synthesis, both immediately and 24 hours after lifting plus protein ([West 2012](https://journals.physiology.org/doi/full/10.1152/japplphysiol.00170.2012)) **[Moderate]**. The accompanying editorial summarised a consensus that protein synthesis does not differ between young men and women "in fasted or fed states, at rest or after exercise" ([PubMed 22442029](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22442029/)). In 56 young men training for 12 weeks, post-exercise rises in growth hormone, free testosterone and IGF-1 did not predict lean-mass gains ([West & Phillips 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3371329/)). In trained men, "neither load nor systemic hormones" determined hypertrophy ([Morton 2016](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/303029912_Neither_load_nor_systemic_hormones_determine_resistance_training-mediated_hypertrophy_or_strength_gains_in_resistance-trained_young_men)) **[Moderate–strong]**. These studies looked at short hormone spikes and at variation among men. They do not show that the large difference in *resting* androgens between the sexes is irrelevant. That difference plausibly explains much of women's smaller baseline muscle mass, especially in the upper body, and their smaller absolute gains **[Opinion]**. "Women can't build much muscle without testosterone" is wrong, and "hormones don't matter at all" overstates what the research shows.

Estrogen is better described as protective than as a growth driver. In female mice, estrogen deficiency undermined maintenance of muscle stem cells (satellite cells) and weakened muscle ([Collins 2019](https://www.cell.com/cell-reports/pdf/S2211-1247(19)30791-0.pdf)). Rat studies link estrogen to dampened inflammation after damaging exercise ([Enns & Tiidus](https://journals.physiology.org/doi/full/10.1152/japplphysiol.00128.2007)) **[Weak for humans]**. The human data are mixed. One 2022 study found *blunted* satellite-cell and inflammatory-gene responses in women after eccentric work ([PMC9099417](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9099417/)), and a 2022 review of sex hormones and satellite cells in women describes an unsettled field ([Oxfeldt 2022](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1155/2022/9065923)). For a regularly menstruating 22-year-old, estrogen matters for training mainly indirectly. The thing to avoid is anything that suppresses it, above all chronic under-eating **[Opinion]**.

### Fibre type differs more in size than in number

The popular claim that women are "mostly slow-twitch" is overstated. Vastus lateralis biopsies from 55 women and 95 men of about 21 years showed no significant difference in fibre-type distribution, but **every major fibre type was larger in men**. Men's type II fibres also tended to be larger than their type I fibres, while the reverse held in women ([Staron 2000](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/002215540004800506)) **[Moderate]**. A 2024 meta-analysis put the type I proportion at **48.0% in men versus 50.6% in women**, with men's fibres larger in every type ([Nuzzo 2024, Clinical Anatomy](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/ca.24091)) **[Strong]**. A physiology review, as summarised, puts men's fibres about **19% larger for type I but 59–66% larger for type IIA and IIX** ([Haizlip 2015](https://journals.physiology.org/doi/full/10.1152/physiol.00024.2014)) **[Moderate; figures need checking]**. Because women's fast fibres are relatively small, slow fibres take up a larger share of their muscle area. That plausibly contributes to the fatigue resistance described below.

It does not mean women grow better with lighter loads. Type II fibre growth was similar between the sexes in Refalo 2025 (relative difference −2.3%, wide interval), with only a possible and uncertain male edge in type I growth ([PMC11869894](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11869894/)) **[Weak]**. And across 7,289 people in 269 studies, sex did not meaningfully change how many reps people can do at a given percentage of their one-rep max ([Nuzzo 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10933212)) **[Strong]**.

### Protein follows the same per-kilogram rules

Muscle protein synthesis is similar in young men and women, both at rest and after exercise. A Gatorade Sports Science Institute review finds "scant evidence that women, young or old, respond markedly differently to resistance training in terms of relative hypertrophy or strength gains" ([GSSI SSE 121](https://www.gssiweb.org/sports-science-exchange/article/sse-121-sex-based-differences-in-muscle-protein-turnover-and-metabolism-in-aging-feeding-and-exercise-responses)). Its companion review on female athletes judges sex differences in protein needs to be minimal "if they exist at all". It finds that needs do not shift meaningfully across the menstrual cycle or with oral contraceptives ([GSSI SSE 270](https://www.gssiweb.org/en/sports-science-exchange/article/muscle-protein-metabolism-and-protein-requirements-for-female-athletes--aligning-science-with-sex-specific-needs)) **[Moderate]**. The per-kilogram target is the same for both sexes. Gains in fat-free mass plateau around **1.62 g/kg/day (95% CI up to 2.20)** ([Morton 2018](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/52/6/376)) **[Strong]**. For a lighter woman that means fewer grams of protein, worked out the same way.

## Women tire less across sets but recover between sessions at the same pace

The earlier "same rules" line was most misleading here, because whether women have a fatigue advantage depends on the time scale.

### Fatigue resistance shows up across sets, not within one

Sex differences in fatigability are real but depend on the task. At the same relative intensity, women are generally less fatigable in isometric and slow contractions ([Hunter 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24433272/)) **[Strong for those tasks]**. When women and men were matched for strength, women's force declined about **half as fast**, and they lasted nearly twice as long ([Fulco 1999](https://scilit.com/publications/b023a1e1720c6995b4f7e58803c3084d)) **[Moderate]**. Even when exercise was set relative to each person's critical power, women's knee extensors lost less contractile function ([Ansdell 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32964441/)) **[Moderate–strong]**. The advantage shrinks for fast, high-velocity contractions ([Hunter 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5777316)) **[Moderate]**.

Resistance-training data show where the advantage matters. Within a single set at a given %1RM, women do not reliably do more reps than men ([Nuzzo 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10933212)) **[Strong]**. Across repeated sets with fixed rest, however, they hold performance far better. In the most practice-like test so far, women completed roughly **twice as many bench-press reps as men across a multi-set protocol (about 58 vs 30)**, yet soreness and recovery of estimated one-rep max did not differ. In the authors' words, women "fatigue slower than males during multiple sets of bench press yet appear to recover from training at the same rate in spite of completing a higher relative workload" ([Nuckols 2026](https://peerj.com/articles/20542/)) **[Moderate; single study]**. Earlier acute work points the same way: women did more bench-press reps than men with 1-, 2- and 3-minute rests, while men lost more bar velocity ([Ratamess 2012](https://stars.library.ucf.edu/scopus2010/4301)) **[Weak]**. In trained lifters the differences are smaller and less consistent, and greater male fatigability shows up mainly in complex free-weight lifts ([Amdi 2025](https://thieme-connect.de/products/ejournals/abstract/10.1055/a-2563-9449)) **[Moderate]**.

| Time scale | Young women vs men | Grade | Key evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Reps in one set at a given %1RM | Same | Strong | [Nuzzo 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10933212) |
| Reps kept across sets at fixed rest | Women keep more (about 2× in one bench protocol) | Moderate | [Nuckols 2026](https://peerj.com/articles/20542/); [Ratamess 2012](https://stars.library.ucf.edu/scopus2010/4301) |
| Fast or near-maximal efforts; trained lifters | Gap shrinks | Moderate | [Hunter 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5777316); [Amdi 2025](https://thieme-connect.de/products/ejournals/abstract/10.1055/a-2563-9449) |
| Recovery 24–72 h after a session | Same, even though women did more work | Moderate (one direct study) | [Nuckols 2026](https://peerj.com/articles/20542/) |
| After heavy, unaccustomed eccentric work | More immediate strength loss, lower CK, same soreness | Moderate | [Sewright 2008](https://coachsci.sdsu.edu/csa/vol141/sewright.htm) |

This has three consequences for programming. First, women can use the shorter end of normal rest periods with less loss of reps, which makes time-capped sessions and supersets cheaper for her than for a man **[Weak–moderate]**. Second, fixed-rep prescriptions behave differently for her. A man doing 3 × 10 with a fixed load drifts toward failure on later sets, whereas she may finish the third set as far from failure as the first. Prescribing each set by reps in reserve (RIR), and letting the rep count float, keeps her effort where it is intended **[Opinion]**. Third, she can probably tolerate more sets in one session before quality drops, although no trial shows that extra volume builds more muscle in women than in men **[Weak]**.

### The "women recover in four hours" claim fails

The idea that women can train the same muscle again within hours traces largely to Judge and Burke's 2010 bench-press study. Its authors suggested **48 hours of recovery for men versus 4 hours for women** ([Judge & Burke 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20625191/)). The sample was tiny, and a later paper pointed out that the conclusions were "erroneously based ... on within-sex time effects and not between-sex differences" ([Amdi 2021](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8618037/)) **[Weak]**. That later study found that women recovered lifting velocity faster after a fixed-volume squat session, but not after a session set to each person's 4–6-rep max. The best direct data show equal recovery between sessions ([Nuckols 2026](https://peerj.com/articles/20542/)), so frequency rules and recovery gaps should be the same as for a man **[Moderate]**.

### Women are not simply protected from muscle damage

After 50 maximal eccentric elbow-flexor contractions, 58 women and 42 men showed different damage profiles. **Women lost more strength immediately afterwards, men had a larger creatine kinase (CK) response, and soreness was the same** ([Sewright 2008](https://coachsci.sdsu.edu/csa/vol141/sewright.htm)) **[Moderate]**. A 2016 study likewise found higher CK in men but no sex difference in damage judged by loss of torque ([PLOS ONE 2016](https://e-space.mmu.ac.uk/605457/11/journal.pone.0150848.PDF)). The idea that estrogen protects women from damage rests mostly on rodent data and on CK. The safe conclusion is to introduce unfamiliar eccentric-heavy exercises, such as Nordic curls, deep Romanian deadlifts or deficit split squats, gradually over a couple of sessions rather than at full volume and effort **[Opinion]**.

### Shorter rest is tolerable, not better

The only hypertrophy meta-analysis on rest found a small benefit for **rests longer than 60 seconds and no clear gain beyond about 90**, possibly because longer rest preserves the total load lifted ([Singer 2024](https://frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2024.1429789/abstract)) **[Moderate; sex was not reported as a moderator]**. One study, reported second-hand, found that short rests cut women's total volume just as they cut men's ([Triathlete, on Millender](https://www.triathlete.com/training/should-women-use-different-weight-training-rest-intervals-than-men/)) **[Weak]**. Another, in trained lifters, found that women recovered better between squat sets ([PubMed 38347629](https://www.pubmed.gov/38347629/)) **[Weak]**. Popular rules such as "45–60 seconds for women, 90–120 for men" could not be traced to any trial ([Fitness Volt summary](https://fitnessvolt.com/women-should-train-differently/)) **[Unsupported]**. The defensible rule is to rest until ready. If her reps hold from set to set, the low end of the normal range (about 60–90 seconds) is fine on isolation and machine work. Heavy compounds should keep about two minutes or more, because the female advantage shrinks near maximal loads. Nothing supports resting less than 60 seconds between straight sets for the same muscle.

### Neither contraception nor the cycle is a training lever

A 2023 meta-analysis of 8 studies and 325 women found that oral contraceptive use had **no effect on hypertrophy (0.01, 95% CI −0.11 to 0.13)** or strength (0.10, −0.08 to 0.28) ([Nolan 2023](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10798924/)) **[Moderate; pills only]**. Individual trials mostly show either no harm or a trend toward greater gains in pill users ([Dalgaard 2019](https://jyx.jyu.fi/jyx/Record/jyx_123456789_65104); [Oxfeldt 2020](https://www.pubmed.gov/32694286/); [Romance 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31491790/); [2025 study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41420473/)) **[Weak each]**. The one negative signal comes from an observational study. Across 72 women aged 18–29, non-users gained **1.6 kg of lean mass versus 1.0 kg in pill users**, and users of more androgenic progestins gained only **0.3 kg** ([Riechman & Lee 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33993156/)) **[Weak–moderate]**. That conflict is unresolved. Hormonal IUDs, implants and injections are essentially unstudied. Contraceptive choice is a matter for her and her clinician, not for the training program.

Menstrual phase is not a lever either. An umbrella review found no meaningful effect of cycle phase on strength or hypertrophy adaptations ([Colenso-Semple 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10076834)). A study with hormonally verified phases found no phase effect on muscle protein synthesis ([Colenso-Semple 2025](https://physoc.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1113/JP287342)). A randomised trial in 24 women over three cycles found no benefit from emphasising training in either phase ([MSSE RCT](https://www.ovid.com/jnls/acsm-msse/fulltext/10.1249/mss.0000000000004031~menstrual-cycle-phase-does-not-influence-training-induced)). Performance is at most "trivially" lower in the early follicular phase ([McNulty 2020](https://rgu-repository.worktribe.com/output/940959)) **[Moderate]**. On days with bad symptoms, normal day-to-day adjustments are enough; a phase calendar is not needed **[Opinion]**.

### Verdict on "women build muscle on the same rules as men"

The line was accurate about **mechanisms and inputs**. Women respond to the same drivers: mechanical tension, effort, volume and progressive overload. They show the same relative growth, the same protein synthesis and the same per-kilogram protein needs. The same reps-to-%1RM tables apply. Recovery between sessions is the same, so frequency rules are the same. And there is no reason to plan training around cycle phase or pill use. These are the strong findings, and they still stand.

The line was oversimplified in four ways. First, it invited the reading "same results", when absolute gains are modestly smaller and women start with about 40% less upper-body muscle. Second, it treated women's fatigue resistance as a footnote. Across sets, the advantage is large enough (about double the reps in one bench protocol) to change rest choices, how fixed-rep schemes feel and how far from failure her later sets end. Third, it said the absolute gap appears "only in the upper body", which is cleaner than the data show. Fourth, it left out what is genuinely female-specific in her situation: the pattern of muscle damage, the hormonal context (energy availability, contraception) and the documented tendency of women training alone to choose light loads. A more accurate version, adapted from the research notes, reads: **women build muscle by the same mechanisms and grow by the same percentage as men, but they start smaller (especially in the upper body), gain less in absolute terms and fatigue less across sets, so the principles are shared while expectations, rest and emphasis reasonably differ.**

## Close to failure on nearly every set, true failure only where it is cheap

The earlier report did not tell her to avoid failure everywhere; it prescribed 0–1 reps in reserve for isolation work. But its headline rule ("not required; 0–3 RIR builds equal muscle") and its "never to failure" on compounds were easy to read that way. The first half of that rule is also too generous.

### Growth rises as sets end closer to failure; strength barely cares

The largest dose-response analysis is a series of meta-regressions that estimated RIR for each study's protocol. It concluded that "strength gains were similar across a wide range of RIR, while muscle hypertrophy improves as sets are terminated closer to failure". A linear model fitted best, though the overall fit was modest ([Robinson 2024](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-024-02069-2); [preprint](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/view/295)) **[Moderate for hypertrophy; Strong for strength]**. Direct comparisons of failure with non-failure find the final rep is worth little. A 15-study meta-analysis found a **trivial edge for failure (ES 0.19, 95% CI 0.00–0.37)** that disappeared when failure was strictly defined, which suggests the curve flattens near failure ([Refalo 2023](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9935748/)). Another found no significant difference for hypertrophy (ES 0.22, CI −0.11 to 0.55) ([Grgic 2022](https://doaj.org/article/dc36ba7ccd994798964f66b93bd8f662)) **[Moderate]**.

In an 8-week within-subject trial with 12 trained men and 6 trained women, the quadriceps grew **0.181 cm with failure and 0.182 cm at 1–2 RIR**, while failure caused consistently more acute fatigue ([Refalo 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38393985/)) **[Moderate]**. In 42 trained men and women doing a single set per exercise, hypertrophy "tended to favor" failure over 2 RIR, but the differences were "generally modest" ([Hermann 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40249908/)) **[Moderate]**. The 2026 ACSM position stand, which reviewed 137 systematic reviews, found that training to failure "did not consistently impact training outcomes", whereas higher volume did ([ACSM 2026](https://www.newswise.com/articles/acsm-unveils-landmark-2026-resistance-training-guidelines-first-update-in-17-years)) **[Strong]**.

| Where the set ends | Expected hypertrophy stimulus per set | Grade |
|---|---|---|
| 0 RIR (momentary failure) | Full; at most a small bonus over 1–2 RIR, at the highest fatigue cost | Moderate |
| 1–2 RIR | Essentially equal to failure with moderate or heavy loads | Moderate |
| 3 RIR | Probably slightly less; the outer edge of a "hard set" | Weak (inferred from model shape) |
| 4–5 RIR | Meaningfully less; count as about half a set | Opinion |
| 5+ RIR, or light loads stopped well short | Little or none | Moderate for light loads; Opinion otherwise |

So the earlier claim that 0–3 RIR "builds equal muscle" needs softening. Stopping 1–2 reps short matches failure, but 3 RIR is the edge of the effective zone rather than an equal alternative, and each additional rep left in the tank costs a little.

### Light loads only work when taken to failure

Load changes the rule. In 25 untrained men training knee extension with volume equated, quadriceps size rose **8.1% at 80% 1RM to failure, 7.7% at 80% short of failure, 7.8% at 30% to failure, and only 2.8% (not significant) at 30% short of failure** ([Lasevicius 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31895290/)) **[Moderate; men only]**. Light loads build as much muscle as heavy ones when sets reach failure ([Schoenfeld 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28834797/)) **[Strong]**. A study in which people stopped light sets when they chose to found results similar to failure ([Nóbrega 2018](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/311788235_Effect_Of_Resistance_Training_To_Muscle_Failure_Versus_Volitional_Interruption_At_High-_And_Low-Intensities_On_Muscle_Mass_And_Strength)), plausibly because people who stop light sets voluntarily still end very close to failure. Light, high-rep sets are also where people judge their proximity to failure worst. Reps-in-reserve predictions are less accurate above 12 reps and with lighter loads ([Halperin 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34542869/)), and differences between people in reps at a given %1RM grow at low loads ([Nuzzo 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10933212)). For a woman tempted by light "toning" weights, light weights do work, but only if she takes them to failure or within one rep of it.

### Failure costs recovery, comfort and power

Failure on big lifts is expensive. In 10 trained men, three sets of 10 to failure on squat and bench press caused larger drops in jump height and bar speed than six sets of 5 with the same load. The non-failure protocols recovered **24–48 hours sooner** ([Morán-Navarro 2017](https://www.springermedizin.de/time-course-of-recovery-following-resistance-training-leading-or/15095268)) **[Moderate; men only]**. Failure also feels worse, and stays that way. In the Refalo cohort, discomfort was higher with failure throughout and was as unpleasant at week 8 as at week 1, while discomfort at 1–2 RIR eased over time ([Refalo 2025, EJSS](https://openrepository.aut.ac.nz/items/4dfe7224-1903-4456-ab55-2c13a264560e)) **[Moderate]**. One quasi-experiment found that only women showed a drop in mood after training to concentric failure ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1360859220301091)) **[Weak]**.

Strength and power can also suffer. Well-trained men who trained to repetition maximum had higher training strain and smaller gains in jump height, rate of force development and maximal strength than men who stopped short ([Carroll 2019](https://digitalcommons.sacredheart.edu/pthms_exscifac/14)) **[Weak–moderate]**. Stopping squat sets at 20% velocity loss matched 40% velocity loss for strength with **40% fewer reps and larger jump gains (+9.5% vs +3.5%)**, although the group that went closer to failure grew more quadriceps ([Pareja-Blanco 2017](https://zendy.io/title/10.1111/sms.12678)) **[Moderate; men only]**. No study has measured injury rates from taking compounds to failure. The safety case for avoiding failure on barbell lifts therefore rests on fatigue physiology and expert judgement **[Opinion]**. In short, each failure set uses up recovery that could pay for another hard set, and weekly volume has the bigger effect on growth.

### Women respond to failure the same way, but tend to stop too early

The only women-only failure trial assigned **89 active young women** to one of three 10-week biceps programs at 70% 1RM: training to failure, stopping short of failure with volume equalised, or stopping short of failure without the extra volume. Strength rose similarly in all groups, and the authors concluded that failure "does not provide additional strength and muscle hypertrophy gains in young women" ([Martorelli 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28713535/)) **[Moderate; one muscle]**. The reported gains in muscle thickness, about **17.5% with failure versus 8.5% with volume-matched non-failure** (as summarised), lean toward failure without reaching significance. The low-volume non-failure group did not grow significantly. This fits the dose-response picture: effort and volume both matter, and failure adds at most a modest extra. No trial suggests women need more failure, and none suggests they should avoid it for hormonal or safety reasons.

How accurately she judges effort, and what loads she habitually picks, matter more than any sex-specific target. Trained women and men estimate RIR equally well on the bench press, with a **mean absolute error of 0.65 reps** ([Refalo 2024, JSCR](https://www.researchgate.net/profile/Martin-Refalo-2/publication/375687905_Accuracy_of_Intraset_Repetitions-in-Reserve_Predictions_During_the_Bench_Press_Exercise_in_Resistance-Trained_Male_and_Female_Subjects/links/65d9591fadc608480ae7db13/Accuracy-of-Intraset-Repetitions-in-Reserve-Predictions-During-the-Bench-Press-Exercise-in-Resistance-Trained-Male-and-Female-Subjects.pdf)), and sex did not affect accuracy across machine exercises ([Remmert 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37036795/)) **[Moderate]**. One study saw slightly larger errors in women with lighter loads ([Jukic 2024](https://physoc.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.14814/phy2.15955)) **[Weak]**. Across studies, people **underpredict their remaining reps by about 0.95** ([Halperin 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34542869/)) **[Moderate]**, and accuracy improves with practice ([Hermann 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40249908/)). Trained women chose loads of about **42% of 1RM when training alone, versus about 51% with a trainer** ([Ratamess 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18296962/)) **[Moderate]**, and novices of both sexes chose 42–57% ([Glass & Stanton 2004](https://sponet.de/sponet/Record/4008734?lng=en)). Until she has calibrated her judgement, her "2 reps left" is likely to be 3 or 4, and her fatigue resistance across sets pushes later sets even further from failure. For a 22-year-old woman training alone, the realistic risk is training too lightly, not pushing too hard.

### Drop sets and rest-pause save time; they are not upgrades

Drop sets produce similar hypertrophy to traditional sets ([Sødal 2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40798-023-00620-5)). A meta-analysis of advanced training systems found that none was better as a group than multiple straight sets, with a possible modest edge for rest-pause ([PMC12922048](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12922048/)) **[Moderate]**. Partial reps past failure have thin evidence, mostly from calf studies ([van den Tillaar preprint](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/download/542/1162/1086)) **[Weak]**. These are tools for the last set of an isolation or stable machine exercise when time is short.

### Verdict on "she shouldn't train to failure"

Parts of this were accurate. She does not need to reach failure on every set. She should never plan it on heavy barbell squats, deadlifts, presses or rows. And stopping 1–2 reps short gives essentially the same growth with less fatigue.

The rest was oversimplified. The useful rule is not "avoid failure" but "**get close on nearly every working set, and use failure deliberately where it is cheap**". The earlier wording underplayed four things. Hypertrophy improves as sets approach failure, so 3 RIR is the outer edge rather than an equal. Light, high-rep sets need failure to work. Failure on isolation and machine exercises carries little systemic cost and is the only reliable way to calibrate her RIR judgement. And her most likely error is stopping too far from failure. The rules below set out what changes: proximity targets by exercise class, a failure budget, a training block that moves from 3 RIR toward failure, a calibration protocol and graded counting of hard sets.

## A female template shifts emphasis, rest and guardrails, not the engine

### Long-standing consensus writes one set of training rules for adults

ACSM's 2009 position stand on progression, and its 2026 successor based on 137 systematic reviews, give one prescription for healthy adults with no separate rules for women. That prescription is every major muscle at least twice a week, around 10 or more weekly sets per muscle for hypertrophy, and heavy loads for strength ([ACSM 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19204579/); [ACSM 2026](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1119943)) **[Strong]**. Seventeen years of research between those documents produced no sex difference large enough to write into the guidance. The consensus documents that do contain women-specific content deal with energy, protein, iron and supplements: the IOC's 2023 statement on relative energy deficiency in sport (REDs) ([Mountjoy 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37752011/)) and the ISSN's 2023 position stand on female athletes ([ISSN 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37221858/)).

Researchers who study premenopausal women agree on "same principles, small tweaks". Lauren Colenso-Semple says women do not need a different rulebook ([The Proof](https://theproof.com/science-based-exercise-tips-for-women-dr-lauren-colenso-semple/)). Jeff Nippard calls sex differences "slight variations and tweaks to an already sound fundamental training philosophy" ([Nippard](https://rosetta.to/u/jeffnippard/men-vs-women-sex-differences-in-training-science-explained-12-studies)). Coaches go further and say women can handle more volume, more frequency and shorter rest. RP's Mike Israetel reportedly describes his own knowledge on this as "largely theoretical" ([BoxRox](https://www.boxrox.com/?p=192197)), and Bret Contreras frames it as a belief ([Huberman Lab](https://www.hubermanlab.com/episode/build-ideal-physique-bret-contreras)) **[Opinion]**. The disagreement is about how big the tweaks should be, not about whether women need a different system.

### The retracted squat trial changes the glute evidence, not the glute plan

The earlier report cited Barbalho 2020 (Int J Sports Med, doi 10.1055/a-1082-1126) for the finding that in 22 well-trained women the back squat beat the hip thrust for glute thickness (9.4% vs 3.7%). That paper has been retracted, so the claim is withdrawn. The problem is not isolated. A separate 2020 Barbalho paper in the Journal of Strength and Conditioning Research, on adding single-joint exercises in young women, is also listed as retracted ([retraction record](https://bvbvf1.bib-bvb.de/vufind/PrimoRecord/cdi_proquest_miscellaneous_2042234000)), and several of the group's papers have been questioned. Until each is checked independently, the safest course is to give no weight to any finding from the Barbalho group. That includes the 2018 paper often cited to argue that isolation work adds nothing for trained women **[source-integrity judgement]**.

The evidence that remains is thinner but cleaner. In untrained college-aged men and women, 9 weeks of volume-matched hip thrusts or back squats produced **similar glute growth on MRI**. The squat also produced more quadriceps growth (+3.6 cm²) and adductor growth (+2.5 cm²). The hip thrust's higher glute muscle activation (EMG) did not predict more growth ([Plotkin 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2023.1279170/full)) **[Moderate]**. Exercises that load muscles at long lengths have their own support; for example, seated leg curls grew the hamstrings more than lying curls (+14% vs +9%) ([Maeo 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33009197/)) **[Moderate]**.

So the weekly glute mix still stands: a deep knee-dominant lift, a hip-thrust or bridge pattern and a hinge. What changes is the reason behind it. Squats and lunges are there because they train the quads and adductors as well as the glutes. Hip thrusts are an equal glute option, not an inferior one. And no trustworthy trial now compares the two in trained women **[Moderate for untrained people; unknown for trained women]**. With Barbalho set aside, it is also unresolved whether isolation exercises add growth on top of compounds for women. Isolation work stays in the program as a low-fatigue way to add volume and long-length loading.

### Health guardrails carry the most female-specific weight

The female-specific advice with the strongest evidence is not about sets and reps. Chronic, severe low energy availability causes REDs, and bone health, including possibly permanent bone loss, is among its key outcomes ([IOC REDs 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37752011/)) **[Strong]**. At 22 she is close to peak bone mass ([Berger 2010](https://latam-cl.demo.elsevierpure.com/fr/publications/peak-bone-mass-from-longitudinal-data-implications-for-the-preval)), so a muscle-building phase should run at maintenance calories or a small surplus. In trained lifters, a +15% calorie surplus built no more muscle than eating at maintenance and added more fat ([Helms 2023](https://doaj.org/article/ab6ce09af2cc4daa9a1c2e9b88e1b1e9)) **[Moderate]**. The ISSN recommends protein in the middle to upper part of 1.4–2.2 g/kg/day for women ([ISSN 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37221858/)) **[Strong]**.

Iron deficiency (ferritin below 20 ng/mL) affected **30.9% of 2,749 NCAA Division I women** ([NCAA iron data](https://nutr.uw.edu/wp-content/uploads/2019/10/Finan.MNTPoster_MA.pdf)) **[Moderate]**, so a ferritin check is sensible if she has fatigue, heavy periods or a plant-based diet. Creatine monohydrate is safe in women; reviews found no adverse effects on the kidneys, liver, gut or heart ([Smith-Ryan 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7998865/)) **[Moderate]**, although the evidence for women-specific benefits is strongest in older women. Missed or irregular periods are a red flag for under-fuelling. On the combined pill, bleeds are triggered by the pill break, so a regular "period" does not confirm she is eating enough. That last point is an inference from how combined pills work, not a sourced finding.

For beginners in their first 4–8 weeks, add 1 RIR to every compound target and allow failure only in calibration sets **[Opinion]**.

### Failure frequency follows a five-week ramp

| Week | Heavy free-weight compounds | Dumbbell and machine compounds | Isolation | Light, high-rep work | Failure sets allowed |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 (calibration) | 3 RIR | 2 RIR | 1–2 RIR | 0–1 RIR | Calibration sets only, on 1–2 machine or isolation exercises |
| 2 | 2–3 RIR | 2 RIR | 1 RIR | 0–1 RIR | None planned |
| 3 | 2 RIR | 1–2 RIR | 0–1 RIR | 0–1 RIR | Last isolation set may reach 0 |
| 4 | 1–2 RIR | 1 RIR (machines 0–1 on the last set) | 0–1 RIR | 0–1 RIR | Last set of each isolation and machine exercise |
| 5 (optional peak) | 1–2 RIR, never 0 | 0–1 RIR on machines; 1 on dumbbells | 0 on the last set | 0–1 RIR | Same as week 4 |
| Deload | 4–5 RIR, about half the sets | 4 RIR | 3–4 RIR | 3 RIR | None |

The ramp follows the coaching convention of starting around 3 RIR and moving toward failure before a deload ([RP-style summary](https://arvo.guru/resources/methods/rp-training)) **[Opinion]**. The budgets keep failure where Refalo 2024 found it matched non-failure for growth, and away from the heavy lifts where Morán-Navarro 2017 found it added 24–48 hours to recovery.

## Conclusion

The argument over whether women follow the "same rules" mostly disappears once two scales and three time frames are kept apart. Relative growth is the same and absolute growth is smaller. Performance within a set is the same, women hold up better across sets, and recovery across days is the same. Each of those facts maps onto a different part of the program. The across-set difference shapes rest and how sets are prescribed. The absolute gap shapes upper-body emphasis and how progress is shown to her. The per-kilogram match governs protein. The failure question turned out to be mainly about calibration. For a young woman training alone, the documented errors are stopping too early and choosing light loads. The generator's job is therefore to pull her toward failure on exercises where failure is cheap, and to keep her off it on heavy barbell lifts, where it adds little.

The Barbalho retraction shows how thin the women-only evidence is. A single trial in 22 women was the main support for the trained-women glute conclusion, and a single 89-woman biceps trial is the only women-only test of training to failure. No randomised trial has assigned women and men to different rest, volume or RIR prescriptions. Two things follow. The generator should store the source and grade of every rule, so that a retraction or a new trial can be traced and the rule patched without rebuilding the system. And her own logs (reps across sets, calibration errors and percentage progress) are the best evidence available for the few settings where women plausibly differ, so those settings should adapt to her rather than stay fixed.
