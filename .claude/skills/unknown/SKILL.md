# Unknown: find what you don't know before you ask

Adapted from Thariq Shihipar's "Know your unknowns"
(https://thariqs.github.io/html-effectiveness/unknowns/). The idea is his;
this turns it into one skill for any kind of request.

Your ask is a map; the real situation is the territory. The gap between
them is the unknowns, and every unknown forces a guess. There are four kinds:

- **Known knowns**: what the user already said.
- **Known unknowns**: what they know they haven't decided.
- **Unknown knowns**: what they'd recognise instantly but would never think to
  write down ("I'll know it when I see it").
- **Unknown unknowns**: what they haven't considered at all.

This skill exists for someone who struggles to prompt because they don't know
enough about the topic or don't have ideas yet. So never make them generate
from a blank page: give them things to react to.

## How to run it

Do the work before asking anything. Read whatever context exists (the request,
files, earlier messages) so your options are grounded in the real situation,
not generic.

Then open with a short read of where things stand, in plain words:

```
What you've told me: <known knowns, one line>
What's still open: <known unknowns, one line>
What I think you haven't considered: <the biggest unknown unknown, one line>
```

Then use whichever of the moves below fill the biggest gaps, usually two or
three, not all of them. Keep everything short enough to read on a phone, and
explain any term the first time you use it.

### 1. Teach me my unknowns

When the topic is unfamiliar, give them the vocabulary a professional would
use, so their next request can be precise. Five to eight terms, each as:

```
Term — what it means in one line
  You could say: "<a sentence they could paste into a request>"
```

### 2. Blindspot pass

Three to seven things they probably don't know they need to decide, ordered
by how much each would change the result. For each: what it is, why it
matters to them, and a ready-made line they could add to their request.

### 3. Brainstorm the options

Six to ten concrete, genuinely different options, from the simplest or
cheapest to the most ambitious, each one sentence, lettered A, B, C. Ground
them in what you know about their situation. End with: "Tell me which
letters appeal, even partly."

### 4. Directions

When the answer is a matter of taste, describe three or four very different
versions of the same thing (for example: minimal, playful, data-heavy,
classic), and ask what to steal or skip from each.

### 5. The interview

For whatever is still open, ask one question at a time, ordered by how much
the answer would change the outcome. Every question carries your best guess
and three to five lettered options plus "something else", so they can answer
with a letter. Wait for each answer before the next question. Stop when you
could predict their next three answers.

## Finish with something they can use

When the open questions are answered:

1. A short decisions list: each decision and what they chose.
2. A ready-to-use prompt, written in their voice with the vocabulary from
   step 1, that they can paste or approve. Ask: "Use this as is, or change
   anything?"

Do not start the actual work until they approve the prompt.

## Rules

- Options, not open questions. If you ask something open, attach your guess.
- One question at a time in the interview; a batch of lettered options is fine
  for brainstorms and directions.
- "Whatever you think" is not an answer: offer two concrete choices instead.
- Plain language. Short lines. No jargon you haven't explained.
- Never pretend to know their preferences: label guesses as guesses.
